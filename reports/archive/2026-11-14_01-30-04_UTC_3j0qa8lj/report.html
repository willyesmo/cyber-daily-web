Cisco Talos is tracking the growing in-the-wild usage of AI-enabled penetration testing frameworks. AI-enabled penetration testing and red team frameworks can automate and chain reconnaissance, vulnerability testing, and follow-on actions, lowering the skill threshold for attackers and accelerating intrusions when the tools are misused. Talos has seen full automation of the attack chain by these frameworks, which not only use free and open-source tools, like nmap and nuclei, but also take advantage of internet mapping services like Censys and Shodan, CAPTCHA bypass services, credential leak management services, residential proxies, and even hash cracking services. This is the case of Hephaestus, which we mentioned in our previous report . Although these frameworks all have the same purpose, they come in different flavors. Some, like Hephaestus, are command line tools which can be deployed with a single script on a VPS and are completely controllable via Telegram. PentAGI has a web interface to monitor the agent activity with a full attack chain graph design and monitoring. Talos found more than 700 instances of currently reachable PentAGI instances on the internet. HexStrike AI integrates with many different clients like Claude Code, VS Code, or any other client that supports MCP. One such framework making waves, ARTEX, has enabled threat actors to scale offensive operations, compressing work that may have previously taken skilled teams hours or days into activity that can unfold at machine speed. Talos’ analysis of ARTEX-associated IP addresses identified a diverse set of tools concurrently present on that infrastructure, reinforcing ARTEX’s role in a broader and varied threat environment. AI-enabled frameworks: A new reality Before we delve into ARTEX, it is clear that these frameworks are the new reality. Their evolution will most likely include autonomous lateral movement followed by data identification and exfiltration. The precursor of this can already be seen in the victim prioritization of the Volta Infostealer, described in detail in this Talos report . It is highly likely that future attack chains involving these frameworks will culminate in data exfiltration and ransomware activation, all being done autonomously by one of these frameworks. Such evolutions present new challenges to defenders: It reduces the time it takes to detect a compromise without any known indicators of compromise (IOCs). We provide guidance to help overcome this challenge in the Recommendations section. After malicious activity is identified, it is crucial to contain the incidents. These frameworks work at speeds that can make it impossible for human operators to identify, assess and react in time to disrupt the attack. These frameworks make mitigation and containment, along with a battle-tested cybersecurity incident response plan, imperative for organizations' resiliency. Defenders using AI can be a force multiplier to provide much-needed improvements, both in the speed and scale of response to such attacks. ARTEX ARTEX is an AI-enabled penetration testing framework developed under the pseudonym Autumn-27 and published on GitHub in July 2026. An operator gives it a target and objective, a planner agent breaks the objective into tasks, worker agents perform them using security tools, and the framework records assets, findings, and progress in a shared graph. ARTEX can use different large language models (LLMs) and requires relatively little operator input after a task begins. It was designed for authorized security testing, but recent public reporting documented its use in both criminal and targeted intrusion campaigns within weeks of its release. It is important to note that ARTEX is not natively bundled with any traditional command-and-control (C2) framework, though the Model Context Protocol (MCP) extensibility layer means any compatible tool server can be added by an operator. This explains why we have observed multiple instances of ARTEX being deployed alongside tools such as VShell, GoPhish, and AdaptixC2. Threat actors leveraging ARTEX in offensive campaigns Multiple threat actors with distinct motives and tradecraft have been associated with ARTEX activity; its release as open-source software, followed by the continued availability of forks and copies, almost certainly facilitated adoption across different actor groups. Public reporting (referenced below) and Talos observations indicate ARTEX is largely used by financially motivated cybercriminals and threat actors with a China nexus. Observed ARTEX campaigns range from opportunistic scanning and credential theft to targeted intrusions and supply chain activity, with targets spanning financial services, cryptocurrency, government, telecommunications, defense, pharmaceuticals, semiconductors, and technology organizations across multiple regions. Reported outcomes vary from unsuccessful attempts to confirmed compromise and data exfiltration. Reported campaigns: VAPOR PANDA : CrowdStrike attributed an espionage campaign (beginning Oct. 17, 2026) leveraging ARTEX to China-nexus actor VAPOR PANDA. The actor used ARTEX against more than 200 organizations in at least 14 countries, with targets that included government ministries, telecommunications providers, and organizations in the defense, pharmaceutical, semiconductor, nuclear, and financial sectors. CrowdStrike confirmed post-compromise activity at nine organizations and reported additional credential-scanning and password-spraying activity against at least 12 others. Storm-0940 : Microsoft attributed ARTEX-driven activity to Storm-0940, who they assess is a “China-state aligned actor”. Following the compromise of ConnectWise application credentials, Storm-0940 used ARTEX to accelerate sign-in attempts against more than 2,250 downstream Microsoft 365 tenants, with follow-on activity involving Microsoft Graph, application credentials, and privilege changes. Storm-3358 : Microsoft also reported financially motivated Storm-3358 activity targeting financial organizations, primarily in Southeast Asia, and describes the cluster as Cambodia-based. Its ARTEX activity included exploitation of public-facing systems and deployment of remote access trojans (RATs). Cisco’s observations for this cluster are in line with Microsoft’s findings. We found IP addresses associated with this cluster conducted asset scanning in October 2026. Unidentified Chinese-speaking threat group : CrowdStrike reported an unattributed campaign against South Korean financial organizations that resulted in data exfiltration; it assessed with moderate confidence that the actor was Chinese-speaking and financially motivated. Talos separately analyzed actor-associated directories used in these attacks and found ARTEX-related artifacts, including directory listings and prompt history references. The archive did not contain the ARTEX executable or source tree, so these artifacts support an association with an ARTEX environment but do not, by themselves, prove that the complete framework was present or used in a successful attack. Unidentified criminal threat actors : Check Point Research documented additional criminal operators pursuing goals that included stealing code-signing certificates and gaming-account credentials and attempting cryptocurrency theft. Unattributed ARTEX-associated activity: Microsoft reported agents using ARTEX to search public GitHub repositories for exposed credentials, including application credentials and AI service API keys, and then use discovered secrets for follow-on activity. In one case, the agent moved from searching GitHub to exfiltrating data from Azure Key Vault within five minutes. Datadog separately observed ARTEX activity in AWS environments involving compromised long-term access keys and public Cognito user pools. The activity included searching cloud logs for financial information and secrets, registering accounts, collecting tokens, and attempting cloud resource enumeration. Datadog did not attribute these observations to a named actor. Finally, Dosu disclosed a separate ARTEX-related incident in which an attacker progressed from account creation and an authorization flaw to access involving integrations, CI, a database, and cloud systems. Dosu reported that the attacker generated more than 80,000 requests in 90 minutes. Observed tactics, techniques, and procedures Across campaigns, ARTEX has served as an orchestration layer for rapid, parallel reconnaissance and intrusion activity, enabling actors to turn exposed credentials or application weaknesses into broader access, persistence, and data theft. Operators typically set the objective and provided limited direction, while agents adapted their approach to the target. The resulting behaviors vary by campaign and should not be treated as a single fixed playbook. Planning and reconnaissance: ARTEX operators provide objectives and may assign tasks across multiple targets; its planner and worker agents can then conduct activities in parallel and record results for subsequent tasks. Check Point’s review of operator consoles found that agents often worked with little interaction after the initial goals were set, while VAPOR PANDA was seen steering agents at selected decision points. Reported reconnaissance includes scanning public-facing applications and services, testing authentication flows, enumerating cloud resources, and searching public code repositories for exposed secrets. ARTEX activity may generate varied requests and rapid retries, but tool signatures and user agents can change and should be treated as supporting indicators rather than definitive attribution. Initial access and exploitation : Reported initial access methods in ARTEX campaigns include password spraying, exposed or stolen credentials, and exploitation of application and authorization flaws. Observed weaknesses that were exploited in these attacks include weak or hardcoded administrative credentials, missing API authentication, insecure direct object references, command injection or template injection vulnerabilities, anonymous file upload and server-side request forgery weaknesses, and lack of defenses against privilege escalation. These observed tactics support that ARTEX can automate the discovery and use of weaknesses in web applications and identity systems, but the specific techniques depend on the target and campaign. Credential access and cloud abuse : Several ARTEX attacks leveraged the discovery or misuse of credentials as a bridge from an initial foothold to cloud resources and downstream systems. For example, agents were observed searching public GitHub repositories for credentials and API keys, then using exposed secrets for follow-on activity, including access to Azure Key Vault. Another campaign included AWS activity involving anonymous S3 requests, compromised long-term access keys, CloudWatch log searches, and public Cognito user pools. In the Cognito activity, agents registered accounts, collected tokens, and attempted AWS resource enumeration. Microsoft’s Storm-0940 reporting describes compromised application credentials being used to access downstream Microsoft 365 tenants and conduct follow-on activity through Microsoft Graph. Privilege escalation, persistence, and lateral movement : Observed follow-on behaviors include changes to application permissions, token or credential abuse, access to privileged accounts, and deployment of web shells or RATs. CrowdStrike describes VAPOR PANDA using historical authentication material to forge an administrator token, read mailboxes, and reach an internal network using recovered VPN information. It also reports endpoint-security discovery, web-shell deployment, and use of the BLOODALCHEMY backdoor in other parts of the campaign. Microsoft reports Storm-3358 deploying WISP, VShell, and AdaptixC2 for persistence and lateral movement. Of note, these actions are actor-specific examples rather than default ARTEX behavior. Collection, exfiltration, and impact : As detailed in the above campaigns, threat actor objectives include credential and data theft, financial gain, and supply chain compromise. Observed impacts range from access to cloud secrets and mailbox data to the use of compromised applications to reach connected environments. it can also relay traffic through proxy or reverse-tunnel configurations Recommendations For frameworks that pursue operator-defined objectives through adaptable workflows and supporting tools, associated activity can vary by target and operator. Defenders should focus on fundamentals that limit the speed and scale of attacks, reduce exposure, protect credentials and cloud identities, and quickly detect suspicious activity. Monitor for rapid, unusual activity : Correlate authentication, cloud API, and endpoint logs to detect high-volume requests, unfamiliar applications, token use, and privilege changes. Harden public-facing systems : Patch promptly, remove default credentials, and test authentication and authorization controls. Defend against credential attacks : Require multi-factor authentication (MFA) for remote and privileged access; apply rate limits and alert on password-spraying patterns. Harden endpoint detection : Enable endpoint detection and response (EDR) tamper protection and sensor health monitoring, alert on service or configuration changes, and retain independent telemetry to investigate attempted defense evasion, as ARTEX has demonstrated EDR bypass capabilities. Protect exposed secrets : Scan public code repositories for credentials and API keys; revoke and rotate any exposed secrets immediately. Limit identity and application privileges : Apply least privilege to service accounts and applications, restrict user consent, and review permission or role changes. Secure cloud resources : Disable unnecessary public or anonymous access and review storage, identity, and secrets services for exposure. Detect post-compromise activity swiftly : Maintain endpoint and network detections for web shells, remote access tools, C2 traffic, and lateral movement; treat tool signatures as leads, not the sole detection method. Prepare for fast containment : Have procedures ready to revoke credentials and tokens, disable suspicious applications, and isolate affected systems. In this article ScreenConnect architecture and attack surfaces Attack chain overview Mitigation and protection guidance Indicators of compromise and supporting artifacts References Learn More Beginning on November 11, 2026, ConnectWise reported an active campaign of a threat actor gaining unauthorized access to cloud-hosted ConnectWise ScreenConnect instances belonging to managed service providers (MSPs). Microsoft Threat Intelligence has confirmed ConnectWise’s reported activity and we have also observed threat actors abusing ScreenConnect, a legitimate remote-support application, to install unauthorized remote-access clients and deploy a malicious worm, SCKit, on ConnectWise customer managed endpoints. Additionally, Microsoft analyzed the malicious worm’s behavior: deployment starts with commands issued through ScreenConnect to silently install an identical client connected to threat actor-controlled infrastructure. A batch launcher retrieves a PowerShell decoder that decrypts and extracts SCKit using device-specific key material. The toolkit establishes persistence through scheduled tasks and registry Run entries and deploys RelayTrap, a component designed to deliver a malicious payload to a connecting technician. We have identified that the worm targets IT support technician workstations using the hijacked authenticated ScreenConnect Session and uses the victim’s management console to distribute payloads to other managed devices. The resulting access to either target enables threat actors to remotely administer affected endpoints, execute commands, and deploy further tools independently of the legitimate support instance. Defenders should investigate technician workstations and downstream affected endpoints. Removing an unauthorized client alone does not remove the toolkit’s persistence or invalidate exposed web sessions. In this blog, we examine SCKit’s attack chain and payload components and provide mitigation guidance, indicators of compromise, and Microsoft Defender XDR advanced hunting queries to help organizations identify and disrupt this activity. ScreenConnect architecture and attack surfaces ScreenConnect, formerly ConnectWise Control, is a remote-support and access platform used by MSPs to administer customer devices, often alongside remote monitoring and management (RMM) software. Its operating model has three parts: Management instance. A cloud-hosted or on-premises ScreenConnect server provides the web console and manages sessions. Its relay service carries communication between the technician and endpoint clients. Technician workstation. Support staff sign in to the console through a browser and launch a local host client for interactive support. Customer endpoint. An installed guest, or access, client maintains unattended access and executes commands delivered through ScreenConnect. An Access session provides persistent, unattended access through the installed client. A Support session is a temporary connection that an end user joins for assistance. ScreenConnect also supports remote commands from the web console without an interactive technician session. The endpoint client receives the command and runs it on the customer device; the technician workstation is not an intermediary for that execution. Reported server-side entry through extensions Extensions are add-ons installed on the ScreenConnect server to add features or integrate with other products, including ticketing and RMM tools. ConnectWise Automate was formerly named LabTech; Labtech Plus is a third-party extension, not the Automate product itself. The RMM+ extension family exposes remote-command APIs protected by a separately configured passphrase rather than the technician’s normal console sign-in. ConnectWise’s advisory describes a reported authentication flaw in RMM+ versions 1.0.31 and 1.0.32: when no access key was configured, the extension accepted an empty key. In that configuration, an unauthorized caller could submit commands to the server’s API for delivery to managed endpoints without a normal ScreenConnect user sign-in. ConnectWise is investigating extension abuse as an initial-access route; the entry method for the reported campaign remains under investigation. A separate path from customer endpoints to technicians SCKit is a malware toolkit that includes RelayTrap, a malicious replacement for the ScreenConnect client on an affected customer endpoint. RelayTrap is designed to deliver a payload to a technician’s workstation when they connect for support. ConnectWise states that the fix for CVE-2026-84869 blocks the toolkit’s attempted endpoint-to-technician spread. This client-side attack path is separate from the server-side access used to deploy SCKit. Figure 1. ScreenConnect’s three operating roles and two distinct attack surfaces. The reported RMM+ entry route targets a server-side extension; RelayTrap targets the client on a connecting technician’s workstation. That local process invokes curl.exe to download an MSI over HTTP from an attacker-controlled ScreenConnect server. The added client connects to an attacker-controlled ScreenConnect instance, creating a remote-access channel separate from the MSP’s approved instance. Securing access to the legitimate console does not remove this additional client or its connection. 2. A batch launcher retrieves the PowerShell decoder Further commands on the customer endpoint stage the toolkit. A PowerShell command decodes a URL-safe Base64 string and writes the result to a batch file in Windows Temp. The batch sets BATPATH to its own location, downloads the decoder from ns[.]mo7sen[.]dev/EadF.php?p=4, and starts it with -Mode full. The decoder reads BATPATH, or falls back to SCKIT_SELF, to locate the launcher. Its hidden-relaunch branch creates a VBScript wrapper and runs it through wscript.exe to start the same batch without a visible window. The scripts use the following p values at the same server path to request individual components: Request selector Component Role in the chain p=4 Decoder Downloads, decrypts, and expands the toolkit p=3&m=<machine-guid> Encrypted bundle Carries scripts, configuration, and trap components p=5 SQLite utility Supports browser-cookie database queries p=1&m=<machine-guid> Encrypted script Supplies the loader’s next PowerShell stage p=6&m=<machine-guid> Cleanup component Removes an old trap before optional redeployment 3. The decoder decrypts and extracts the SCKit bundle The decoder reads MachineGuid from HKLM\SOFTWARE\Microsoft\Cryptography and includes it as the m request parameter. The response provides a Base64-encoded body and two headers, X-Lab-Half and X-Lab-Salt. To derive the decryption key, the decoder hashes the UTF-8 machine identifier with SHA-256, XORs that hash with 32 bytes decoded from X-Lab-Half, and appends the machine-identifier bytes to the recovered secret material. PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 then derives a 32-byte key using the response salt and 4,096 iterations. The decoder uses the first 16 bytes of the decoded response body as the initialization vector (IV). It decrypts the remaining bytes using AES-256-CBC with PKCS7 padding, decompresses the plaintext with GZip, and writes the resulting ZIP archive. The archive is then extracted to %TEMP%\SCKitBundle. Figure 4. Selected decoder code for key derivation, decryption, decompression, and bundle extraction. In full mode, the decoder prepares %TEMP%\SCKit\trapkit\payload and stages the watcher, installer, alert script, and RelayTrap.cs. It also copies its batch launcher into the payload directory for RelayTrap to distribute. The copied launcher retrieves the decoder and starts toolkit deployment. The decoder also stages ToolboxRelayUploader.exe for console-based payload distribution and retrieves sqlite3.exe for browser-cookie queries if a suitable copy is absent. It includes two launch paths: full mode starts the distribution script without a separate configuration file and supplies a local script path; the other path stages a helper and lab-context.json and passes -SkipLocalExecution. 4. Additional component: PowerShell script loader Alongside the bundle decoder, SCKit includes a separate loader for encrypted PowerShell scripts. Unlike the decoder, which retrieves and expands an archive, this component requests a script using p=1 with the machine identifier. It applies the same secret-recovery, PBKDF2, AES-CBC, and GZip operations, but uses a salt embedded in its source rather than one supplied in a response header. After decompression, the loader converts the bytes to UTF-8 text and writes %TEMP%\k<32-hex>.ps1, using a newly generated random GUID for the filename. It starts a hidden PowerShell process to run the file with the forwarded arguments, then attempts to remove the script after the child process exits. Figure 5. RelayTrap adds persistence and targets connecting technicians On the customer endpoint, persistence keeps the toolkit available for a later support connection. The deployment places scripts under C:\ProgramData\RelayTrap. The RelayTrapLiteAlert value in the machine-wide CurrentVersion\Run key launches an alert script at user logon. A scheduled task starts the watcher with a payload filename and lists of client identifiers and relay hosts to exclude. The -PollSeconds 10 argument configures the watcher to poll every 10 seconds. The watcher enumerates ScreenConnect client services and reads their executable paths and connection parameters. For each client, its build routine creates a directory under trapbins, copies libraries from that client’s installation, and uses the .NET C# compiler to build RelayTrap.exe. It checks for changes to the source or client libraries before rebuilding. Figure 6. The endpoint-side watcher builds RelayTrap using libraries from the corresponding ScreenConnect client. RelayTrap is designed to reuse the customer endpoint’s ScreenConnect connection details in place of its normal client. When a technician connects with a vulnerable host client, RelayTrap attempts to deliver the staged batch launcher through that session. The launcher retrieves the same decoder, extending the toolkit’s deployment design from the customer device to the technician workstation. Our Global Emergency Response Team (GERT) recently investigated an engagement in which an adversary destroyed an organization’s virtualization estate over a 10-day period. The campaign targeted four ESXi hosts, every virtual machine hosted on them, both vCenter Server Appliances, and all connected backup infrastructure. The ransom notes identified the attacker as the BlackNevas group, first observed in November 2024. What’s interesting about this attack is that no virtualization layer vulnerabilities were exploited: no guest-to-host escapes, no hypervisor memory corruption. Every action taken against ESXi was executed through legitimate VMware management interfaces. In September 2021, the Babuk ransomware source code was leaked online, including its ESXi version, which made ESXi ransomware accessible to cybercriminals with relatively low technical skills. At least ten subsequent ransomware families have leveraged this leaked code, including groups that otherwise lacked the expertise to develop ESXi-compatible ransomware lockers from scratch. We saw multiple operators using Babuk-derived ESXi lockers along with the leaked LockBit Windows builds. It is worth noting that while the Babuk ESXi version may target both old and up-to-date systems, the target organizations run unsupported ESXi versions, making them easier to attack. This article examines why the hypervisor layer remains difficult to monitor, what common access paths adversaries use to reach it, and how they establish persistence on the management plane. We’ll also look into the architecture of ESXi encryptors, the deliberate destruction of backup infrastructure, and the log sources that enable post-incident reconstruction. At the end of this article, we’ll provide indicators of compromise recovered during the investigation. Ten days of dwell time from initial access to the destructive phase. Timeline reconstructed from incident evidence Why the hypervisor changes ransomware economics Virtualization enables cost-effective and efficient resource management, simplifies simultaneous updates and policy enforcement. However, at the same time it reduces the number of control points and enables broader impact in case of a cyberattack. In non-virtualized environments, a single guest compromise normally affects one operating system. Compromise of the virtualization management plane gives the attacker access to many hypervisor hosts, while direct impact on an ESXi datastore can render many guest systems unavailable at once. This makes the hypervisor layer an attractive target for ransomware operators, especially when domain controllers, application servers, databases, and backup workloads share the same virtualized estate. According to Kaspersky’s global cybersecurity services report , ransomware accounted for 39.4% of incidents GERT handled in 2025, and the data for the beginning of 2026 shows that it remains a major threat. Back in 2023, SentinelOne documented that multiple ransomware families are using ESXi lockers derived from the leaked Babuk source code, which lowered the development barrier for actors who otherwise lacked the necessary expertise to build ESXi-capable samples. This makes ransomware targeting virtualization environments more accessible to a broader range of threat actors, and this trend is still with us. The visibility asymmetry ESXi is a specialized hypervisor based on VMware’s proprietary VMkernel. It shares certain similarities with Linux (and other Unix-like systems), accepts Linux commands and runs certain Linux-like software, but it is different from general-purpose Linux distributions. Broadcom states that off-the-shelf security tools designed for general-purpose operating systems cannot run on ESXi without workarounds and that direct installation of third-party agents or antiviruses on ESXi and vCenter Server Appliance (VCSA) is not supported. Defenders therefore rely heavily on built-in hardening, management-plane telemetry, agentless security solution integrations, and telemetry collection from the infrastructure outside ESXi hosts. Guest operating systems are usually protected by EDR software, but if an ESXi-side process modifies a virtual machine disk (VMDK) or related datastore file, an EDR agent inside a guest cannot directly observe or attribute the hypervisor process performing the operation. Guest telemetry may record consequences, such as I/O failures or an abrupt shutdown, but the malicious execution occurs outside its boundary. In the engagement examined here, this made host-native and management-plane telemetry central to the incident timeline reconstruction. Credential reuse from the Windows estate The third path requires no vulnerability. Administrative credentials present on domain controllers, jump servers, backup systems, identity infrastructure or administrator workstations may provide access to vCenter or ESXi if the same admin accounts are used across different infrastructure segments. MITRE ATT&CK treats the Valid Accounts technique as applicable to ESXi and specifically notes that compromised default or domain accounts can provide privileged access to other systems. In this incident , credentials obtained from non-virtualization systems inside the organization’s perimeter were used to reach the vCenter Server Appliances. Unlike vulnerability exploitation, successful authentication with a valid account may appear operationally normal. To reliably detect such activity, analysts must therefore evaluate the source host, account history, time, destination, subsequent administrative actions, and whether the authenticated account normally manages virtualization infrastructure. Backup infrastructure as a credential bridge Backup platforms are not passive storage. They commonly require service accounts, hypervisor credentials, repository access and broad network reach so they can protect and restore critical workloads. This makes the backup server a concentrated trust point. In this incident, the adversary extracted stored credentials after gaining administrative execution on backup infrastructure, creating a route toward other infrastructure tiers. Persistence on the management plane With the potential risk possibilities explained, let’s go back to our case study. The attackers managed to compromise via SSH the vCenter appliance managing virtualization disaster recovery where they established two layers of persistence. The first used MeshAgent, the endpoint component of the legitimate MeshCentral remote management platform. The second used Ligolo-ng as a reverse tunnel implemented as a systemd service. This activity maps to two ATT&CK techniques: Systemd Service (T1543.002) and Protocol Tunneling (T1572). The timeline was as follows. The attackers authenticated on the appliance as root via SSH. Forty-three seconds later, they fetched and deployed MeshAgent through a shell script. The SSH session lasted 2 minutes and 26 seconds in total. After it ended, the adversary didn’t use SSH to access the compromised appliance anymore, thus reducing the usefulness of monitoring focused only on interactive SSH sessions. Roughly four hours later, they returned through MeshAgent and installed the Ligolo-ng tunnel as a persistent systemd unit. Beyond the incident in question, more advanced ESXi persistence techniques have also been documented. For example, Mandiant observed attackers using malicious vSphere Installation Bundles (VIBs) as a persistence mechanism on compromised ESXi hosts. This demonstrates that attackers with administrative access can establish persistent access at the hypervisor layer, including persistence that survives host restarts. However, this incident shows that common remote-management software and a systemd service can be sufficient when vCenter itself is trusted and poorly monitored. Turning vCenter into an offensive control plane One interesting technique observed in the incident was the use of vCenter Host Profiles. Host Profiles are intended to capture and enforce configuration across ESXi hosts. A Host Profile can be applied to separate hosts, clusters of hosts or all hosts associated with a profile. It also may contain subprofiles covering specific configuration aspects, e.g., authentication. Among other things, Host Profiles may contain configuration for Tech Support Mode (TSM) and TSM-SSH. In this incident, the adversary used Host Profiles to change TSM-SSH service state to enable SSH on target ESXi hosts. The evidence recorded the following sequence: Offset Source Observed event Primary evidence T+0 vCenter Host configuration read through Host Profile workflow vpxa.log T+10m18s vCenter Configuration change pushed to enable SSH vpxa.log T+10m51s vCenter / ESXi TSM-SSH service reached RUNNING state, indicating that SSH has been enabled on the host and it is accessible from a remote device vpxa.log + vobd.log Host Profile manipulation sequence observed before direct ESXi access Note that the 33-second interval between the configuration push and TSM-SSH reaching RUNNING state is short enough for us to be able to quickly correlate these events by comparing logs from different sources. Overall, it took the attacker eleven minutes after the initial configuration read to reconfigure the host from policy-disabled SSH to direct remote access support. Subsequently, direct SSH access to the target ESXi hosts originated from the vCenter appliance IP, a source that would normally be considered highly trusted on the management network. Compromise of the management plane converted a legitimate compliance mechanism into an access path to the managed hypervisor Why ESXi configuration change via vCenter is a trust-boundary failure Host hardening is important, but centrally administered controls should not be considered independent once the central management tool is compromised. If vCenter has authority to modify host state, an attacker controlling it may be able to make a malicious change look structurally similar to legitimate compliance activity: read configuration, apply a desired state, and observe the result. The distinguishing context becomes authorization, the timing, the source account, the change window, and whether the desired state itself weakens security. This is why vCenter should be treated as a Tier 0 asset and provided with the highest possible level of protection. Management-plane administrative accounts should be isolated from ordinary enterprise administration, network access should be restricted to dedicated management paths, high-risk configuration changes should require strong authentication and monitoring, and vCenter-to-ESXi traffic should be thoroughly compared to a normal behavior baseline (connection patterns, ports, protocols, source and destination systems) rather than implicitly trusted. All significant discrepancies should be treated as suspicious and investigated. High-fidelity detection opportunity An alert should be generated when Host Profile compliance (or remediation in VMware terms) changes TSM or TSM-SSH configuration outside an approved window, particularly when followed by a new SSH session from vCenter or another management appliance. This sequence is more precise than alerting on SSH alone because each step is legitimate in isolation but dangerous in combination. Partial overwrite and what 2 KB actually implies Incident artifacts indicated that approximately the first 2 KB of virtual disk files were overwritten. Damage to the beginning of a disk can destroy the protective MBR, primary GPT header, and other early boot or filesystem structures, which can immediately make a guest unbootable. However, the UEFI specification requires GPT media to keep both primary and backup GPT headers and partition-entry arrays in different locations. If the primary GPT is corrupt and the backup is valid, GPT-aware software should use the backup, which may help restore the primary. Therefore, an overwrite of the first 2 KB does not by itself mean that the partition metadata is unrecoverable. Practical recovery depends on whether additional boot records, filesystem metadata, volume structures, descriptors, or application data were also modified, and on the exact virtual-disk layout. Destroying the recovery path first In this incident, backup infrastructure was not collateral damage. The attacker disabled antivirus protection on the backup environment days before the main attack, and later stopped backup services, encrypted the recovery infrastructure, and disrupted administrative access — all before ESXi detonation. The order of operations reduced the defenders’ ability to restore production when they needed it most. Recovery capabilities were disrupted before the final ESXi impact. The relative timings are based on the incident sequence This changes the definition of a resilient backup architecture. Storage immutability is valuable, but it does not protect the identities, management servers, repositories, network paths, credentials, or procedures required to perform system restoration. Recovery should be designed as a separate security boundary with independent administrative identities, restricted management paths, offline or logically isolated recovery options, tested restore procedures, and monitoring that does not depend on the production control plane. Two recent vulnerabilities in Veeam are a perfect illustration of this. Although neither of these vulnerabilities was seen exploited in this case, they take advantage of weak privilege management policy, which may make them exploitable by any domain user, including those with the lowest privileges. CVE-2024-40711 is listed in CISA’s KEV database as a vulnerability exploited by ransomware. CVE-2025-23120 is more recent and still absent from KEV, but it is worth noting that according to Veeam, it allows RCE, but only affects domain-joined backup servers. The general lesson is not vendor-specific: a recovery system that depends on the same identity plane as production infrastructure can be compromised and used to achieve the attackers’ goals. Architecture hardening derived from the case Treat vCenter as Tier 0 VMware vCenter is not merely an inventory console. It can hold the authority to configure hosts, manage identities, power VMs, apply Host Profiles, and mediate access across the virtual estate. The incident in question shows why compromise of vCenter can undermine controls enforced through the virtualization management plane, providing an attacker with sufficient privileges to configure hosts, control VMs, and modify security-relevant settings across the virtual estate, potentially enabling the entire virtualized environment to be disrupted or destroyed within minutes. Protect vCenter with dedicated administrative identities, phishing-resistant MFA through supported identity federation, restricted management-network access, monitored privileged sessions, and close monitoring of Host Profile and cluster-level change operations. Separate virtualization identities from ordinary enterprise administration Avoid broad reuse of domain administrator or workstation admin credentials for virtualization. Where ESXi is domain-joined, apply the secure Active Directory configuration following Broadcom’s recommendations and continuously audit group mappings and permissions. Note that the Valid Accounts attacker technique is applicable to ESXi environments. Moreover, as an example of the Default Accounts subtechnique, MITRE specifically mentions the vpxuser account used by vCenter for host management. Keep direct ESXi management paths narrow SSH is useful for troubleshooting but should be treated as an exceptional administrative path rather than routine day-to-day access. Unauthorized SSH enablement on ESXi hosts both directly and through vCenter should be detected by monitoring TSM-SSH changes and treated as a suspicious event. To further harden ESXi hosts, use Lockdown Mode where operationally suitable, providing access only to a small set of explicitly authorized users, apply management-network filtering, and an approval and tracking policy for any changes, especially for enabling shell access. Make the logging path independent of the affected host Remote syslog from ESXi and vCenter should be stored on infrastructure outside the virtualization trust domain. Persistent scratch improves log survivability after reboot, but local evidence can still be lost if the data storage or host is destroyed. It is recommended to use centralized logging (e.g., SIEM logging), which should include authentication, shell, host management, Host Profile, and vCenter task telemetry. Proper logging should be configured before any incident happens, so the full picture is available to the response team in case of an emergency. Design backup as a separate security boundary Use independent administrative identities for backup, minimize domain dependencies, isolate repositories and management interfaces, protect immutable copies from production credentials, and test recovery under the assumption that vCenter and production AD are unavailable. A backup design should be judged not only by whether data is immutable, but by whether a clean operator can authenticate, reach the repository, validate the data, and restore workloads after the production control plane is compromised. Resilience improves when enterprise identity, virtualization administration and recovery authority are separated rather than chained through shared privileged credentials Conclusion The central lesson from the incident in question is not that ESXi needs another endpoint agent. It is that virtualization security depends on the integrity of the management plane, the identity plane, and the recovery plane at the same time. A hypervisor can remain fully patched and protected and still become the point of maximum ransomware impact when the system authorized to manage it has already been compromised. The adversary did not need to break VMware’s cryptography or escape from a guest. Once they accessed a privileged vCenter control, a legitimate compliance mechanism could be used to change host state, enable SSH, and create a path into ESXi that looked structurally similar to normal administration activities. The case also shows why destructive ransomware incidents are often won or lost before encryption begins. The attacker established alternate persistence, validated the ESXi payload hours before detonation, disrupted backup capability first, and then executed disruptive activities from the layer least visible to guest controls. Each stage created a detection opportunity, but those opportunities existed in management, virtualization and recovery telemetry rather than in a conventional endpoint process tree. The durable defense is therefore architectural. Treat vCenter as Tier 0. Separate virtualization identities from ordinary enterprise administration. Keep direct ESXi access narrow. Preserve native logs outside the affected platform. Detect high-risk configuration changes as sequences rather than isolated commands. Finally, design recovery so that compromise of production identity or virtualization management does not also provide the authority needed to destroy the systems required to restore them.
