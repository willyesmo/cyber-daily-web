ARTEX and beyond: AI-enabled penetration testing platforms
Cisco Talos | Research / advisory | 2026-11-13T20:57:13+00:00
Cisco Talos is tracking the growing in-the-wild usage of AI-enabled penetration testing frameworks. AI-enabled penetration testing and red team frameworks can automate and chain reconnaissance, vulnerability testing, and follow-on actions, lowering the skill threshold for attackers and accelerating intrusions when the tools are misused. Talos has seen full automation of the attack chain by these frameworks, which not only use free and open-source tools, like nmap and nuclei, but also take advantage of internet mapping services like Censys and Shodan, CAPTCHA bypass services, credential leak management services, residential proxies, and even hash cracking services. This is the case of Hephaestus, which we mentioned in our previous report . Although these frameworks all have the same purpose, they come in different flavors. Some, like Hephaestus, are command line tools which can be deployed with a single script on a VPS and are completely controllable via Telegram. PentAGI has a web interface to monitor the agent activity with a full attack chain graph design and monitoring. Talos found more than 700 instances of currently reachable PentAGI instances on the internet. HexStrike AI integrates with many different clients like Claude Code, VS Code, or any other client that supports MCP. One such framework making waves, ARTEX, has enabled threat actors to scale offensive operations, compressing work that may have previously taken skilled teams hours or days into activity that can unfold at machine speed. Talos’ analysis of ARTEX-associated IP addresses identified a diverse set of tools concurrently present on that infrastructure, reinforcing ARTEX’s role in a broader and varied threat environment. AI-enabled frameworks: A new reality Before we delve into ARTEX, it is clear that these frameworks are the new reality. Their evolution will most likely include autonomous lateral movement followed by data identification and exfiltration. The precursor of this can already be seen in the victim prioritization of the Volta Infostealer, described in detail in this Talos report . It is highly likely that future attack chains involving these frameworks will culminate in data exfiltration and ransomware activation, all being done autonomously by one of these frameworks. Such evolutions present new challenges to defenders: It reduces the time it takes to detect a compromise without any known indicators of compromise (IOCs). We provide guidance to help overcome this challenge in the Recommendations section. After malicious activity is identified, it is crucial to contain the incidents. These frameworks work at speeds that can make it impossible for human operators to identify, assess and react in time to disrupt the attack. These frameworks make mitigation and containment, along with a battle-tested cybersecurity incident response plan, imperative for organizations' resiliency. Defenders using AI can be a force multiplier to provide much-needed improvements, both in the speed and scale of response to such attacks. ARTEX ARTEX is an AI-enabled penetration testing framework developed under the pseudonym Autumn-27 and published on GitHub in July 2026. An operator gives it a target and objective, a planner agent breaks the objective into tasks, worker agents perform them using security tools, and the framework records assets, findings, and progress in a shared graph. ARTEX can use different large language models (LLMs) and requires relatively little operator input after a task begins. It was designed for authorized security testing, but recent public reporting documented its use in both criminal and targeted intrusion campaigns within weeks of its release. It is important to note that ARTEX is not natively bundled with any traditional command-and-control (C2) framework, though the Model Context Protocol (MCP) extensibility layer means any compatible tool server can be added by an operator. This explains why we have observed multiple instances of ARTEX being deployed alongside tools such as VShell, GoPhish, and AdaptixC2. Threat actors leveraging ARTEX in offensive campaigns Multiple threat actors with distinct motives and tradecraft have been associated with ARTEX activity; its release as open-source software, followed by the continued availability of forks and copies, almost certainly facilitated adoption across different actor groups. Public reporting (referenced below) and Talos observations indicate ARTEX is largely used by financially motivated cybercriminals and threat actors with a China nexus. Observed ARTEX campaigns range from opportunistic scanning and credential theft to targeted intrusions and supply chain activity, with targets spanning financial services, cryptocurrency, government, telecommunications, defense, pharmaceuticals, semiconductors, and technology organizations across multiple regions. Reported outcomes vary from unsuccessful attempts to confirmed compromise and data exfiltration. Reported campaigns: VAPOR PANDA : CrowdStrike attributed an espionage campaign (beginning Oct. 17, 2026) leveraging ARTEX to China-nexus actor VAPOR PANDA. The actor used ARTEX against more than 200 organizations in at least 14 countries, with targets that included government ministries, telecommunications providers, and organizations in the defense, pharmaceutical, semiconductor, nuclear, and financial sectors. CrowdStrike confirmed post-compromise activity at nine organizations and reported additional credential-scanning and password-spraying activity against at least 12 others. Storm-0940 : Microsoft attributed ARTEX-driven activity to Storm-0940, who they assess is a “China-state aligned actor”. Following the compromise of ConnectWise application credentials, Storm-0940 used ARTEX to accelerate sign-in attempts against more than 2,250 downstream Microsoft 365 tenants, with follow-on activity involving Microsoft Graph, application credentials, and privilege changes. Storm-3358 : Microsoft also reported financially motivated Storm-3358 activity targeting financial organizations, primarily in Southeast Asia, and describes the cluster as Cambodia-based. Its ARTEX activity included exploitation of public-facing systems and deployment of remote access trojans (RATs). Cisco’s observations for this cluster are in line with Microsoft’s findings. We found IP addresses associated with this cluster conducted asset scanning in October 2026. Unidentified Chinese-speaking threat group : CrowdStrike reported an unattributed campaign against South Korean financial organizations that resulted in data exfiltration; it assessed with moderate confidence that the actor was Chinese-speaking and financially motivated. Talos separately analyzed actor-associated directories used in these attacks and found ARTEX-related artifacts, including directory listings and prompt history references. The archive did not contain the ARTEX executable or source tree, so these artifacts support an association with an ARTEX environment but do not, by themselves, prove that the complete framework was present or used in a successful attack. Unidentified criminal threat actors : Check Point Research documented additional criminal operators pursuing goals that included stealing code-signing certificates and gaming-account credentials and attempting cryptocurrency theft. Unattributed ARTEX-associated activity: Microsoft reported agents using ARTEX to search public GitHub repositories for exposed credentials, including application credentials and AI service API keys, and then use discovered secrets for follow-on activity. In one case, the agent moved from searching GitHub to exfiltrating data from Azure Key Vault within five minutes. Datadog separately observed ARTEX activity in AWS environments involving compromised long-term access keys and public Cognito user pools. The activity included searching cloud logs for financial information and secrets, registering accounts, collecting tokens, and attempting cloud resource enumeration. Datadog did not attribute these observations to a named actor. Finally, Dosu disclosed a separate ARTEX-related incident in which an attacker progressed from account creation and an authorization flaw to access involving integrations, CI, a database, and cloud systems. Dosu reported that the attacker generated more than 80,000 requests in 90 minutes. Observed tactics, techniques, and procedures Across campaigns, ARTEX has served as an orchestration layer for rapid, parallel reconnaissance and intrusion activity, enabling actors to turn exposed credentials or application weaknesses into broader access, persistence, and data theft. Operators typically set the objective and provided limited direction, while agents adapted their approach to the target. The resulting behaviors vary by campaign and should not be treated as a single fixed playbook. Planning and reconnaissance: ARTEX operators provide objectives and may assign tasks across multiple targets; its planner and worker agents can then conduct activities in parallel and record results for subsequent tasks. Check Point’s review of operator consoles found that agents often worked with little interaction after the initial goals were set, while VAPOR PANDA was seen steering agents at selected decision points. Reported reconnaissance includes scanning public-facing applications and services, testing authentication flows, enumerating cloud resources, and searching public code repositories for exposed secrets. ARTEX activity may generate varied requests and rapid retries, but tool signatures and user agents can change and should be treated as supporting indicators rather than definitive attribution. Initial access and exploitation : Reported initial access methods in ARTEX campaigns include password spraying, exposed or stolen credentials, and exploitation of application and authorization flaws. Observed weaknesses that were exploited in these attacks include weak or hardcoded administrative credentials, missing API authentication, insecure direct object references, command injection or template injection vulnerabilities, anonymous file upload and server-side request forgery weaknesses, and lack of defenses against privilege escalation. These observed tactics support that ARTEX can automate the discovery and use of weaknesses in web applications and identity systems, but the specific techniques depend on the target and campaign. Credential access and cloud abuse : Several ARTEX attacks leveraged the discovery or misuse of credentials as a bridge from an initial foothold to cloud resources and downstream systems. For example, agents were observed searching public GitHub repositories for credentials and API keys, then using exposed secrets for follow-on activity, including access to Azure Key Vault. Another campaign included AWS activity involving anonymous S3 requests, compromised long-term access keys, CloudWatch log searches, and public Cognito user pools. In the Cognito activity, agents registered accounts, collected tokens, and attempted AWS resource enumeration. Microsoft’s Storm-0940 reporting describes compromised application credentials being used to access downstream Microsoft 365 tenants and conduct follow-on activity through Microsoft Graph. Privilege escalation, persistence, and lateral movement : Observed follow-on behaviors include changes to application permissions, token or credential abuse, access to privileged accounts, and deployment of web shells or RATs. CrowdStrike describes VAPOR PANDA using historical authentication material to forge an administrator token, read mailboxes, and reach an internal network using recovered VPN information. It also reports endpoint-security discovery, web-shell deployment, and use of the BLOODALCHEMY backdoor in other parts of the campaign. Microsoft reports Storm-3358 deploying WISP, VShell, and AdaptixC2 for persistence and lateral movement. Of note, these actions are actor-specific examples rather than default ARTEX behavior. Collection, exfiltration, and impact : As detailed in the above campaigns, threat actor objectives include credential and data theft, financial gain, and supply chain compromise. Observed impacts range from access to cloud secrets and mailbox data to the use of compromised applications to reach connected environments. it can also relay traffic through proxy or reverse-tunnel configurations Recommendations For frameworks that pursue operator-defined objectives through adaptable workflows and supporting tools, associated activity can vary by target and operator. Defenders should focus on fundamentals that limit the speed and scale of attacks, reduce exposure, protect credentials and cloud identities, and quickly detect suspicious activity. Monitor for rapid, unusual activity : Correlate authentication, cloud API, and endpoint logs to detect high-volume requests, unfamiliar applications, token use, and privilege changes. Harden public-facing systems : Patch promptly, remove default credentials, and test authentication and authorization controls. Defend against credential attacks : Require multi-factor authentication (MFA) for remote and privileged access; apply rate limits and alert on password-spraying patterns. Harden endpoint detection : Enable endpoint detection and response (EDR) tamper protection and sensor health monitoring, alert on service or configuration changes, and retain independent telemetry to investigate attempted defense evasion, as ARTEX has demonstrated EDR bypass capabilities. Protect exposed secrets : Scan public code repositories for credentials and API keys; revoke and rotate any exposed secrets immediately. Limit identity and application privileges : Apply least privilege to service accounts and applications, restrict user consent, and review permission or role changes. Secure cloud resources : Disable unnecessary public or anonymous access and review storage, identity, and secrets services for exposure. Detect post-compromise activity swiftly : Maintain endpoint and network detections for web shells, remote access tools, C2 traffic, and lateral movement; treat tool signatures as leads, not the sole detection method. Prepare for fast containment : Have procedures ready to revoke credentials and tokens, disable suspicious applications, and isolate affected systems. SCKit abuses ScreenConnect to deploy remote access and target technician sessions
Microsoft Security | Research / advisory | 2026-11-13T21:31:44+00:00
In this article ScreenConnect architecture and attack surfaces Attack chain overview Mitigation and protection guidance Indicators of compromise and supporting artifacts References Learn More Beginning on November 11, 2026, ConnectWise reported an active campaign of a threat actor gaining unauthorized access to cloud-hosted ConnectWise ScreenConnect instances belonging to managed service providers (MSPs). Microsoft Threat Intelligence has confirmed ConnectWise’s reported activity and we have also observed threat actors abusing ScreenConnect, a legitimate remote-support application, to install unauthorized remote-access clients and deploy a malicious worm, SCKit, on ConnectWise customer managed endpoints. Additionally, Microsoft analyzed the malicious worm’s behavior: deployment starts with commands issued through ScreenConnect to silently install an identical client connected to threat actor-controlled infrastructure. A batch launcher retrieves a PowerShell decoder that decrypts and extracts SCKit using device-specific key material. The toolkit establishes persistence through scheduled tasks and registry Run entries and deploys RelayTrap, a component designed to deliver a malicious payload to a connecting technician. We have identified that the worm targets IT support technician workstations using the hijacked authenticated ScreenConnect Session and uses the victim’s management console to distribute payloads to other managed devices. The resulting access to either target enables threat actors to remotely administer affected endpoints, execute commands, and deploy further tools independently of the legitimate support instance. Defenders should investigate technician workstations and downstream affected endpoints. Removing an unauthorized client alone does not remove the toolkit’s persistence or invalidate exposed web sessions. In this blog, we examine SCKit’s attack chain and payload components and provide mitigation guidance, indicators of compromise, and Microsoft Defender XDR advanced hunting queries to help organizations identify and disrupt this activity. ScreenConnect architecture and attack surfaces ScreenConnect, formerly ConnectWise Control, is a remote-support and access platform used by MSPs to administer customer devices, often alongside remote monitoring and management (RMM) software. Its operating model has three parts: Management instance. A cloud-hosted or on-premises ScreenConnect server provides the web console and manages sessions. Its relay service carries communication between the technician and endpoint clients. Technician workstation. Support staff sign in to the console through a browser and launch a local host client for interactive support. Customer endpoint. An installed guest, or access, client maintains unattended access and executes commands delivered through ScreenConnect. An Access session provides persistent, unattended access through the installed client. A Support session is a temporary connection that an end user joins for assistance. ScreenConnect also supports remote commands from the web console without an interactive technician session. The endpoint client receives the command and runs it on the customer device; the technician workstation is not an intermediary for that execution. Reported server-side entry through extensions Extensions are add-ons installed on the ScreenConnect server to add features or integrate with other products, including ticketing and RMM tools. ConnectWise Automate was formerly named LabTech; Labtech Plus is a third-party extension, not the Automate product itself. The RMM+ extension family exposes remote-command APIs protected by a separately configured passphrase rather than the technician’s normal console sign-in. ConnectWise’s advisory describes a reported authentication flaw in RMM+ versions 1.0.31 and 1.0.32: when no access key was configured, the extension accepted an empty key. In that configuration, an unauthorized caller could submit commands to the server’s API for delivery to managed endpoints without a normal ScreenConnect user sign-in. ConnectWise is investigating extension abuse as an initial-access route; the entry method for the reported campaign remains under investigation. A separate path from customer endpoints to technicians SCKit is a malware toolkit that includes RelayTrap, a malicious replacement for the ScreenConnect client on an affected customer endpoint. RelayTrap is designed to deliver a payload to a technician’s workstation when they connect for support. ConnectWise states that the fix for CVE-2026-84869 blocks the toolkit’s attempted endpoint-to-technician spread. This client-side attack path is separate from the server-side access used to deploy SCKit. Figure 1. ScreenConnect’s three operating roles and two distinct attack surfaces. The reported RMM+ entry route targets a server-side extension; RelayTrap targets the client on a connecting technician’s workstation. Attack chain overview The deployment sequence below runs on the customer endpoint. It establishes a separate remote-access connection and prepares the scripts and RelayTrap component that target a later technician Figure 2. SCKit deployment on a customer endpoint and its technician-targeting capabilities. The dashed path describes the toolkit’s propagation design, not a confirmed sequence of infections. 1. ScreenConnect commands install an additional client The threat actor uses the MSP’s ScreenConnect management instance to direct the installed endpoint client to start cmd.exe. That local process invokes curl.exe to download an MSI over HTTP from an attacker-controlled ScreenConnect server. The command requests /Bin/ScreenConnect.ClientSetup.msi with access-session parameters and a c=Exploited<N> marker (c=Exploited9 in the illustrated command), and saves the installer to C:\Windows\Temp\sc.msi. Figure 3. Command delivered through ScreenConnect and executed on the customer endpoint to download the client MSI. The attacker-controlled server’s IP is redacted. Windows Installer then installs the package silently on that endpoint, without restarting it, using /i, /qn, and /norestart. The added client connects to an attacker-controlled ScreenConnect instance, creating a remote-access channel separate from the MSP’s approved instance. Securing access to the legitimate console does not remove this additional client or its connection. 2. A batch launcher retrieves the PowerShell decoder Further commands on the customer endpoint stage the toolkit. A PowerShell command decodes a URL-safe Base64 string and writes the result to a batch file in Windows Temp. The batch sets BATPATH to its own location, downloads the decoder from ns[.]mo7sen[.]dev/EadF.php?p=4, and starts it with -Mode full. The decoder reads BATPATH, or falls back to SCKIT_SELF, to locate the launcher. Its hidden-relaunch branch creates a VBScript wrapper and runs it through wscript.exe to start the same batch without a visible window. The scripts use the following p values at the same server path to request individual components: Request selector Component Role in the chain p=4 Decoder Downloads, decrypts, and expands the toolkit p=3&m=<machine-guid> Encrypted bundle Carries scripts, configuration, and trap components p=5 SQLite utility Supports browser-cookie database queries p=1&m=<machine-guid> Encrypted script Supplies the loader’s next PowerShell stage p=6&m=<machine-guid> Cleanup component Removes an old trap before optional redeployment 3. The decoder decrypts and extracts the SCKit bundle The decoder reads MachineGuid from HKLM\SOFTWARE\Microsoft\Cryptography and includes it as the m request parameter. The response provides a Base64-encoded body and two headers, X-Lab-Half and X-Lab-Salt. To derive the decryption key, the decoder hashes the UTF-8 machine identifier with SHA-256, XORs that hash with 32 bytes decoded from X-Lab-Half, and appends the machine-identifier bytes to the recovered secret material. PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 then derives a 32-byte key using the response salt and 4,096 iterations. The decoder uses the first 16 bytes of the decoded response body as the initialization vector (IV). It decrypts the remaining bytes using AES-256-CBC with PKCS7 padding, decompresses the plaintext with GZip, and writes the resulting ZIP archive. The archive is then extracted to %TEMP%\SCKitBundle. Figure 4. Selected decoder code for key derivation, decryption, decompression, and bundle extraction. In full mode, the decoder prepares %TEMP%\SCKit\trapkit\payload and stages the watcher, installer, alert script, and RelayTrap.cs. It also copies its batch launcher into the payload directory for RelayTrap to distribute. The copied launcher retrieves the decoder and starts toolkit deployment. The decoder also stages ToolboxRelayUploader.exe for console-based payload distribution and retrieves sqlite3.exe for browser-cookie queries if a suitable copy is absent. It includes two launch paths: full mode starts the distribution script without a separate configuration file and supplies a local script path; the other path stages a helper and lab-context.json and passes -SkipLocalExecution. 4. Additional component: PowerShell script loader Alongside the bundle decoder, SCKit includes a separate loader for encrypted PowerShell scripts. Unlike the decoder, which retrieves and expands an archive, this component requests a script using p=1 with the machine identifier. It applies the same secret-recovery, PBKDF2, AES-CBC, and GZip operations, but uses a salt embedded in its source rather than one supplied in a response header. After decompression, the loader converts the bytes to UTF-8 text and writes %TEMP%\k<32-hex>.ps1, using a newly generated random GUID for the filename. It starts a hidden PowerShell process to run the file with the forwarded arguments, then attempts to remove the script after the child process exits. Figure 5. Temporary script creation, execution, and cleanup in the PowerShell script loader. 5. RelayTrap adds persistence and targets connecting technicians On the customer endpoint, persistence keeps the toolkit available for a later support connection. The deployment places scripts under C:\ProgramData\RelayTrap. The RelayTrapLiteAlert value in the machine-wide CurrentVersion\Run key launches an alert script at user logon. A scheduled task starts the watcher with a payload filename and lists of client identifiers and relay hosts to exclude. The -PollSeconds 10 argument configures the watcher to poll every 10 seconds. The watcher enumerates ScreenConnect client services and reads their executable paths and connection parameters. For each client, its build routine creates a directory under trapbins, copies libraries from that client’s installation, and uses the .NET C# compiler to build RelayTrap.exe. It checks for changes to the source or client libraries before rebuilding. Figure 6. The endpoint-side watcher builds RelayTrap using libraries from the corresponding ScreenConnect client. RelayTrap is designed to reuse the customer endpoint’s ScreenConnect connection details in place of its normal client. When a technician connects with a vulnerable host client, RelayTrap attempts to deliver the staged batch launcher through that session. The launcher retrieves the same decoder, extending the toolkit’s deployment design from the customer device to the technician workstation. Configured skip lists exclude specified client identifiers and relay hosts from processing. ScreenConnect services are also configured for automatic startup. Registry writes target Microsoft Defender Antivirus path exclusions for staged MSI copies and CWInstall_…\Installer.ps1. The installer configuration lists sc[.]payproofkit[.]com and cdn007-digitaldownloads[.]com as additional ScreenConnect MSI sources. A subsequent curl.exe command downloads another client installer from sc[.]payproofkit[.]com. 6. Browser access targets authenticated ScreenConnect sessions SCKit also targets the technician’s browser, which is used to sign in to the management console. The browser-access code searches Chrome, Edge, Opera, and Opera GX profiles for cookies named .ASPXAUTH. Its database route identifies associated hosts and queries encrypted cookie values. It copies cookie databases and their supporting files, with a volume-shadow-copy fallback for locked databases. The decryption code uses the Chromium master key and the Windows Data Protection API (DPAPI) in the current user’s context, with AES-GCM routines for v10 and v11 cookie values. The memory-scanning route first examines browser network-service processes identified by network.mojom.NetworkService in their command lines. An embedded C# scanner uses OpenProcess, VirtualQueryEx, and ReadProcessMemory to inspect readable committed memory regions for cookie strings and nearby host strings. If the initial scan finds no candidates, it scans other browser processes. Figure 7. Database queries and browser-memory scanning target ScreenConnect authentication cookies. The console-distribution component is designed to combine an authentication cookie with the required anti-forgery and access tokens, enumerate accessible sessions, upload a payload using ToolboxRelayUploader.exe, and queue execution through the Shared Toolbox. The workflow also includes removing the uploaded Toolbox item after distribution. This component targets the technician’s management-console access, rather than only the original customer device. The account’s permissions determine which additional sessions and devices the distribution workflow can reach. 7. Cleanup and redeployment The toolkit’s cleanup script places a stop marker in the trap directory. It removes trap-related tasks, services, processes, Run entries, and files, and restores ScreenConnect client services unless client removal was requested. It then removes any remaining stop marker to allow a newly deployed watcher to run. After running the cleanup script, the decoder’s cleanup mode starts the bundled dropper again unless -NoReArm is set. Cleanup therefore also serves as a path to redeploy the toolkit. Figure 8. Cleanup restores ScreenConnect services; the decoder checks NoReArm before starting the bundled dropper again. Mitigation and protection guidance Secure ScreenConnect administration and technician clients Address the server-side extension APIs and technician clients separately. Extension authentication and client-side file transfer are different attack surfaces; securing one does not remove the other or an existing endpoint installation. Keep the identified extensions disabled. Follow the vendor advisory for Labtech Plus, RMM Plus, RMM+, RMM Plus Password Link, and RMM+ Password Link. Review their installation and configuration history alongside administrative access. Update technician clients separately from the console. ConnectWise states that ScreenConnect client 26.6.5 and later block the toolkit’s attempted endpoint-to-technician transfer technique. The fix for CVE-2026-84869 was released on September 8, 2026. Verify each technician client’s installed version; a cloud-console update does not automatically update those clients. The vendor bulletin also describes temporary file-transfer restrictions while updates are pending. Remove established access Investigate through a separate channel. Use Microsoft Defender for Endpoint live response or another approved out-of-band method instead of ScreenConnect on a suspected affected device. Isolate affected endpoints and include connecting technician workstations in scope. Preserve console and endpoint evidence. Retain extension, authentication, Run Command, and Toolbox records together with process, file, task, and registry history. Review Toolbox history for tools removed after use, and inspect queued commands and tools before reconnecting devices. Revoke exposed sessions from a clean device. Invalidate affected ScreenConnect web sessions and associated identity-provider sessions, rotate exposed credentials, and review accounts and permissions. Password changes alone do not address every existing session. Remove persistence and unapproved clients. Validate each client’s enrolled instance and relay destination before removing it. Remove malicious tasks, Run values, trap files, and unauthorized exclusions through approved response tooling. Do not use a cleanup script recovered from the kit as a remediation tool. Verify the complete state before restoring connectivity. Confirm approved services and destinations, effective antivirus settings, and the absence of re-created tasks or scripts. Review tamper-protection events when investigating exclusion changes. Rebuild devices when integrity cannot be established. Keep Microsoft Defender Antivirus, cloud-delivered protection, and tamper protection enabled. Verify that devices meet the requirements for protecting antivirus exclusions . Maintain an inventory of authorized remote-management instances and use application control to restrict unapproved software.
