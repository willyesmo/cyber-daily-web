Cisco Talos is aware of eight compromised intermediate certificates belonging to the intermediate certificate authority, eMudhra . Rather than possessing the mandatory 384 bits of pseudo-random security, several private keys evaluated single-digit and double-digit integers. This misconfiguration allows an attacker with the private key of any of the compromised intermediate CAs to impersonate any website or service. Six of the TLS certificates, trusted by Chrome, Mozilla, and Apple have been blocked by these vendors already. Two other certificates, used for codesigning by Microsoft, have also been blocked. Talos expects there will be additional patches available across a variety of technologies as software applications work to remediate the certificates’ exposure. Following the disclosure, eMudhra has revoked the compromised certificates and stopped issuing new certificates under its G3 root. Compromise details The cause of this issue, originated during an April 30, 2025 key generation activity conducted by eMudhra under its emSign Root TLS CA - G3 and cross-signed Comodo AAA Certificate Services. During a key generation ceremony for 24 subordinate CAs, a flaw in eMudhra’s key generation process created a second set of weak P-384 keys during a wrap/unwrap step. Those keys were mistakenly used for eight intermediate CA certificates. This flaw took the encryption from 384 bytes of entropy down to 3 – 8, making recovering the private keys trivial. Impact These compromised certificates could allow adversaries to impersonate servers and intercept or modify traffic. The impact of their exposure is far more than just web browsers. Other programs that check for TLS certificates might still be impacted since they may not check revocation lists. One clear example is related to virtual private networks (VPNs); these technologies will likely require software updates to mitigate the issue. Based on this guidance, Talos strongly recommends users update software using and handling TLS certificates as fixes become available. Talos is actively monitoring the impact of these compromised certificates and has not seen any malicious incidents involving them in the wild yet (as of October 21, 2026 14:00 UTC / 10:00 a.m. ET). Significance of this breach Public key infrastructure relies on trust being delegated through certificate chains: root CAs authorize intermediate CAs, which can issue certificates within the scope of their authority. An attacker who obtains an intermediate CA’s private key may be able to issue fraudulent certificates that clients accept, subject to the intermediate certificate’s constraints and each client’s validation controls. In this incident, six compromised intermediates were for TLS and two were for code signing. For TLS, an attacker able to intercept or redirect a connection could use a fraudulent certificate to impersonate a server and potentially read or modify traffic if the client still trusts the affected key and no applicable protection blocks it. Protections such as revocation, key distrust, certificate transparency enforcement, and certificate pinning can prevent or expose such an attack. Browser vendors have blocked the affected TLS keys, but other applications may rely on separate trust stores or validation paths: Enterprise infrastructure and VPNs Organizations should assess TLS-using software and appliances, including proxies, reverse load balancers, VPN clients, and gateways. Exposure depends on the certificate chain being validated and the trust store and revocation behavior in use. A stale or separately managed trust store may need an application, operating-system, firmware, or trust-bundle update; verify the required action for each product. Software supply chains and CI/CD Build runners and package managers may be exposed if they fetch content over TLS while still trusting an affected key and an attacker can intercept the connection. A fraudulent TLS certificate could enable registry impersonation, but it would not by itself bypass separate package integrity checks, such as lockfile hashes, checksums, signatures, or provenance controls. Assess the actual package-manager configuration and dependency path. Industrial IoT and embedded systems Review devices that use bundled or independently managed CA stores, especially where certificate revocation or trust-store updates may be limited. Some devices may require a vendor update or compensating control, but the necessary action depends on each device’s validation and update mechanisms. Talos recommends updating affected software and trust stores as vendor fixes become available. Coverage The following SNORT® SIDs provide coverage for this threat: 67363 - 67374. In this article What’s happening What is ARTEX? Mitigation and protection guidance References Learn more As early as September 2026, Storm-0940, a China-state aligned actor, used the agentic AI penetration testing framework ARTEX to automate attacks targeting hundreds of organizations globally across a broad range of industries. Several of these Storm-0940 ARTEX-driven attacks have resulted in the actor obtaining privileged access to an organization’s cloud infrastructure – including multiple instances where the time from initial targeting to privileged access was less than 3 hours. Microsoft assesses that Storm-0940 was responsible for the recently disclosed ConnectWise supply chain compromise of a multi-tenant application. This activity demonstrates how threat actors are integrating agentic AI into existing offensive security workflows and allows them to scale their operations exponentially. Agents are not constrained by factors affecting humans – they can work around the clock, on weekends, and on holidays and attempt multiple breach paths simultaneously enabling significantly reduced time from selecting a target to achieving objectives, increasing the speed and impact of complex attacks like supply chain compromises. Storm-0940’s use of ARTEX accelerated the speed of compromised application abuse to authenticate to Microsoft 365 customer environments through applications that had already been granted access. This allowed the threat actor to operate without requiring an interactive user sign-in. In multiple affected environments, Microsoft observed AI-accelerated follow-on activity through Microsoft Graph, including reconnaissance, application credential changes, and attempts to modify privileges that could enable expanded or persistent access. The activity conducted by the threat actor through the usage of ARTEX framework did not result from a compromise of or a vulnerability in Microsoft corporate systems, products, or services. Although Microsoft identified Storm-0940-initiated authentication and follow-on activity in multiple customer environments, the presence of an affected application or successful threat actor authentication does not necessarily indicate that customer data was accessed or exfiltrated. Microsoft Defender XDR provides detections and hunting queries for suspicious Microsoft Graph activity and activity associated with known threat infrastructure. Customers should review service principal sign-ins, investigate unexpected application credential or privilege changes, and follow ConnectWise’s published containment guidance while preserving relevant evidence for investigation. For a full list of detections please see the detections section. What’s happening Storm-0940 is a China-state aligned actor (with no known industry aliases) that has been active since 2021 and targets organizations across multiple industries for data theft. The actor has historically gained initial access using password spraying and exploitation of internet-facing applications, before using compromised accounts to move through targeted environments. In cloud environments, the actor has also established persistence by adding credentials to existing OAuth applications, allowing continued access to Microsoft 365 resources. The actor has used these applications to access and exfiltrate email and other data through Microsoft Graph. As early as September 2026, Storm-0940 used the agentic AI penetration testing framework ARTEX to automate attacks targeting hundreds of organizations globally across a broad range of industries. These include aerospace and defense, automotive, chemicals, energy, financial services, government, legal, manufacturing, media, military, semiconductor, software, technology, and think tanks and non-profits. During the ConnectWise supply chain compromise, Storm-0940 used ARTEX to accelerate scanning and reconnaissance, password spraying, and OAuth application abuse at downstream victims. Several of these Storm-0940 ARTEX-driven attacks have resulted in the actor obtaining privileged access to an organization’s cloud infrastructure – including multiple instances where the time from initial targeting to privileged access was less than 3 hours. When automated with AI, these activities generate a large volume of traffic and log data that may generate alerts. However, the speed of the activity allows very little time for Defenders to respond to those alerts and remediate the actor’s access in a coordinated fashion. Storm-0940’s activity illustrates how a threat actor with a broad target set can use an agentic AI tool and privileged authentication material to quickly attempt to compromise many organizations. While the majority of the targeting wasn’t successful, Storm-0940 achieved privileged cloud persistence at many organizations in a brief time. What is ARTEX? ARTEX is an open-source penetration testing framework that can use any AI model to automate a variety of offensive security workflows. The ARTEX framework is a multi-agent LLM system written in GO with a Next.js frontend. While the UI and generated output default to English, the framework can be switched to Korean, Mandarin, Spanish, or Japanese. ARTEX is a system of LLM-driven agents that run an autonomous penetration test from reconnaissance through data exfiltration with little needed human interaction. The agents then accumulate the discovered assets and vulnerabilities into a graph during the penetration test. In October 2026, public reporting noted that the ARTEX framework was being used by threat actors, such as the personal data breaches targeting Korean financial institutions. Microsoft Threat Intelligence has identified threat actors across multiple geographic regions leveraging the ARTEX framework. ConnectWise published an initial advisory on October 16, 2026, and subsequently expanded its disclosures and containment guidance. The advisory details ConnectWise’s investigation of attempted unauthorized access associated with Cloud Backup. Following the unauthorized access, Storm-0940 used ARTEX to attempt to sign-in to more than 2,250 tenants at 2,900 sign-in attempts per minute, a pace that leaves defenders with little time to detect and respond. While OAuth application abuse is a typical part of Storm-0940’s toolset, in this case the actor used ARTEX to accelerate the activity to machine speed. Figure 1. Scale and speed of a subset of Storm-0940’s automated ARTEX campaign on downstream tenants, including tenant reach and rapid sign-ins. Password Spraying Between October 10 and 14, 2026, Storm-0940 infrastructure at 144.172.64[.]99 conducted an automated cross-tenant password-spray campaign targeting 327 accounts across eight organizations to gain access to Microsoft 365 and Exchange Online. During this large campaign, Microsoft telemetry captured 151,000+ failed logon events, with activity peaking on October 11 at 2,900 failures per minute and concentrating more than 111,000 failures into a single day. The campaign blended broad, low-rate account spraying with sustained, high-volume authentication attempts against selected identities; 48 accounts received more than 1,000 failures, and lockout responses represented nearly 98% of all activity. Despite the scale, no successful authentication, token issuance, Graph call, or directory change was observed. Downstream customer access On October 15, immediately after this notable activity, public reports of the compromise of the popular cloud backup application ConnectWise started to surface with direct references to indicators of compromise matching the same network range observed for the earlier password-spray campaign (144.172.64.x). An advisory from the affected cloud backup vendor posted on October 16 shared later additional indicators with one IP address matching exactly the 144.172.64[.]99 infrastructure responsible for the earlier password-spray campaign. Additionally, after acquiring control of the identities of multiple ConnectWise cloud applications, the AI agents operating on behalf of the threat actor continued to use the same IP range to authenticate to customer environments using the illicit access acquired through these stolen app credentials, effectively taking advantage of the already established trust by these apps within a tenant. In some of these environments, the AI agents (on behalf of the threat actor) progressed beyond authentication to Microsoft Graph and directory operations, including reconnaissance, application credential modifications to establish persistence, and changes involving identities, groups, and privileged roles. Microsoft identified multiple customer environments with evidence of follow-on activity, highlighting how a compromised third-party application with high privileges could provide access beyond its originally intended purpose. The application credential modification made by Storm-0940’s associated agents in the targeted victims’ environments can lead to persistence through other identities, credentials, and cloud services. These changes can remain active as a backdoor even after the original compromised application is mediated. Organizations should examine service principal sign-ins alongside subsequent Microsoft Graph activity, directory changes, and resource access, correlating suspicious events with authorized administrative activity. The presence of an affected application, or even successful authentication, does not necessarily indicate unauthorized data access or data exfiltration. Do not delay urgent containment while waiting for exports. Review activity starting from at least October 15, 2026. Review successful and failed service-principal sign-ins separately. Investigate unfamiliar sources and activity outside expected vendor infrastructure; do not limit review to the published IPs. Review Microsoft Entra audit records for application or service-principal credential additions; new applications, service principals, or owners; app-role assignments and consent grants; privileged role assignments; and changes to users, groups, authentication methods, or Conditional Access. On confirmed suspicious activity, revoke unnecessary permissions and rotate credentials. Review Microsoft 365 audit records to look for reconnaissance activities like listing directories, permissions, and approvals coming from unusual IPs. Review Microsoft 365 audit records for unusual mail or file access, downloads, forwarding, inbox or transport rules, delegated mailbox permissions, and related resource activity. Preserve evidence, then have an appropriately authorized responder remove confirmed unauthorized credentials, grants, owners, assignments, and identities. Introduction Back in October 2022, Kaspersky GReAT was looking into a peculiar advertisement on a hacking forum. A cybercriminal had been advertising a UEFI bootkit, dubbed BlackLotus, that could bypass Secure Boot, for $5,000. Given the low price for such powerful capabilities, the cybersecurity community initially took this advertisement with a grain of salt. As no BlackLotus samples were found in the wild, no one could at the time verify the claims made in the forum post. BlackLotus advertisement However, about half a year later, in March 2023, ESET confirmed that the BlackLotus advertisement was genuine. The researchers tracked down samples of this bootkit and confirmed that it can indeed bypass Secure Boot through a vulnerability called Baton Drop (CVE-2022-21894). As this vulnerability was still exploitable in 2023, Microsoft had to release multiple security updates to address it. Since the release of these updates, the BlackLotus bootkit has gone quiet — no publicly known attacks have used it. Yet in early 2026, we discovered a malware sample similar to BlackLotus that was used in an APT campaign in Central Asia. In this report, we share our findings, including new payloads and possible attribution of this campaign. The return of the infamous bootkit In early 2026 we identified a complex APT attack targeting diplomatic agencies in Central Asian countries — namely Tajikistan, Uzbekistan, and Turkmenistan. This attack involved a UEFI bootkit that we dubbed BlackX. Having examined it, we found it to be similar to BlackLotus. Attackers have been deploying BlackX after compromising the victim machine networks and obtaining administrator privileges. To install the bootkit, we observed them dropping the following files: inst.exe , an installer executable PVMDIJGKAX2 , an RC4-encrypted extensionless file containing files and strings required for the installation The installer was launched with the command line C:\ProgramData\Intel\inst.exe PVMDIJGKAX2 Q46F6MDLI3RX2KV1 . The last argument here is the RC4 key used for decryption. Curiously, the file inst.exe does not contain any malicious strings or embedded blobs. All the files needed to install the malicious payload are inside PVMDIJGKAX2 , which makes it harder for incident responders to retrieve the bootkit components. The installer package drops a set of files to both system and UEFI partitions, designed to exploit the Baton Drop vulnerability. We noted that the implementation of the exploit is remarkably similar to the one used in the BlackLotus malware. As with BlackLotus, the goal of the exploit is to install custom Machine Owner Keys (MOK), which allows loading arbitrary EFI files on the target machines to manipulate Secure Boot. While the vulnerability exploitation chain was implemented very similarly to BlackLotus, we spotted differences between the exploits in BlackLotus and BlackX. That said, the source code of the BlackLotus bootkit has never been published online. This could mean that the attackers behind BlackX either reverse-engineered the BlackLotus bootkit or purchased its source code from cybercriminals. Either way, this is an interesting observation, as we rarely see APT actors borrow ideas or code from cybercrime actors. Payload launched by the bootkit The UEFI payload delivered during the Baton Drop exploitation has a classic bootkit goal — to inject a malicious driver into the Windows kernel. This driver differs from the one deployed by BlackLotus and is signed with a certificate belonging to an organization named 温江区明宇网络技术服务工作室 (English: Wenjiang District Mingyu Network Technology Service Studio). At the time of writing, this certificate (serial number: 0bcdf5e0da32d68900b8b7638459d072) has been revoked by its issuer. The driver aims to tamper with security solutions installed on the machine. For example, it disables process, thread, and registry callbacks related to security solutions. It also tampers with ETW providers so that security solutions cannot access their output. Curiously, some of the tampering procedures are designed to run only if Kaspersky or Avast solutions are installed on the infected machine. However, during dynamic analysis of this driver, we found that these procedures are useless — they fail to interfere with Kaspersky products in any way. The driver also protects the bootkit’s files from tampering. It intercepts kernel requests to open these files and returns a handle to calc.exe instead of a malicious file handle. User-mode second-stage payloads Apart from tampering with security solutions, the driver also deploys a user-mode payload. We observed two such payloads, used for cyberespionage, in the wild. One of them is a RAT that can load further-stage .NET payloads. This RAT uses cloud storage for C2 server communications — we observed its versions using Google Drive, OneDrive, and Yandex.Disk. Having connected to a cloud storage service, this RAT iterates over files available there and interprets them depending on the file suffix. Below is a list of supported suffixes: File suffix Description Type 0X01 A .NET file. The task is to parse it and run the method TEST.Class1.RunCMD(argument_array) from the module Input 0X02 Result of the call TEST.Class1.RunCMD(argument_array) Output 0X03 Task to upload a file to the drive Input 0X04 Uploaded file Output 0X05 Task to download a file to an infected machine Input 0X06 File download status Output SSSR Contains initial system info Output We also observed another payload delivered through the bootkit, which we dubbed OxiDLLRunner. It is a malicious DLL obfuscated with OLLVM. It is loaded by the legitimate file googleupdate.exe (original filename: jcef_helper.exe , MD5: AF8A983B9ADA07EAE1259BF2953ABD40), which is also delivered by the bootkit. The DLL decrypts the contents of the file v8_context_snapshot.bin , which resides in the same folder, and then jumps to the beginning of the decrypted content. The decrypted data starts with shellcode followed by a PE file, which is a Rust payload designed to communicate with the www[.]archivetj[.]com C2 server. During communication, this payload is supposed to receive DLL files exporting a function named create_plugin , which are then executed in separate threads. Attribution Based on the following facts, we have attributed this attack to the APT41 threat actor with medium confidence: In June 2026, ESET published a blog post on attacks by the FishMonger threat actor, which the community assumes to be related to APT41. This post describes a kernel implant with a PDB path starting with C:\Users\ xdd \Desktop. While describing the campaign, the post also suggests the possible use of an unspecified UEFI bootkit component, which might exploit CVE‑2023‑24932. We observed the identifier “xdd” used multiple times in the BlackX bootkit payloads, so we assess that BlackX is the UEFI bootkit referenced in the ESET blog post; The payloads deployed by BlackX are loaded using a legitimate executable abused via DLL sideloading, and an encrypted file — this pattern is common among Chinese-speaking threat actors; The OxiDLLRunner file has been obfuscated with OLLVM, an obfuscator commonly used by Chinese-speaking actors. Why did the target organizations get infected? During our initial analysis of BlackX, we thought that attackers succeeded in deploying it due to the negligence of the target organizations. Baton Drop, the vulnerability used to deploy BlackX, was disclosed back in 2023 and was patched, so we first thought that the infected machines lacked the relevant updates. However, our investigation proved this wrong — the targeted machines were fully patched. Instead, we found out that the problem was with the patch itself. As it turned out, the patch for the Baton Drop vulnerability is designed to revoke the “Windows Production PCA 2011” certificate used to sign vulnerable UEFI components. However, this revocation does not happen automatically upon patch installation — the user has to trigger it manually . This is done with the following steps: A registry flag is set inside HKEY_LOCAL_MACHINE\SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Control\Secureboot; A scheduled task named Secure-Boot-Update is created. This patch is designed to be applied manually — for instance, applying it automatically could break dual-boot systems relying on the “Windows Production PCA 2011” certificate to load. At the same time, this patch deployment practice creates a problem — as it is extremely unusual for a system administrator to take extra steps after installing an update. Usually, when applying patches, system administrators take no further action unless an installation failure occurs. These instructions for activating the patch are buried deep on the Microsoft website, and we assess that they are quite hard for an average IT administrator to notice. Curiously, Microsoft’s plan for deploying the Baton Drop patches mentions an “Enforcement Phase” that involves revoking the “Windows Production PCA 2011” certificate automatically, without user interaction. At the time of publishing this post, this stage has not been implemented. On October 19, 2026, this certificate expired, but this does not prevent the vulnerable binaries from loading unless the certificate is blocklisted. It is likely that the threat actor behind this campaign took into account that few organizations realized the importance of manually activating the Baton Drop patch. As such, we believe they decided to invest time in understanding how BlackLotus works and creating a copycat of this bootkit. Conclusion Patch management is important for every organization, as updates from vendors help protect software and hardware against known vulnerabilities. However, sometimes installed patches fail to protect against the flaws they address. The BlackLotus story we described is not the only such example — for instance, in 2025 Microsoft released an update against the ToolShell vulnerability that also had to be applied manually. However, modern security solutions are designed to account for such cases when installed patches fail to protect against security flaws for one reason or another. For instance, the Exploit Prevention component available in Kaspersky products prevents exploits from running if the patch is not applied, or if it is a zero-day vulnerability with no available patch. It is thus crucial to equip all machines in an organization with security solutions and constantly monitor the alerts they raise.
