VOLTA is a mature stealer implant with an optional AI-integration layer. After stealing credentials, sessions, and cryptocurrency wallets, VOLTA sends a structured evidence bundle to an LLM to rank the victim’s assets by monetization value. If a model refuses to perform the target assessment, VOLTA fails over to a three-model chain, implying a direct engineering effort and expectation of hitting model guardrails. This stealer is self-deleting, and there is no attacker-owned infrastructure. The novelty in the AI-integration is that it is not supporting a traditional malware capability but working as an intelligence analyst on behalf of the operator. There is a new idea in the cybercrime world — cast a wide net, steal everything, and let AI sort out the specifics. A new malware self-named “VOLTA-AUTODOXER,” surfaced through the CAIRN research effort , is a self-deleting Windows infostealer that augments its post-theft workflow with an LLM-powered victim-profiling stage. After harvesting the standard stealer haul, including browser passwords; cookies; payment cards; cryptocurrency wallet seed phrases; Discord, Telegram, Steam, and Roblox sessions; Microsoft OAuth tokens; SSH and cloud-provider keys; Wi-Fi profiles; and screenshots, VOLTA builds a structured evidence bundle about the person behind the machine. This bundle contains geolocation and home address, employer and employer domain, inferred birth year, card last-4 digits, Slack workspaces, document authorship metadata, iPhone backup indicators, and a live Roblox account valuation. It then uses stolen session access to enrich that bundle further: searching the victim's Gmail for purchase receipts to corroborate a home address, pulling UberEats and DoorDash order history, and enriching Discord accounts via the stolen token. The bundle is serialized and POSTed to OpenRouter under a system prompt that instructs the model to act as "a forensic intelligence analyst drafting a target assessment." The required output includes a section titled “WHAT THEY'RE WORTH” with valuables ranked by monetization with a low/medium/high tier, and a section on “THEIR OPSEC” containing an assessment of the victim's security posture and password hygiene. The model is the operator's triage analyst; it turns a raw credential log into a prioritized and citation-backed dossier of the victim and their monetizable assets. Figure 1. The VOLTA-AUTODOXER workflow. The AI configuration: “Go deep, not wide... Be blunt, specific, accurate.” VOLTA uses a multi-model architecture to fall back resiliently if it hits a model’s refusal, checking each response for phrases like “I can’t” or “I’m not able,” before passing on to a chain of up to three models. Endpoint https[ :]// openrouter [ .]ai/ api /v1/chat/completions Default model anthropic/claude-opus-4.6 Fallback chain moonshotai /kimi-k2 → google/gemini-2.5-flash Temperature 0.2 — deterministic, consistent output Request format Hand-assembled JSON; no SDK; User-Agent Mozilla/5.0 Figure 2. Hardcoded model selection. Figure 3. Failover model selection to kimi-k2, and then gemini-2.5-flash. Model temperate controls the randomness and predictability of LLM-generated text. The low hardcoded temperature value (0.2) suggests the attacker is emphasizing accuracy and consistency. The malware also employs a chain of cascading models in the event of failures. This multi-model architecture suggests that the operator is anticipating encountering refusals, with the fallback pivoting to a few, smaller, and more permissive models. The system prompt directly reveals the operator’s use case: Produce a concise analytical assessment of the victim’s suitability as a target, backed by an auditable trail of citations. Figure 4. VOLTA's raw system prompt. The prompt also asks for something out of the ordinary, but that agents excel at: making connections between sparse and disparate data. For example, encouraging connections like the victim’s names, addresses, or universities to patterns in their passwords. In this case, the model isn't being asked to find data; rather, it’s being asked to stretch the data and make useful inferences that extend the value of what has been scraped. Stealer capabilities VOLTA’s capabilities are directly aligned with the records produced in modern digital life, and well beyond basic credential theft. In a single pass, the stealer can extract browser credentials, cookies, and saved payment cards across Chromium-based browsers. It hits Firefox and Thunderbird, Outlook profiles, Slack workspaces, Steam and Battle.net sessions, Discord tokens, Telegram sessions, Roblox accounts, and SSH and cloud-provider config files (gcloud, OpenVPN, Hide.me). VOLTA doesn’t just copy cryptocurrency wallets, but runs an embedded cracker against the vault inline, writing the result to a WALLET VAULTS CRACKED line in its own report. Every saved Wi-Fi password on the machine is pulled via a direct call to WlanGetProfileList and dumped to plaintext. Figure 5. VOLTA dumps plaintext passwords for every saved Wi-Fi network on the host. Figure 6. The organized collection targets. What really sets VOLTA apart from commodity stealers is what comes after the initial collection. There is a live enrichment pass that leverages stolen sessions to deep dive the victim’s data. For example, using the victim's own authenticated Gmail tokens, it queries their inbox for receipts from Amazon, DoorDash, Uber Eats, Grubhub, Walmart, and PayPal, then review those receipts to establish a confirmed home address. Stolen Uber Eats and DoorDash sessions are called directly for order history. The Discord token is re-used to enrich the victim's account profile. Roblox inventory is valued via a live “Rolimons[.]com” lookup. Figure 7. That’s right: Your Uber Eats history is now a potential liability. After the collection and enrichment stages are complete, the data is assembled and structured locally in a file called “Identity_Report.txt”. This file is serialized into a bundle as JSON and POSTed to OpenRouter along with the system prompt. The returned payload is received as “AI_Analysis.txt”. Both files exfil together in “volta_dox.zip”. Figure 8. VOLTA’s exfil can happen in three stages: 1) after the stealer runs (“volta_log.zip”), 2) after the AI call (“volta_dox.zip”), (3) if the network enrichment produces anything (“volta_doxnet.zip”). The inference round-trip happens entirely through the victim's own network connection, on their own machine, before the result is exfiltrated. Deployment model Instead of hardcoding its Telegram channel, API keys, and settings directly into the code (which is likely what someone writing a tool for personal use would do) VOLTA uses a template with six swappable slots, filled in at build time. This suggests it’s a builder model: The author sells access, the customer provides their values, and the builder compiles a custom binary for each order. The credentials in those slots are encrypted, but not in a meaningful way. The key is SHA-256 of two constants sitting in the same binary, but it's presumably there because customers expect their API keys not to be sitting in plaintext, not because it provides real protection. Anyone who reverses the file can recover them. If VOLTA is indeed a commoditized malware, then the AI dossier capability isn't something the author invented for their own operations. It is a feature which enables a customer of any level of sophistication to point it at victims and start receiving ranked target assessments in their Telegram inbox. All the observed endpoints for this malware are third-party controlled. There is no actor-owned infrastructure. Countermeasures Much of the token-stealing capability of VOLTA relies on techniques to bypass App-Bound Encryption (ABE) in Chrome. An application control policy that restricts which processes may launch Chrome with --headless would block the ABE decryption path entirely. At minimum, you can also alert when Chrome is launched with --headless from any process not in a known developer. Best practices for storing credentials in hardware backed stores also apply. In the event of compromise, the main executable may have deleted itself, but users should attempt to preserve the “APPDATA%\volta_debug.txt” file. This is a timestamped harvest log and contains a record of exactly what was collected and whether the LLM call succeeded. Revoke any potentially compromised tokens and change passwords. For cryptocurrency wallets, if the seed phrase was in plaintext on disk, treat the wallet as fully compromised and migrate funds to a new wallet with a seed generated on an air-gapped device. Conclusions VOLTA is a mature stealer with a late-stage AI layer added for one purpose: relieving the operator of analytical work. The axis of AI improvement here is not speed or scale but effort displacement; the same logic visible in CLOSEDQUORUM , though applied to a different phase. These implants reflect the same underlying logic: Identify the step that requires the most sustained human attention and replace it with a model call. The question for defenders is not whether this pattern will appear elsewhere, but which phase it touches next. Figure 9. End-to-end VOLTA workflow. The second trend to watch is the anticipation of hitting model refusals, and the approaches to building in resiliency. In this case, it includes a fallback chain from Claude to Kimi to Gemini and the ability to run without the LLM stage altogether if no key is present. As AI integration in malware matures, refusal resilience may become a standard design requirement. The open question underlying both trends is whether effort displacement in crimeware changes the landscape in a meaningful way: lowering the operational floor, raising the ceiling on scale, or both. Cybercrime operations have always been opportunistic, and the sophistication bar has always been lower than assumed, but there is a difference between a wide net and a wide net that sorts itself. Cursor reports that it was live from about 2:27 AM to 1:35 PM Pacific Daylight Time (PDT). We have confirmed that the malicious packages found on Cursor marketplace were never published nor distributed from Microsoft Visual Studio Marketplace and from npm @antropic-ai/claude-code, which were not affected by this incident. Anthropic confirmed to Microsoft that their systems were not compromised and they did not develop the malicious package posted in Cursor marketplace. At this time, all evidence suggests that the incident is limited to Cursor marketplace. Microsoft Threat Intelligence analyzed the injected JavaScript embedded in the malicious extension package. The injected loader runs when the extension module loads and attempts to add an attacker-controlled Secure Shell (SSH) key, enable the local SSH service, establish reverse SSH forwarding, configure persistence, and report host details. Successful remote access depends on host privileges, SSH configuration, and the attacker’s server configuration; however, the loader may still write keys and persistence artifacts even when tunnel access fails. Organizations should identify systems that installed or automatically updated to this version from the Cursor marketplace, preserve evidence, remove confirmed malicious artifacts, review endpoint and network telemetry, and rotate credentials or revoke sessions when investigation indicates exposure. Microsoft customers can use the detections and hunting guidance in this post after the listed coverage and query validation gates are complete. Attack chain overview A malicious artifact associated with the Cursor Marketplace incident included a JavaScript function that executed before the extension’s expected code. When the module loads, this function can use Node.js APIs to collect host information, modify files, and execute operating-system commands. The following sections describe capabilities and attempted actions based on our analysis of the injected code. Attack chain The loader first collects host reconnaissance. It then attempts to install an SSH key, enable SSH, create a reverse tunnel, and configure persistence. A delayed callback subsequently attempts to send host details. The following sequence reflects code execution order; it does not prove that every step succeeded on every host. Module loading and reconnaissance collection. The injected function executes before normal extension initialization and collects host and user information. SSH access preparation. The loader appends an embedded public key and attempts to enable the local SSH service. Reverse forwarding. An embedded tunnel identity authenticates an outbound connection that requests forwarding to the workstation’s SSH port. Platform-specific persistence. Scripts and startup configuration are written to relaunch the connection. Host reconnaissance reporting. After the access and persistence setup attempts, a delayed callback attempts to transmit the previously collected host details and selected environment-variable names. Figure 1. Attack chain reconstructed from the injected code. The loader uses embedded components and built-in system tools to establish persistent access without downloading another executable. It suppresses command output and silently catches errors to conceal failures and malicious activity, while the extension continues functioning normally to avoid alerting users. Host reconnaissance collection The loader collects the hostname, username, operating system, architecture, home directory, working directory, and global Git email. It also looks for environment-variable names containing terms such as KEY, TOKEN, SECRET, PASS, AWS, or GCP, keeping up to forty matches. The callback sends matching environment-variable names, not values. This reveals potentially sensitive configuration categories but does not demonstrate theft of the secrets stored in those variables. Collection occurs during module loading. The collected details are retained for the delayed reporting callback described after the persistence stage. SSH authorization and tunnel identities The loader uses two SSH identities with distinct roles. One public key authorizes access to the workstation’s user account, while a separate private key authenticates the outbound connection to the tunnel server. First, the loader checks the user’s authorized_keys file for the comment svc-telemetry. If the marker is missing, it appends an embedded Ed25519 public key. That creates an authorization entry for whoever holds the corresponding private key, subject to the local SSH server’s configuration. Next, it decodes a separate private key for the outbound connection. The initial copy is written to a hidden temporary file named .stk_ followed by ten hexadecimal characters derived from the hostname and home directory. SSH receives that file through its -i option and connects using the remote account tunnel. The public-key material is redacted but the format is similar to the following: ssh-ed25519 [REDACTED PUBLIC KEY] svc-telemetry Protocol Tunneling (T1572): reverse SSH forwarding The tunneling SSH process is launched with the command line containing the following arguments: -N -R '*:0:localhost:22' It requests a listening port on the remote SSH server and forwards connections through the tunnel to port 22 on the victim’s loopback interface. The remote port value 0 asks the server to allocate a port; it does not change the local target from port 22. The -N option tells SSH not to run a command on the tunnel server. This design starts with an outbound connection from the workstation rather than waiting for a direct inbound connection to it. The loader disables ordinary host-key checking, bypasses the normal known-hosts file, sets keepalives, and avoids interactive prompts. On Unix-like systems it prefers autossh, falling back to ssh; on Windows it uses a hidden PowerShell loop. The loops wait sixty seconds after SSH exits before retrying. Figure 2. Terminal-style SSH command configuring an outbound reverse tunnel from a server-assigned remote port to the workstation’s local SSH service on port 22. Host-key verification is disabled, and the identity-file path is redacted. A running SSH process does not necessarily mean the attacker gained access. Both the attacker’s server and the victim’s workstation must allow the connection, and server settings determine whether the tunnel is publicly reachable. Cursor reports that errors and administrator-permission requirements blocked SSH access on most macOS and Windows hosts. However, the malware could still attempt to plant its SSH key, establish persistence, and report host information. Platform-specific startup persistence The loader writes platform-specific scripts and startup configuration outside the extension directory. The following table summarizes these mechanisms and their associated artifacts. The code attempts to configure persistence, but the presence of an artifact alone does not establish that the startup mechanism ran successfully. Platform Startup mechanism Key artifacts macOS User LaunchAgent with RunAtLoad and KeepAlive ~/Library/LaunchAgents/com.apple.sysmond.plist, ~/.config/.smt, ~/.config/.smtk Linux User-crontab @reboot entry ~/.config/.smt, ~/.config/.smtk Windows Attempted SysMon scheduled task requesting SYSTEM execution %USERPROFILE%\.config\smt.cmd, %USERPROFILE%\.config\smtk Windows fallback Batch file in the user’s Startup folder %APPDATA%\Microsoft\Windows\Start Menu\Programs\Startup\sysmon.cmd On Unix-like systems, the .smt script reconstructs the persistent .smtk identity when it runs, then starts its own SSH retry loop. On Windows, the loader writes the identity and batch script directly. The startup objects use names such as SysMon and com.apple.sysmond, which resemble monitoring components. Behind these names, their configured actions invoke the generated scripts that maintain reverse SSH forwarding. Mitigation and protection guidance Immediate actions Run advanced hunting queries below to identify affected systems and investigate systems where users installed or automatically updated anthropic.claude-code version 2.1.313 and 2.1.312 through the Cursor Extension Marketplace. Review extension-installation records and correlate endpoint, network, file, process, and authentication telemetry with the published indicators. Preserve the extension package, modified SSH authorization files, generated keys and scripts, and startup configuration before cleanup. Isolate devices with evidence of loader execution or backdoor installation. Remediation actions After evidence collection, check the affected user’s ~/.ssh/authorized_keys on macOS and Linux for the attacker-controlled key labeled svc-telemetry. On Windows, check both %USERPROFILE%\.ssh\authorized_keys and C:\ProgramData\ssh\administrators_authorized_keys, where present. Confirm the key against the fingerprint in Indicators of Compromise before removing the exact unauthorized entry. Preserve unrelated SSH keys and legitimate remote-development configuration. A missing marker alone should not replace review of the installation, process, network, and persistence evidence. Remove the trojanized extension and confirmed malicious persistence after evidence collection. Inspect each startup object’s configured action before removal; names alone are not sufficient to distinguish malicious objects from legitimate monitoring software. macOS: Unload and remove the malicious user LaunchAgent at ~/Library/LaunchAgents/com.apple.sysmond.plist and remove its associated ~/.config/.smt script and ~/.config/.smtk identity. Linux: Remove the malicious user-crontab @reboot entry referencing ~/.config/.smt, preserving unrelated entries, and remove the associated script and ~/.config/.smtk identity. Windows: Remove the malicious SysMon scheduled task, the Startup-folder sysmon.cmd fallback, and associated %USERPROFILE%\.config\smt.cmd and %USERPROFILE%\.config\smtk files. All applicable platforms: Stop confirmed malicious SSH and retry processes and remove their temporary .stk_ identity files after collection. Cursor published platform-specific checks and remediation scripts in its security notice . Review the scripts and execution requirements before use, then verify that no listed artifacts remain. Cleanup does not replace investigation of device activity during the exposure window. Removing or updating the extension does not remove artifacts written outside its installation directory. Remove confirmed unauthorized SSH keys, tunnel identities, scripts, and startup entries separately. Cursor’s reported server takedown does not remove artifacts already present on a device. Follow-up Investigation and recovery Review telemetry after the extension loaded for SSH key acceptance, tunnel processes, local SSH sessions, command execution, and file activity. Rotate credentials and revoke sessions from a known-clean device when telemetry indicates credential access or when device trust cannot be restored. Rebuild the device when required by the investigation and verify that the tunnel and startup artifacts do not return after login or restart. Once connected, the threat actors staged portable Python, Bun, or Java runtimes, performed host and network discovery, and assessed Active Directory Certificate Services (AD CS) for certificate-based authentication paths. Although the incidents began with the same social-engineering and remote-access pattern, they diverged at the certificate layer. In Case 1, the threat actor abused an Enterprise CA Security Configuration 1 (ESC1) -vulnerable certificate template to obtain authentication certificates for privileged accounts and performed DCSync. In Case 2, the threat actor used PetitPotam, a technique that causes a Windows server to authenticate to an attacker-controlled system. The attacker relayed the domain controller’s New Technology (NT) LAN Manager (NTLM) authentication to AD CS Web Enrollment (ESC8), obtained a certificate for the domain-controller account, and used it for certificate-based Kerberos authentication (PKINIT – Public Key Cryptography for Initial Authentication). This intrusion pattern is high impact because a user-approved remote session can become certificate-backed access to privileged identities and domain infrastructure. In Case 1, Microsoft Defender attack disruption restricted covered identities and blocked substantial follow-on activity, but a privileged identity excluded from automated containment remained usable and later supported data exfiltration. In Case 2, Defender generated ESC8-related alerts, after which the customer isolated the associated workstation and no further progression was observed. The activity did not exploit a vulnerability in Microsoft Teams; instead, it relied on social engineering and insecure or overly permissive AD CS configurations. In this blog, we analyze the shared attack chain, the ESC1 and ESC8 certificate-abuse paths, and the different response outcomes. We also provide investigation methods, mitigation recommendations, Microsoft Defender detection guidance, and advanced hunting queries to help defenders identify and interrupt this access path. The activity described here is not attributed to a specific threat actor. Activity overview Risk to enterprise environments AD CS can turn a certificate request into a durable authentication path. An attacker who obtains an authentication certificate for a privileged user or machine account can use that certificate independently of the initially compromised password. Resetting the user’s password or reimaging the first workstation does not revoke an issued certificate or invalidate Kerberos tickets that have already been issued. Two AD CS abuse techniques are central to these incidents: ESC1 – certificate template abuse: A low-privileged requester abuses a misconfigured authentication-capable template that permits requester-supplied identity information and broad enrollment, allowing the requester to obtain a certificate for a privileged account. ESC8 – NTLM relay to Web Enrollment: An attacker relays a user or machine account’s NTLM authentication to an AD CS web enrollment endpoint that lacks relay protections, then requests a certificate as the relayed identity. Attackers can use Microsoft Teams to impersonate IT support personnel and persuade users to approve a remote session, gaining hands-on-keyboard access without a conventional malware attachment. Portable or renamed runtimes such as Python, Bun, and Java can execute attacker-controlled scripts from user-writable locations while retaining legitimate file metadata. Automated disruption is most effective when every compromised identity and device remains eligible for containment; exclusions can preserve the attacker’s path. Attack chain overview Both incidents followed the same initial sequence: help desk impersonation through Teams, a user-approved Quick Assist or other remote-support session, archive transfer and extraction, portable runtime execution, and host and AD CS discovery. The certificate technique and response outcome then split into two distinct branches. Figure 1. Both incidents followed a shared entry and execution pattern before diverging into two AD CS abuse paths. Case 1 used an ESC1-vulnerable certificate template to obtain certificates for privileged identities. Case 2 used PetitPotam to coerce domain-controller authentication and relay it to AD CS Web Enrollment through ESC8. The response outcomes differed based on containment coverage and removal of the active execution point. Shared initial access and execution pattern Teams helpdesk impersonation and remote access Both incidents began with Teams phishing messages and calls that impersonated support personnel. The social engineering persuaded a user to establish an attacker-controlled remote session , primarily by guiding the user to open Quick Assist and provide the connection code. This gave the attacker hands-on-keyboard access to the workstation. Across the broader activity, organization-themed onmicrosoft.com tenant names and support-oriented display names were used to make the external contact appear familiar. Figure 2. Example Teams call displaying External and Scam suspected indicators. These labels are important user-facing signals during support impersonation attempts. The remote session was the decisive transition in the attack. Immediately after the user granted control through Quick Assist, the attacker used the session to place an archive in the victim’s Downloads folder. Commands launched during the session created a KB-prefixed staging directory under the user’s profile, extracted the archive into that directory, and started the included payload with a portable Python, JavaScript, or Java runtime. The payload then performed host and network discovery and began assessing AD CS. Portable runtime staging and execution variants The runtime and packaging varied between deployments, but the execution pattern remained consistent: an archive was placed on the device during the remote session, extracted into a newly created user-profile directory, and used to launch attacker-controlled code. In one JavaScript variant, a renamed executable retained version metadata identifying it as the Bun runtime and launched the staged loader. A separate variant used a staged Java runtime to launch a Java source file. Figure 3. Sanitized Bun staging command. The update-themed path, renamed runtime, script operand, and remote-session context form the useful investigation pattern. File names alone are weak indicators. The actionable pattern is a portable runtime launched from a newly created, user-writable staging directory after an unauthorized remote session, followed by script execution, discovery commands, or connections to identity infrastructure. Figure 4. Sanitized Java staging variant. The campaign could substitute runtime and packaging choices while keeping the broader access pattern. The Java variant confirms that runtime choice can change between deployments. The AD CS behavior in each incident should be validated through the initiating process, account, device, and certificate records rather than assumed from the presence of Bun or Java alone. Host discovery and AD CS assessment The staged components queried Windows version information, network configuration, and running processes. These commands are common in administration, but they become high-value signals when it is launched by a renamed runtime from an uncommon location and follow an unexpected remote-support session. Figure 5. Host-discovery commands from the first draft. Their value comes from the initiating staged runtime, user-writable path, and preceding unauthorized remote session. The compromised workstation also connected to domain infrastructure using LDAPS and SMB. From this shared workstation foothold, the incidents followed different certificate-abuse paths. In Case 1, the attacker exploited an ESC1-vulnerable certificate template. In Case 2, the attacker used PetitPotam to coerce domain-controller authentication and relay it to AD CS Web Enrollment through ESC8. Case 1: ESC1 abuse and the cost of an identity exclusion ESC1 certificate requests expand privilege The first Teams-phishing wave targeted three user accounts and compromised one workstation. From the shared foothold described above, the attacker examined AD CS for a privilege-escalation path. Figure 6. Case 1: Help desk impersonation led to ESC1 certificate abuse. Attack disruption restricted most compromised identities, but an excluded privileged account remained usable and later supported data exfiltration. The attacker found a certificate template vulnerable to ESC1. ESC1 describes a template configuration in which a requester can supply identity information in the certificate request and obtain a certificate that supports authentication. When broad enrollment rights and weak issuance controls are combined with that setting, a low-privileged account can request a certificate representing a privileged identity. In the first wave, the attacker obtained certificates for three highly privileged accounts. Microsoft Defender attack disruption targeted the initially compromised user and those three certificate-compromised identities. Remote logons and Server Message Block (SMB) access were restricted and active sessions were disconnected. One privileged account was excluded from automated response, so containment did not apply to that identity. Renewed phishing and ESC1 privilege expansion About 90 minutes after the first disrupted session, the attacker launched a larger Teams-phishing wave targeting 16 users and compromised another workstation. The attacker again abused the ESC1-vulnerable template, this time obtaining certificates for eight additional privileged accounts. Attack disruption targeted the newly compromised user and the eight certificate-compromised identities. More than 1,000 subsequent SMB file-access attempts and additional logon attempts were blocked, limiting the attacker’s ability to reuse those accounts for lateral movement. DCSync, the excluded account, and data theft Within seconds of obtaining one privileged certificate, the attacker authenticated to a domain controller and performed DCSync while containment was taking effect. Attack disruption limited additional replication and follow-on use, but the operation had already begun. The privileged account excluded during the first disruption remained available. The attacker continued to use it for network and remote-interactive logons, reached a file server, and launched a renamed copy of Rclone using the filename SearchApp.exe. The command recursively copied data from a file-server share to an external Secure File Transfer Protocol (SFTP) destination, used 20 concurrent transfers, selected files up to three years old, and excluded several executable, database, virtual-disk, media, temporary, and log file types. Figure 7. Sanitized Rclone command used to copy data from a file-server share to an external SFTP destination. The incident demonstrates that partial identity containment can reduce attack activity while still leaving a viable privileged route. Case 2: PetitPotam and ESC8 turn a workstation into a domain-controller identity PetitPotam coerces domain-controller authentication The ESC1 path used in the first case was not available in this environment. Instead, the attacker used PetitPotam to send requests over the Encrypting File System Remote Protocol (EFSRPC), causing the domain controller to authenticate to an attacker-controlled system using its machine account. The attacker then relayed that NTLM authentication to AD CS Web Enrollment without knowing the domain controller’s password. Figure 8. Case 2: Help desk impersonation led to PetitPotam authentication coercion, ESC8 relay to AD CS, certificate-based Kerberos authentication, and customer-led device isolation. NTLM relay reaches AD CS Web Enrollment The attacker relayed the domain controller’s NTLM authentication to AD CS Web Enrollment. This technique is known as ESC8. It targets an enrollment web endpoint that accepts NTLM without sufficient relay protection, allowing the attacker to submit a certificate request as the relayed account. The AD CS server authenticated the domain-controller machine account even though the connection originated from another device. That identity-to-source mismatch is a strong investigation pivot: correlate the machine account, source device, Web Enrollment request, Certification Authority (CA) issuance record, and subsequent Kerberos activity. After authenticating to Web Enrollment as the domain controller, the attacker requested and retrieved a certificate using the Domain Controller template. The important service paths are the certificate submission and retrieval endpoints under /certsrv; isolated URL access is not malicious by itself and must be correlated with the authenticated identity, source device, process, and CA request. Figure 9. Sanitized view of AD CS Web Enrollment activity involving certificate submission and retrieval through /certsrv, used during ESC8 certificate issuance for a relayed domain-controller identity. Certificate-based Kerberos authentication The attacker used the newly issued domain-controller certificate for Kerberos PKINIT and obtained a ticket-granting ticket (TGT) for the domain controller. This step converted a relayed NTLM authentication into a certificate-backed Kerberos identity. PKINIT success is the response threshold: responders should investigate the requesting device, authenticated account, issued certificate, active Kerberos sessions, and any service-ticket use. Certificate revocation and Kerberos ticket review are separate containment actions. Customer-led device isolation and observed outcome Microsoft Defender generated ESC8-related alerts, after which the customer isolated the workstation associated with the certificate-enrollment and Kerberos activity. The documented containment action in this case was customer-led device isolation. No further attack progression was observed following isolation. Why the response outcomes differed In Case 1, an exclusion left a compromised privileged account available to the attacker despite disruption of other identities. In Case 2, customer-led device isolation followed Defender alerts, and no further attack progression was observed. No subsequent attack progression was documented after customer isolation. Actionable investigation methods The most useful artifacts are not individual commands or ports in isolation. Investigators should reconstruct the chain across collaboration, endpoint, identity, and certificate systems. Teams and remote access: identify the external tenant, user interaction, call or chat timing, remote-session start, approved remote monitoring and management (RMM) process, and child processes created during the session. Runtime staging: inspect newly created KB-prefixed or application-like folders, archive extraction, runtime metadata, script operands, parent-child relationships, and subsequent network activity. Directory interaction: use network data to identify LDAPS and SMB connections, then use endpoint and directory logs to determine what the initiating process did. TLS intentionally hides LDAP query content from passive network inspection. ESC1: review certificate request and issuance events, requester identity, subject and Subject Alternative Names (SAN) values, template name, enrollment permissions, and the first authentication performed with the issued certificate. PetitPotam and ESC8: correlate EFSRPC activity, outbound authentication by the domain controller, the device that relayed the authentication, Web Enrollment access, CA request and issuance records, and PKINIT from the originating workstation. Disruption and containment: enumerate every compromised identity and device, review exclusions before and during response, revoke unauthorized certificates, and assess active Kerberos tickets separately Reduce support impersonation and unauthorized remote access Require users to verify unsolicited support requests through a known internal channel before joining a call, sharing a screen, or starting a remote-control session. Encourage users to pay attention to warnings in incoming messages and calls when impersonation is detected. Review Teams external-access and federation policies. Limit external domains to business requirements and investigate newly observed organization-themed or helpdesk-themed tenants in context. Restrict remote-support software to approved products, operators, and workflows. Alert when approved RMM tools extract archives, create interpreters, or launch scripts from user-writable paths. Constrain runtime-based execution Use App Control for Business or equivalent application control to restrict unapproved portable runtimes and script execution from user-writable locations. Evaluate file identity through signer, original filename, product metadata, path, parent process, and behavior. A renamed executable can retain legitimate version information. Test relevant attack surface reduction rules and endpoint protection policies. Do not assume controls for Windows script hosts automatically cover JavaScript executed through Bun or source files launched through Java. Harden AD CS against ESC1 and ESC8 Follow Microsoft’s secure configuration guidance for mitigating NTLM relay against AD CS: https://support.microsoft.com/help/5005413 Review authentication-capable templates for requester-supplied subject information, broad enrollment permissions, weak issuance requirements, and excessive certificate purpose. Remove unnecessary enrollment rights and require approval where appropriate. Inventory AD CS Web Enrollment and Certificate Enrollment Web Service endpoints. Remove endpoints that are not required. Require HTTPS and enforce Extended Protection for Authentication (EPA) on enrollment web endpoints. HTTPS protects confidentiality; EPA provides the channel binding needed to resist NTLM relay. Disable NTLM on enrollment services where operationally feasible and validate Kerberos-only authentication before broad enforcement. Use Defender for Identity AD CS security posture assessments to identify ESC1-related template exposure and insecure enrollment Internet Information Services (IIS) endpoints associated with ESC8: https://learn.microsoft.com/defender-for-identity/security-posture-assessments/certificates Enable certificate auditing and prepare certificate-aware response Enable the Certification Services advanced audit subcategory for success and failure, and configure the Certification Authority audit filter to record request and issuance activity. Preserve CA security events, CA database records, IIS or Web Enrollment logs, endpoint process lineage, identity alerts, and Teams interaction records. If an unauthorized certificate was issued, identify its serial number and thumbprint, revoke it, publish updated revocation information, and confirm that relying systems can obtain the update. Review certificate-based Kerberos activity and active tickets. Revoking a certificate does not retroactively invalidate TGTs or service tickets that were already issued. Treat containment exclusions as incident decisions. If an account or device cannot be automatically contained, assign a compensating manual action and verify completion.
