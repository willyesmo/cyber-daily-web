In this article ARTEX: Publicly available AI-powered offensive security framework AI agent architecture Capabilities Identifying ARTEX network activity Identifying ARTEX system activity Mitigation and protection guidance Microsoft Defender detections Hunting MITRE ATT&CK techniques Since its release in July 2026, the AI-powered penetration testing framework ARTEX has been abused by many threat actors to accelerate their existing workflows related to network reconnaissance, credential attacks, and web application exploitation. As of November 2026, Microsoft Threat Intelligence has observed both state-sponsored and financially motivated threat actors such as Storm-0940 , Storm-3358 , Onyx Sleet , Pearl Sleet , Crystal Typhoon, and Salt Typhoon integrating ARTEX into their tooling. In the past few weeks, we’ve reported several distinct campaigns that abused ARTEX to search public repositories for exposed secrets, enable supply chain compromise, and scan internet-facing systems for exploitation. These campaigns illustrate how threat actors use ARTEX to automate various stages of the attack chain. Threat actors define scope and targets for ARTEX, which then uses AI agents to set verifiable goals, define tactical steps for each stage of the operation, use tools to attack targets, create graphs to track discovered systems and results of actions, and report back to the threat actor. Depending on the threat actor’s configuration and the target’s defenses, ARTEX can advance from reconnaissance to persistent, privileged access to a target organization in a matter of minutes. ARTEX does not introduce new attack techniques; it uses common, pre-existing penetration testing tools and techniques. ARTEX does sometimes transmit distinctive characteristics associated with its integrated penetration testing tools. However, threat actors using ARTEX can and do vary network indicators like source IP address or user agent string, especially to bypass static or simple signature-based detection and blocking. The most reliable and durable signal of ARTEX activity is behavioral: ARTEX sends a wide variety of requests in a brief time and retries variations on the same technique or moves on to next-stage actions at machine speed. In this blog, we’re sharing a detailed breakdown of ARTEX, including its architecture and capabilities, as well as guidance for identifying related network and system activity. Microsoft disrupts and contains threats posed by ARTEX-enabled attacks across the Microsoft Defender suite of products. Defenders can further reduce and mitigate the effectiveness of ARTEX and similar AI agentic attacks by following Microsoft’s recommendations outlined in this report to reduce internet-facing exposure, secure credentials and secrets, and limit lateral movement paths in their environment. Additionally, Microsoft recommends customers to enable automatic attack disruption in Microsoft Defender XDR to contain and isolate affected devices during an attack. To help defenders keep pace with machine-speed attacks, Microsoft Perception coordinates red, blue, and green team agents that find exploitable weaknesses, investigate threats, and remediate risks, with security teams approving critical actions. As threat actors increasingly adopt AI, we’re publishing this information to equip defenders with insights that can help protect systems, secrets, identities, and networks from AI-assisted attacks. ARTEX: Publicly available AI-powered offensive security framework ARTEX (“AI 自主渗透测试系统,” or AI autonomous penetration testing system) is an AI-driven penetration testing platform. A Go-based backend coordinates multiple large language model (LLM) agents that can take an attack from reconnaissance and attack surface discovery through exploitation, verification, and reporting, with little or no human involvement. ARTEX includes an embedded Next.js web interface and uses PostgreSQL for storage. ARTEX is primarily a web application penetration testing tool. Its built-in guidance, skills, and findings categories center on vulnerabilities in HTTP applications and APIs, including SQL injection, server-side template injection, XML external entity (XXE) injection, file upload flaws, authentication bypass, and insecure direct object references (IDOR). ARTEX agents also perform general network reconnaissance and credential attacks. After agents gain initial access to a system or network, they can pivot through interactive shells. By default, ARTEX does not take any measures to obfuscate its malicious activity. In July 2026, GitHub user Autumn-27 published ARTEX as open-source code with a Chinese-language interface. While the original developer removed the source code in October 2026, other developers who had forked the project continue to publish their own versions of ARTEX in many other languages. In November 2026, the China-based cybersecurity company ThreatBook (微步在线) claimed ownership of ARTEX and began offering commercial access to “ARTEX Pro”, in addition to CyberStrikeAI Enterprise Edition and other AI-powered penetration testing frameworks. AI agent architecture ARTEX uses multiple AI agents for different functions. The threat actor defines the target scope, and a goals model breaks the engagement into objectives that can be verified. A planner agent continuously reviews a knowledge graph containing discovered assets and findings, then issues short attack directions called “intents.” Worker agents claim those intents and carry them out with a real shell and offensive tools. They write new assets, facts, and confirmed vulnerabilities back to the graph. Each update wakes the planner agent, which decides what to do next. The cycle continues until the goals are met or the task reaches its time or step limit. A reporter agent then documents each confirmed finding with reproduction steps and a proof of concept. ARTEX AI agent architecture The threat actor can configure ARTEX to power its agents with virtually any large language model, including frontier models, open-weight models, or models that the threat actor has abliterated (modified to remove built-in guardrails) or otherwise manually tuned. For that reason, ARTEX activity is not necessarily constrained by any single provider’s safety controls or visible in any single provider’s telemetry. An optional pool function allows ARTEX to switch to different models when one fails for any reason (for example, quota exhausted, invalid key, rate-limited, or erroring). The function includes a configurable priority order and cooldown, so a long campaign can continue across multiple providers or keys. Capabilities ARTEX agents call tools to act on intents. Defenders can detect three types of evidence that may suggest AI-driven activity: The most durable signal of AI-driven activity is the pattern formed by the bursts of machine speed activity. ARTEX agents, sometimes working in parallel, continually adapt and retry a given tactic until they succeed or run out of time. ARTEX agents quickly and exhaustively iterate through different methods of access, exploitation, or abuse. A human threat actor takes time to move from one stage of the attack chain to the next. In contrast, after successfully compromising a system or credentials, the framework then dispatches additional agents to move on to the next stage of the activity at machine speed. While ARTEX is a popular tool, other AI-driven offensive security frameworks also generate similar patterns of behavior. Automated penetration testing tools, including the ones that ARTEX agents call, can produce a characteristically large volume of anomalous traffic from a single source in a short time. ARTEX does not obfuscate its malicious activity; a large number of these traffic bursts occurring at intervals is consistent with automated targeting. A small amount of traffic might have user agent strings, network protocol options, or other default values strongly associated with ARTEX or the tools it calls. However, threat actors and ARTEX agents frequently change these values, particularly when evading static rules that block such values. Reduce internet-facing exposure Find and fix exposed assets first by using Microsoft Defender External Attack Surface Management discovery to inventory the internet-facing hosts, websites, and services you own, including ones you didn’t know about. Then prioritize fixes with Microsoft Defender Vulnerability Management , which shows internet-exposed resources that lead to sensitive data or privileged identities. ARTEX agents start with port scans and web path enumeration and act on whatever they find. Block and slow down attacks on web applications and APIs. After tuning in detection mode, switch Web Application Firewall on Application Gateway to prevention mode so that SQL injection and other common web attacks are blocked, not only logged. Add rate limiting on Application Gateway or rate limiting on Azure Front Door to cap how many requests one client can send in a short time, and turn on bot protection to block or log requests from known malicious IP addresses. For APIs in Azure API Management, follow Mitigate OWASP API security top 10 threats to enforce authentication, object-level authorization, and rate limits. ARTEX agents send hundreds of requests to learn which paths a server allows and harvest API documentation to find internal routes, then test them for injection, authentication bypass, and access-control flaws. Map and test attack paths Map what an exposed asset or stolen credential could reach before an agent does. Use attack paths in Microsoft Security Exposure Management and attack path analysis in Defender for Cloud to find defensive choke points, where paths to critical assets converge, and explore the blast radius of each one. Classify your critical assets so the paths are complete, and query the enterprise exposure graph in advanced hunting to explore the same connections. During an incident, blast radius analysis in the incident graph shows the paths an attacker could take from a compromised entity to a critical asset. ARTEX agents record the assets and vulnerabilities they discover in a graph , so mapping your own graph first shows where they would go next. Test the paths that matter most, within Microsoft’s rules. Microsoft recommends regular red team operations to complement vulnerability scanning. Agree on the scope and constraints with resource owners and fix all critical findings. Protect web servers Run endpoint protection on every internet-facing server and keep it blocking. Use Defender for Endpoint on Linux and the Defender for Servers plan so that the endpoint detections and hunting queries in this report cover the servers that ARTEX agents exploit, including Linux servers. Secure accounts and sign-ins Apply Conditional Access to every user and workload identity. Create policies that require multifactor authentication (MFA) for all users , enforce phishing-resistant MFA for administrators , and block legacy authentication . Consider wider adoption of phishing-resistant passwordless authentication deployment to all users. Protect application secrets and privileges Keep application secrets out of code and replace any that leak. Use application management policies to limit which kinds of secrets and certificates apps can use and to enforce rotation, and move workloads to managed identities where possible. Turn on secret scanning and push protection for Azure DevOps repositories and secrets scanning in Defender for Cloud for virtual machines and cloud deployments and invalidate and replace any secret found in code or configuration files, including .env files. In one case that Microsoft observed, an AI agent went from searching GitHub for client secrets to taking data from Azure Key Vault in five minutes. Govern app registration, consent, and privileged roles by restricting app registration to the users who need it, configuring user consent so that users can’t grant broad permissions to unverified apps, and using app governance in Microsoft Defender for Cloud Apps to see which apps can reach your data. Use Privileged Identity Management for time-bound role activation, and monitor application credential changes and role assignments as described in the Microsoft Entra security operations guide for applications . Protect cloud resources and data Limit what a stolen credential or token can reach. Follow best practices for Azure RBAC to limit each service principal to the resources and operations its application needs, and grant managed identities only the access they need so that a token taken from the Azure Instance Metadata Service through an XXE or SSRF flaw can’t reach other resources. ARTEX agents map the permissions of the credentials they capture and have used an XXE flaw to query a cloud metadata service for role credentials, and in one investigation AI agents used a compromised OAuth application to run scripts on Azure virtual machines. Harden and monitor the services that hold secrets and data. Follow Secure your Azure Key Vault ; for storage accounts, prevent Shared Key authorization and remediate anonymous read access to blob data ; disable basic authentication for App Service publishing; and disable local authentication so that Foundry Tools accept only Microsoft Entra ID authentication. Then turn on Defender for Key Vault , Defender for Storage , Defender for Resource Manager , Defender for App Service , Defender for open-source relational databases , and AI threat protection for the workloads you run. Limit lateral movement and contain attacks automatically Detect credential theft on domain controllers. Install Microsoft Defender for Identity sensors on all domain controllers. Its alerts, listed in the Detections section, cover the Active Directory credential theft and reconnaissance that ARTEX agents run in compromised domains. Remove reusable passwords and standing access. Use group managed service accounts , whose passwords Windows manages automatically, so that service accounts don’t rely on passwords that people choose and rarely change. Use Windows LAPS to rotate each device’s local administrator password so that one captured password doesn’t work across many devices and use just-in-time machine access in Defender for Cloud to keep management ports such as RDP and SSH closed until they’re needed. ARTEX agents reuse captured credentials to move laterally over SSH and WinRM. Enable automatic attack disruption to contain compromised assets that an attacker is using and limit lateral movement early, as well as automated investigation and response , which investigates and remediates alerts automatically. We’re honored to share that Microsoft has once again been named a Leader in the 2026 Gartner® Magic Quadrant™ for Email Security. AI is changing the threat landscape and this is shifting how organizations think about their existing security strategy. In response, we’ve invested in two areas over the past year: using AI to protect people and the agents that now read their email and making protection measurable so customers can verify that it works. AI is changing both sides of the inbox The first area of investment starts with a simple reality: AI now reads email alongside people, attackers are using AI to target both, and defense must use AI to stay ahead. Picture an ordinary vendor email: a meeting recap, a few action items, and a friendly sign-off. Hidden in white text on a white background is prompt injection: an instruction telling an AI assistant to gather financial documents and send them to an outside address. The employee isn’t the target; their AI agent is. People can be persuaded by messages that exploit fear, trust, or urgency, but agents don’t need persuading. Once an agent acts on an instruction, pulling the message back won’t undo it. This is why Microsoft Defender stops malicious messages before delivery and keeps reevaluating delivered mail as new intelligence comes in. This summer, we introduced prompt injection protection for email in Defender to detect and quarantine messages carrying hidden malicious AI instructions before they reach Microsoft Copilot or other agents. In Defender, analysts can also see what an agent would see: the ‘Show concealed text’ button on the ‘Email Entity’ page reveals hidden content in the email preview, so they can inspect the instructions an agent could encounter. These attacks don’t show up often, yet. Over a four-week timeframe, we caught almost 10,000 prompt injection attempts, around 330 a day, out of the billions of emails Defender scans every day. The hard part is spotting those few without tripping over legitimate mail, so we don’t lean on a single model. Heuristics clear out the bulk first. What’s left goes to a fine-tuned small language model, and only the messages that still look suspicious reach a large language model for the final call. Saving the heaviest analysis for the smallest set of messages is what keeps false positives low and business email moving. AI is also reshaping the attacks aimed at people. The phishing emails that worry us most are the ones that look completely normal. No malicious link, no infected attachment, just a plausible request written with perfect grammar and the right context. Generative AI makes those messages cheap to produce and endlessly variable. To meet that shift, we rebuilt how Microsoft Defender makes decisions rather than simply adding another filter. That decision-making now operates at scale, screening 5.2 billion emails a day on average 1 . The redesigned detection stack combines multiple machine learning models and LLM-based classifiers, with an ensemble model weighing their outputs to determine the final verdict. It also uses natural language processing signals, including message topic, to judge what a message is asking for and not only what it contains. That helps Defender catch attacks more accurately. While detection helps with the message in front of you, defenders also need to see how it connects to the rest of an attack. Microsoft Perception extends that help beyond the message, connecting an email investigation to the broader exposure and response picture. Blue team agents investigate threats and improve detections, red team agents identify paths to compromise, and green team agents prioritize actions to close security gaps, while people retain control of critical decisions. The Integrated Security Operations Center (ISOC) in Microsoft Defender, launched in September, provides a shared foundation for people and AI agents to investigate and respond to the full attack—not just an isolated email. By bringing security information and event management (SIEM) and extended detection and response (XDR) together, it connects email signals with endpoint, identity, cloud, and third-party data, giving AI-assisted security operations broader context. Proof over promises: Making protection measurable But using AI to defend only matters if customers can verify that it works. The second investment area is one the whole industry has struggled with: how do you know your email security is actually working? Detection claims are easy to make and hard to verify, so we publish the data. Since July 2025, we’ve released quarterly email security benchmarking reports built on real-world threats, not synthetic tests. They compare environments protected by Microsoft Defender alone with those that add a secure email gateway in front of it or an integrated cloud email security (ICES) layer behind it. We believe transparency strengthens the whole market, and an industry that measures itself in the open improves faster. While benchmarking shows how protection performs across organizations, the Protection & Posture Insights report shows how it performs in yours. This on-demand, tenant-specific report helps security leaders understand which threats target their organization, how well Microsoft Defender stops them, and where configuration can be strengthened. Recent updates show URL clicks checked and malicious-site attempts blocked across email, Microsoft Teams, Office apps, and Microsoft Copilot, and also highlight allow lists and overrides that may be letting threats through. Together, benchmarking and tenant-specific insights give leaders evidence to evaluate protection and identify opportunities to strengthen it. We’re grateful to our customers, partners, and the analyst community for their trust, and to the teams turning insight into protection. Our engineers start with customer problems, while our researchers draw on more than 165 trillion signals a day across Microsoft to anticipate emerging threats and build protections before real-world examples appear 2 . This recognition from Gartner is a milestone for us, not the finish line. Our focus remains on protecting people and agents and giving customers evidence they can use to evaluate their defenses.
