There's only so much code: the rise in vulnerability numbers
Cisco Talos | Research / advisory | 2026-10-20T15:31:22+00:00
Frontier AI models (like Mythos) are rapidly mining decades of technical debt across ubiquitous legacy libraries, driving monthly Microsoft Patch Tuesday vulnerability advisories up more than tenfold (reaching 1,415 this month). This volume is overwhelming open-source maintainers and putting pressure on the traditional 90-day coordinated disclosure model. Despite a 105% year-over-year surge in CVEs, Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEVs) grew by only 37%, and only 3 of September's 975 Microsoft advisories saw in-the-wild exploitation, demonstrating that the vast majority of AI-discovered bugs are not practically exploited. We may be able to take new-bug-class vulnerability spikes as evidence that these vulnerabilities will eventually level off and decline, once the historical code backlog is remediated and AI verification is embedded directly into new development pipelines. With attackers able to parse patches faster via AI, defenders cannot cover for all possible threats. Organizations should continue to build robust defense-in-depth. Prioritize high-exploitability flaws in their specific environments, implement interim mitigations (such as LiveProtect), deploy behavioral anomaly detection, and integrate current AI evaluation models into their software build chains. The September Microsoft Tuesday release included 975 vulnerability advisories. This month, 1,415 vulnerabilities were just dropped. Compared to a pre-June average of 100 a month, that increase is staggering. Where did they come from? As of April, frontier models came onto the scene more fine-tuned than ever toward vulnerability detection, particularly with the introduction of Mythos. The deluge brings a lot of questions: If these numbers are real, why hasn’t the internet collapsed? Are these vulnerabilities a new breed, and if not, why didn’t analysts find them in code that is sometimes decades old? Is the coordinated disclosure model still relevant in an era of this kind of speed? How in the world do defenders deal with those numbers? Should we go home, kiss our spouses, and prepare for the end? The answer to many of these questions boils down to this: The majority of these vulnerabilities are coming from repositories of old code from large-profile products and systems; and disclosure does not equal weaponization. Where is it from? AI is chewing through a backlog of technical debt in libraries and tools used as the dependable, best-practice lynchpins of so many environments. These models have a new perspective, an ability to "think" without some of the hangups or assumptions of human defenders, and they are thorough in listing those vulnerabilities. Entrenched older tools have always been prime targets because of their predictability; attackers know those tools and all their secrets, and they know what is likely to be present in a network with them. Multi-exploit attacks can be deployed with little variability. Pre-Mythos threat actors exploited log4j ( CVE-2021-44228 ) to immense success. The library was ubiquitous, included in nearly every environment, but not a component that got cataloged in visibility scans. A combination of scale and low visibility meant defenders were blindsided, scrambling to find out if they even used the log4j library before starting to concoct a mitigation plan. Talos has ongoing relationships with some of the largest technical vendors (such as the Microsoft Active Protections Process ( MAPP ) agreement). Organizations share additional information—detection guidance or maybe a POC — for vulnerabilities that are most likely to be exploited, to enable Talos to provide adequate coverage when the advisories are announced. "Microsoft believes it is important for us to provide detection content, based on their guidance," as Yves Younan, Director of Talos Vulnerability Discovery, Detection, and Testing, says. Talos has needed to invest more resources to get coverage in on time for critical vendors like Microsoft to keep up with notifications coming in fast and often on staggered or out-of-band timelines. For larger vendors, the pressure is handleable for now. However, open-source projects are getting flooded. Some projects have a trusted group that can combine to tackle patches, and some share with the full community for immediate assistance, but it is up to the project. The internet runs on open-source work. The pace of discovery presents a challenge for the traditional coordinated disclosure system vulnerability hunters have been using for years, and some are calling it into question. Security vulnerabilities should be reported, to enable an informed defender base and regular patching, and vendors responsible for vulnerable code need time to create coverage. A timed disclosure model where notifications are sent to a vendor, who then has a set period of time (typically 90 days) to provide coverage before the disclosure is released publicly, has created a more secure online environment for decades. Now, many practitioners are releasing advisories publicly without a disclosure period beforehand. The reasoning is that if one AI can find a weakness, any other could, so the vulnerability is already, effectively, released. This conversation is actively changing expectations around vulnerability disclosure, and guidance is in flux. Will it stay this high? No one knows the future, but we can always look to the past for insight. Numbers of vulnerabilities jump drastically when a new bug class is identified, as their possibilities are explored to the fullest. Then the discoveries plateau and drop off. When format string vulnerabilities were discovered as a new bug class in 2000, the initial influx of discoveries ramped up and up, until reaching a peak in 2005 and then slowly declining over the next couple of years. Format string vulnerabilities are still discovered, but they are a smaller overall fraction of the number of CVEs discovered in a year. Theoretically, AI-assisted vulnerability discovery could go the same way. While frontier models have not discovered a new vulnerability class, they are more efficient at discovering known types of software vulnerabilities. Given that a lot of vital software has a long history of code releases, there’s plenty of code to mine for weaknesses. But there’s only so much code… right? Old and commonly used tools are often under the highest scrutiny, lending them one kind of safety: Because of their popularity, utility, and stability, when popular tools have vulnerabilities, they are discovered fast and patched promptly. As the historical backlog of essential systems is turned over by AI, vulnerabilities will be found and patched. New code will ideally be run through AI bug-checkers that will prevent new vulnerabilities from being introduced. The rate of new vulns should level off and drop accordingly. There are some caveats. In the past, as vulnerability disclosures increased through coordinated disclosures, we saw increased adoption of mitigations. Exploitation became significantly harder. Attackers often need to chain together 3 or more vulnerabilities to successfully exploit a system. However, more awareness of vulnerabilities often leads to more exploitation. Attackers often use vendor patches meant to protect customers to discover where vulnerabilities might exist. The frequency of patching has delayed human attackers via parsing bottlenecks, but AI can eliminate that problem. Are the numbers the real issue? The volume is certainly up. Fresh as this blog is being finished, the October Patch Tuesday dropped almost 500 more than last month. With refinement, any vulnerability could become a link to a path of effect. But... not all vulnerabilities are exploitable. Some are wildly impractical for attacker use. In fact, the percent of Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEVs) is vastly smaller than the found vulnerabilities. Of the 975 vulnerabilities Microsoft reported in September, there were only three in-the-wild exploits detected. When viewed by percentage, vulnerabilities versus KEVs tell a different and perhaps reassuring story. Compared with the same period last year, additions to the known-exploited vulnerability list increased by 37%, while published CVEs increased by 105%. However, we wouldn't necessarily expect those figures to grow in proportion. Adversaries only need enough useful vulnerabilities to compromise their targets, and the same vulnerabilities can be reused against many organizations. The KEV count tracks distinct vulnerabilities known to be exploited, not the number of attacks or successful compromises. AI helps ingest code, advisory reports, and attack surfaces with inhuman speed, but it cannot currently make judgment calls about the value of a weakness. For defenders, the question remains the same: Which vulnerabilities provide a viable path into their environment, and how do you close those paths? What do we do now? The undeniable danger here is in exploitation time going down rapidly, while the vulnerabilities that could become exploits is still going up. The biggest hurdle for a defender is determining what is a real vulnerability for their environment, and what is not. Different defenders have different forms of control depending on their role. For those who are writing code, the new standard validation chain should include AI evaluation by models at least as up to date as those being used to hunt for vulnerabilities. It is relatively cheap to run new code through AI, but very expensive to run a cumbersome backlog of old code through. Bite it off in chunks. For those whose role involves keeping the tools ready: Upgrade ASAP. Don't stop there. For Cisco customers, implement LiveProtect to mitigate vulnerabilities between advisory release day and patching, to give time for that patching. Do this for everything you own. Move beyond static domain blocking and implement behavioral detection that alerts on anomalous traffic patterns directed toward automation platforms. Hone your focus to the vulnerabilities with the highest exploitability in your environment. KEVs are far less common among the sea of advisories, and their numbers have not risen proportionally with the AI-assisted deluge. Focus on building defense-in-depth. Employ multiple protection systems, to cover for delays in patching. Implement interim mitigations (such as LiveProtect), create custom IPS/Snort triggers to block pre-patched traffic using SnortML, deploy behavioral anomaly detection, and integrate current AI evaluation models in software build chains. Dive deeper into this conversation with the latest TTP interview with Yves Younan and vulnerability researcher Bella Fusari. Operation FlashHole: Lazarus breaks security, defies control
Securelist | Research / advisory | 2026-10-21T02:07:29+00:00
In January, we responded to the Lazarus Group’s novel attack campaign against South Korea, which followed last year’s Operation SyncHole . In this new operation, the group employed an updated malware chain that included both their old and new malware, which we disclosed in full, and used another mandatory South Korean security software program as a part of infection chain using a watering‑hole strategy. Notably, the group targeted victims more selectively, sending spear‑phishing emails with links to fabricated but relevant sites, such as portfolio and résumé pages tailored to each target. We dubbed this activity Operation FlashHole. Operation FlashHole attack flow during initial compromise Our examination reveals that the earliest evidence of this new operation dates back to July 2025. This suggests the group began preparing for the campaign shortly after ending Operation SyncHole, which lasted at least four months. We identified a minimum of three organizations impacted by the campaign in South Korea’s software, construction, and wired communication equipment manufacturing sectors. These sectors differ from those affected by Operation SyncHole, indicating that the group has expanded its target scope over time. Background After more than a year since the first observation of Operation SyncHole, we detected a suspicious execution chain originating from legitimate South Korean software. This chain mirrors the one from the previous operation; however, this time, the attacker abused CrossWeb EX instead of Cross EX, which was abused in Operation SyncHole. Both applications appear to be maintained by the same developer, and both are related to financial security. CrossWeb EX is a financial security solution that is required to access widely used online banking and government websites. It encrypts communications between the user’s browser and the web server using public key infrastructure (PKI). This is not the first time Lazarus has abused this software. It was also used in 2021 to deploy the Racket downloader and again in 2022 and 2023 . Although the exact method by which CrossWeb EX was abused remains unclear, we determined that the attack chain ultimately leads to the execution of the malware in memory. We noted that the spawned process was running with a high integrity level. The parent process, INISAFECrossWebEXSvc.exe , originates from the installation bundle of INISAFE CrossWeb EX V3 version 3.4.1.2, released in 2023 after KrCERT’s public advisory . As we already mentioned in our SyncHole blog post, this pattern closely mirrors the long‑standing strategy employed by the Lazarus Group to target South Korea. Since CrossWeb EX and similar software is mandatory for South Korean users and typically runs in the background to interact with the browser, it has been leveraged as a critical attack vector by the group. In 2025, a paper was presented at the USENIX Security Symposium that investigated vulnerabilities in South Korea’s mandatory security software and demonstrated how such software can unintentionally create a massive attack surface. In July 2026, the South Korean government and several local security vendors also published a joint security advisory covering typical Lazarus attack techniques, including exploitation of financial security software. While this advisory overlaps with our findings from Operation FlashHole and Operation SyncHole, and AhnLab’s detailed analysis report Operation Double Barrel covers a wide range of recent Lazarus Group activities, we are conducting a more granular classification based on their TTPs. We will discuss this further in the attribution section. Initial infection vector Although we could not precisely determine what triggered the infection chain, our analysis uncovered a suspicious website hosted on GitHub Pages that is associated with the GitHub account used as the C2 channel. The GitHub account was created last September, and the initial version of the site was designed to showcase the résumé of a senior network appliance developer. In January, however, this site was replaced with another featuring an interior design showcase. The first site was likely accessed by an employee of a wired communication manufacturing company, while the second site likely affected a target in the construction sector. Appearance of the malicious websites The first site with the résumé had a photograph supposedly of a job seeker, but the lack of EXIF metadata and the unnatural texture of the image indicate that it was generated by AI. This suggests the Lazarus Group has incorporated AI into its long‑standing strategy, like other Korean‑speaking threat actors ( 1 , 2 ). The group most likely also used AI to create the lure site: the job seeker’s name resembles the Korean version of “John Doe,” and the portfolio was populated with seemingly meaningless details and random companies. The generated site was then manually tailored to specific targets. Suspected use of an AI-generated image on the résumé site The websites contained embedded links that loaded an external JavaScript file via a script tag. Malicious link embedded in the résumé site: hxxps://javadevhouse.co[.]kr/ajax/libs/jquery/1.7.3/jquery.min.js Malicious link embedded in interior design site: hxxps://pythonapischool[.]com/api/detach Based on the analysis, we assess with medium-to-high confidence that the targets received phishing emails containing links to the malicious site hosted on GitHub Pages (using the domain <username>.github.io). The research results showed a high possibility that visiting the malicious site initiated an infection process involving CrossWeb EX software. This assessment aligns with Lazarus’s recent approach, as detailed in the joint advisory published by the South Korean government and security companies. Full infection chain Following the initial compromise, the legitimate Windows binary fsquirt.exe was executed under the CrossWeb EX process and injected with shellcode containing a malicious DLL – TechHub malware. From our extensive and thorough examination, we identified several malicious payloads and reconstructed the complete infection chain. The infection chain is rather complex and reveals the Lazarus Group’s advanced operational techniques on targeted hosts. These techniques conceal their malicious payloads, intent and their sophisticated malware delivery framework. The latter was updated extensively compared to the previous campaign, despite the new operation starting less than a year later. Significantly, the execution chain below exists only in memory during the initial infiltration, highlighting the attackers’ considerable efforts to hide the initial compromise. Initial infection chain Following the initial compromise, the group sought to maintain persistence on the network by installing a pair of malware strains – TouchHub and TouchShift – leveraging DLL side‑loading under the name mscoree.dll . TouchHub was deployed by the first instance of TechHub. It then used DLL side-loading to launch TouchShift, which led to the in‑memory execution of another TechHub instance. After launching the second time, TechHub deployed another pair, ClientHub and LPEClient, rather than executing Cubridge directly. Only minimal traces of these files were found on disk; the remainder of the malicious code persisted exclusively in memory. Expanded infection chain after gaining persistence Since Cubridge remains only in memory at the initial compromise stage, we suspect the group designed this indirect, multistage chain to deliberately redeploy Cubridge after gaining persistence, as it serves as the most sophisticated orchestrator of the attack. Malware set The full chain comprises advanced variants of the known Lazarus malware strains, including TechHub, Cubridge, TouchShift, LPEClient, and the newly identified TouchHub and ClientHub. TechHub – the start of the chain TechHub, a downloader that plays a central role in this attack, communicates with GitHub using a Personal Access Token (PAT). It serves as the initial payload, providing the top of the chain with sufficient functionality to profile compromised hosts and facilitate subsequent payloads. This variant is a more advanced version of the malware named CLOUDBURST by Mandiant and NickelLoader by ESET. According to their reports, the earlier version communicated with a C2 server via HTTP(S) to fetch additional payloads. The latest iteration we found, however, communicates with GitHub instead and has updated command strings. We identified three variants of TechHub in the chain: an in‑memory version, a simple DLL side‑loading version, and a DLL side‑loading version that uses a registered Windows service. Depending on which stage loads the malware, it contacts a private GitHub repository via the GitHub API using either hard‑coded credentials or credentials retrieved from its configuration file. When TechHub loads, it creates a 4‑byte hexadecimal identifier (ID) in the range 0x1000–0xFFFF or reads one from the configuration file, enabling the attackers to distinguish between compromised hosts. TechHub then checks a GitHub issue by sending the request GET /issues?state=[open/closed] to determine whether any previous communication remains active and removes any traces of it if found. It then proceeds with the handshaking process. It creates a new issue titled 1[ID] with the body “hel” using the POST /issues request, and sets its status to open. It then retrieves a file from the repository containing the 2[ID] value in its name. This file contains the RSA public key. It generates a random 16‑byte XOR key, encrypts it with the provided RSA public key, and uploads the result, prefixed with 1[ID] , to the repository. The hard‑coded IV is XORed with the generated 16‑byte key and then used after the handshake. During file exchange, filenames follow the pattern [1|2][ID][random-16-byte-key] throughout communication. The “2” prefix indicates delivery from the repository to TechHub, while “1” indicates delivery from TechHub to the repository. The random 16‑byte key in the filename is used for AES encryption or decryption of each file’s contents, employing either a hard‑coded IV or an updated IV. After the key exchange is complete, TechHub reads a file with the prefix “2” once again and verifies that the delivered data equals “hel”. ClientHub We also identified a new piece of malware stored in TechHub’s private repository, which we named ClientHub. It supports various commands formed using a bitmask. There are at least two versions of ClientHub: one that communicates with websites via HTTP(S) and one that interacts with the Dropbox API using credentials. Depending on the version, its configuration is sourced from internal resources or the external configuration file C:\Program Files\Common Files\System\msadc\msadc.inc. The configuration comprises keys and IVs for the SNOW 1.0 algorithm, scheduled execution times, and C2 server addresses encrypted with the SNOW stream cipher. The SNOW cipher is a family of high‑speed stream ciphers used for secure data encryption in telecommunications and wireless networks. This is the first time it has been observed in the malware of the Lazarus Group. To establish C2 communication, the malware initially generates a 32‑byte random array. It subsequently overwrites portions of this array with data collected from the compromised device. The resulting 32‑byte array comprises a blend of random bytes and relevant information. This array is later used as the key for the SNOW algorithm to decrypt data received from the C2 server. Finally, the key array is encrypted with a hard‑coded RSA public key and encoded in Base64. LPEClient LPEClient is the only malware that was also observed in Operation SyncHole, indicating that the actor relies heavily on it for sophisticated victim profiling and payload delivery across multiple operations. LPEClient first appeared in 2019, and was discovered in 2020 . Since then, the tool has been widely observed in the Dream Job and Cascade Hole campaigns that targeted South Korea. Recent research conducted by Check Point revealed that LPEClient was used to deploy the FudModule malware using a zero-day. LPEClient has evolved continuously over time, but we discovered that it recently underwent a major operational revamp. It no longer contains the C2 server address itself; instead, it interacts with ClientHub via a shared buffer. We believe this change is intended to enhance concealment. Timeline of LPEClient The previous version collected the computer name, memory status, CPU specifications, operating system details, version of the ntoskrnl.exe file, firmware table, antivirus name, domain name, user name, the integrity level of the current process, and a random byte sequence of 1,568 bytes. The newly updated LPEClient variant mostly collects the same information; however, it also introduces several changes. It does not collect the version of the ntoskrnl.exe file by querying the FileVersion value. Instead, depending on the specific build, it reads either the HKLM\SOFTWARE\Microsoft\Windows NT\CurrentVersion\UBR registry key or the SYSTEM_BUILD_VERSION_INFORMATION structure to retrieve the revision portion of the Windows build number. These values can be used to infer the system’s cumulative update or patch level. To collect information about installed antivirus products, it switched from querying a WQL command to reading the WMI repository file directly. It now reads C:\Windows\System32\wbem\Repository\OBJECTS.DATA and searches for keywords such as AntiVirusProduct, companyName, and displayName to identify the installed products. It also collects low‑level Windows security mitigation states. To achieve this, it queries the SYSTEM_KERNEL_VA_SHADOW_INFORMATION structure for the Kernel VA Shadow mitigation status and the SYSTEM_SHADOW_STACK_INFORMATION structure for the Shadow Stack (CET) protection status. Additionally, it generates a 32‑byte random unique identifier by using the HighwayHash algorithm and an XOR operation with the current system time. The collected information, including the generated identifier, is compressed with the XPRESS Huffman algorithm using the maximum compression setting. The compressed data is then concatenated with the message ID 0xF07D6B34 and encrypted using GOST (also known as Magma), Russia’s national cryptographic standard that employs a DES‑like structure, before being sent to the ClientHub via a shared buffer. Initially, KrCERT described the algorithm as a custom DES‑like scheme. However, further analysis by us and Check Point confirmed that it is actually the GOST algorithm. The key is randomly generated by a proprietary key‑generation algorithm and prepended to the encrypted message. The next request handles the key exchange by delivering the message ID 0xA39CF278 together with the generated unique identifier. The response is decrypted using the AES‑CBC‑256 algorithm. The SHA‑256 hash of the previously generated 1,568‑byte random value, which was originally sent to the C2 server during the collection phase, serves as the key for this decryption. Through this process, LPEClient obtains a byte array comprising four 1,568‑byte blocks from the C2 response. Each block contains an ML‑KEM‑1024 public key, where ML‑KEM is a NIST‑standardized post‑quantum key encapsulation mechanism derived from CRYSTALS‑Kyber. LPEClient then generates four distinct pairs of shared keys and ciphertexts from these blocks, encrypts the ciphertexts with the GOST algorithm, and transmits them to the C2 server via ClientHub together with the message identifier 0xEF8C0D51 and the associated identifier. Now that the preparation phase for receiving payloads is complete, the malware is ready to receive payloads forwarded by ClientHub from the C2 server, along with the necessary parameters. The use of post-quantum cryptography shows that the group intends to hide subsequent payloads. The response to the 0xEF8C0D51 message is decrypted with the Threefish algorithm using a 256-byte key, composed of the four distinct shared keys. Threefish is a tweakable block cipher that accepts a ‘tweak’ value alongside the key and the message. The concept of a tweak is similar to that of a nonce or initialization vector, which are used in different types of ciphers. In LPEClient communication, the tweak for the decryption is derived by XORing the unique identifier with 165 (0xA5) . The first four bytes of the decrypted message must specify the parameter’s length, followed by the parameter itself and then the payload. The payload must be larger than 4 KB and start with the characters 'MZ' , which indicates that the decrypted payload is an executable file. The payload is then reflectively loaded into memory. After successful loading, LPEClient sends the message ID 0xCB790A25 to the C2 server. Before loading the acquired payload, LPEClient takes measures to bypass security products. Instead of invoking VirtualProtect directly, the malware uses the identified HeapPageAllocator::ProtectPages in jscript9.dll to modify the memory page protection of the MD4Update function. The beginning of the function is then overwritten with a hard‑coded opcode sequence that serves as a syscall stub with the required arguments. LPEClient then uses the syscall stub to disable a user‑mode syscall instrumentation hook. We identified the final payload that was ultimately loaded as Cubridge. Overlaps between the recent attacks by Lazarus Group Conclusion Our analysis shows that the Lazarus Group begins preparing for its next operation shortly after completing the previous one, ultimately deploying an enhanced malware chain by refining existing tools. The journey of a paper unicorn: adventures inside a modern warehouse
Securelist | Research / advisory | 2026-10-20T07:00:45+00:00
Introduction Imagine you’ve ordered a small paper unicorn from an online marketplace. You clicked “Buy”, received a cheerful notification, and expect a courier to bring the box tomorrow. If the unicorn is delayed by a day, it will be annoying but not catastrophic: an apology, a discount promo code — and the incident is forgotten. Now let’s multiply one late unicorn by several hundred thousand orders. Packages start heading to the wrong cities. The website shows an item is in stock, even though the required bin is empty. Conveyors stop, boxes get stuck between floors, the cargo piles up with no freight manifest. Trucks line up at the gates, and every minute of downtime turns into blown schedules, support tickets, fines, and lost sales. It is at exactly this scale that a cyberincident in a warehouse becomes a problem for the entire business. A modern warehouse is no longer just a big building with shelving. It is a tightly interconnected hybrid system: web applications, radio channels, and an automated factory. Physical processes here depend on data, and errors in data have very physical consequences. Examples include an outage that affected warehouse management systems at the grocery giant Morrisons , and the 2025 Form 10-K annual report of United Natural Foods, Inc. (UNFI), which confirmed unauthorized activity on information technology systems that impacted order fulfillment. More recently, reports emerged that a cyberattack disrupted eight CEVA Logistics warehouses across Europe and exposed customer delivery data. This paper follows a single order through a warehouse to show where the operation is vulnerable, the stages it traverses, the systems that drive it, and the points where an attacker can reach them. We then set out the threat model: what is at risk, who is in a position to take it, and how each risk lands on the business. From there, we look at how an attacker gets in, and what reaching the warehouse management system costs in practical terms: orders unshipped, inventory records nobody can trust, and an operation that stops. We close with recommendations on where the controls that matter actually sit. Tracing the unicorn A modern warehouse is a sequence of stages involving many machines and people, all working together to keep the business process stable so that, in the end, your order gets delivered. This process consists of eight main stages, shown in the figure below. Stages of order fulfillment So let’s assume we’ve ordered our paper unicorn and are waiting for it at home. Let’s follow our order through all the main stages, from its arrival at the warehouse to the final stop on its journey. Unloading First, the unicorn arrives in the unloading zone, packed in a big truck. The driver is assigned a gate, for example, via a tablet with kiosk mode enabled, or an outdoor display board, indicating where the truck should park and where the cargo will be unloaded. Even at this very first step, a digital command (the gate number on the screen) is already triggering a physical action. Therefore, incorrectly assigned gates can create a queue, delay unloading, or send goods into the wrong workflow. Receiving At this stage, employees identify the cargo. They scan a barcode using a portable data terminal (PDT) and the central system receives a message indicating that this object arrives at the warehouse. From this moment, the unicorn exists in two forms at once: as a real box and as a record in the information system. If these two versions diverge, the error is inherited by all subsequent processes. Putaway Next, the goods are moved into internal storage. Two new devices come into play here. First, a thermal printer, connected to the internal network over Wi-Fi, creates an internal label for each item. Then an employee, forklift, or automated system moves the goods to the assigned bin, often with the help of lifts controlled by PLCs. Both of these devices can be valuable attack entry points: one can let an adversary pivot into the internal network, while the other can give them control over an electric motor. Storage Then the unicorn goes into storage. Many distribution centers use multi-level mezzanine structures: several floors of metal shelving inside a huge space. They allow efficient use of the building’s height, but at the same time complicate control over the technical infrastructure. Wi-Fi access points, cameras, switches, cables, and sensors may be mounted high overhead or buried deep inside the rows. A forgotten Ethernet cable or misconfigured networked device (access point or switch) left behind in a dark corner can become a convenient initial access point into critical infrastructure. Picking When the customer places an order, picking begins if the goods were already stored in the warehouse or as soon as they are unloaded. A PDT, workstation, light indicator, or voice terminal directs the employee to the required bin where they pick the goods and move them to the next stage. These devices are themselves potential attack entry points: compromising a workstation or PDT could allow an attacker to manipulate picking information, which could misdirect employees to the wrong bin. Here, both the speed and integrity of the information are critical, and any manipulation or disruption can lead to wrong orders, delays, or problems in subsequent stages. Sorting At the sorting stage, the warehouse starts to look more like a factory. Boxes move through conveyor belts and pass through different scanners, diverters, and chutes. The sorting plans are usually stored and managed by dedicated servers, while PLCs are responsible for controlling the machinery and executing these plans. For example, when the warehouse management system (WMS) decides that our unicorn should take route A, the control system needs to activate the right diverter at the right moment, sometimes within a fraction of a second. Packing During packing, an employee or a robotic arm places the goods into the final box, prints the shipping label, and sometimes measures the dimensions and weight of the package. Safety mechanisms usually prevent robotic arms from leaving the controlled zone and harming nearby operators, but their compromise might inflict physical damage on the packages being processed. Shipment Finally, at the shipping stage, the parcel heads to the gates. Automated guided vehicles (AGVs) and autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) move containers and pallets across the warehouse, while the dock management system coordinates the truck that will carry the unicorn onward to a store, a sorting center, or straight to the customer. These robots are themselves a potential target for attackers. Attacks can be conducted against AGVs and AMRs to interfere with their operation, for example by affecting their navigation or preventing them from reaching their intended destination. All eight stages are coordinated by the WMS. It looks like a large web application, but in its role it’s closer to the operating system of the entire warehouse: it knows which goods exist, where they’re located, who or what should move them, and where they go next, linking employees, inventory levels, storage addresses, tasks, and orders. It also exchanges data externally: with ERP systems, transport platforms, marketplaces, dark stores, and external carriers. Surrounding the WMS is an even larger digital ecosystem: identity services, databases, APIs, printers, carrier systems, technicians’ laptops, video surveillance, access control, fire and gas alarms, power supply, and a building management system (BMS). That is why a single “warehouse network” doesn’t really exist. As we’ve seen, there are many overlapping technologies — IT infrastructure, OT systems, radio, cloud services, building security, and automation — layered on top of one another. A lone vulnerability scanner will never tell you whether a warehouse is secure. Hunting the unicorn Securing an environment such as a warehouse requires a comprehensive security assessment covering all of its interconnected systems. This involves identifying vulnerabilities across each system and understanding how weaknesses can be combined into attack paths that a real attacker could exploit. But a vulnerability only matters in proportion to what it exposes and who is able to reach it, so before looking at the weaknesses themselves, we need to understand what an attacker in this environment is actually after, because in a warehouse, the blast radius extends well beyond data. Everything we just traced — the systems, the devices, and the people operating them — exists to keep goods moving, and attacks in various parts along that chain can affect the business differently. The chart below shows what is at risk in the warehouse and where those risks land for the business. The segment on the left groups them under the three core security properties: integrity, availability, and confidentiality. The segment on the right maps each property to the business outcomes a failure can cause. Warehouse risks The first area at risk is integrity. Inventory counts, locations, orders, and shipment data must stay in sync with the physical world. Corrupting a record silently desynchronizes the operation, and because the eight stages form a dependency chain, one falsified entry early on is inherited downstream, surfacing later as stockouts and mis-shipments. In its deliberate forms, this lets an attacker redirect goods, release stock, and perform other actions on behalf of the platform’s vendors under cover of legitimate records. Integrity is also a safety concern: when people or machines act on information that no longer reflects the physical environment, a data failure can become a physical incident. This is why integrity is the only property linked to all five outcomes, including harm to people. The second is availability, the warehouse’s ability to keep goods moving. Halting a conveyor, a sorter, label printing, or the WMS stops orders from shipping, and in a business built on speed, every hour of downtime means delayed deliveries and broken promises. Ransomware can bring the whole warehouse to a standstill. The impact shows up as operational disruption, lost revenue, and reputational damage. The third is confidentiality. Customer data, business records, and system credentials are valuable targets. Their exposure leads to legal liability and reputational damage, and stolen credentials often open the door to further attacks. Where the WMS connects to financial or ERP processes, the risk extends to theft of funds. Knowing what is at risk tells us what an attacker stands to gain; the next question is who is in a position to take it. Threat actors differ in the access they already hold. For example, an attacker may be authenticated and privileged, have limited physical or remote access, or have no legitimate access to the environment at all. Based on their relationship with the warehouse and their level of access, threat actors can generally be divided into two categories: External. An attacker with no legitimate relationship with the warehouse or the organization, operating without authorized access. External attackers can be divided into two categories based on their physical proximity to the environment: Remote. An attacker operating from outside the site’s physical area, reaching the environment through whatever the organization exposes or makes reachable over the internet, including web services, remote access infrastructure, and third-party connections used by suppliers, carriers, and integrators. Proximate. An attacker with no legitimate access who has nevertheless moved within physical range of the site’s locally reachable technologies. They may, for example, be in a parking lot, a neighboring unit, or a delivery bay. Their proximity may allow them to interact with wireless, radio, Bluetooth, or other short-range interfaces without possessing an account, badge, employment relationship, or other authorized access. Internal. An individual with legitimate access to the warehouse, its systems, or its information, who intentionally or unintentionally creates a security risk. This includes employees, administrators, operators, contractors, and temporary staff. Combining these threat actors and their access levels with the risks identified earlier, the vulnerabilities present in each system, the resulting attack surfaces, and the potential attack paths between them provides a threat model for the warehouse environment. This allows an organization to understand how different systems and people could be targeted, how individual weaknesses could be combined, and where security controls are most needed. Cornering the unicorn Knowing what is at risk and who might come after it still leaves the most practical question open: how they would actually get in, and how far they could go once they did. The diagram below sets out the warehouse’s attack surface: every point where an attacker can get in, organized by the access that reaching each point requires. Warehouse attack surface On the left are the attacker categories discussed above, distinguished by where they stand and what access they already hold. Each reaches the environment through a different set of entry points, and the impact of a successful attack can land on several systems, from the WMS to OT systems such as robots, sorters, and physical access control system (PACS). The WMS is highlighted because it is the most significant of these: it coordinates the OT systems, so compromising it can affect the entire operation rather than a single process. Next, we will cover the initial access vectors, the points a threat actor can use to reach the warehouse environment. Following that, we turn to the impact on the logistics processes themselves, focusing on the WMS, since its compromise affects every logistics operation that depends on it. Initial attack vectors Internet-facing services Remote access through internet-facing services, third-party connections, and corporate networks gives an external attacker a foothold on the warehouse network infrastructure. It is a well-covered problem and not specific to this environment, so we note it here as an entry point but will not examine it further. Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and other radio channels Wi-Fi connects handhelds, tablets, printers, cameras, and workstations. Bluetooth serves scanners, headsets, and positioning beacons. Zigbee and similar low-power protocols run lighting, sensors, and building automation, while LoRaWAN carries telemetry over long distances. Cellular provides a backup channel for kiosks and contractor equipment, and proprietary protocols control industrial panels and specialized machinery. Radio passes through windows, gates, roofing, and thin external panels, so a network built for receiving and processing goods may be reachable from the parking lot and, with a directional antenna, from considerably farther away. That reach matters in two ways. It gives an external attacker a place to start, as proximity alone is enough to reach the Wi-Fi and the low-power protocols, and from there to gain an initial foothold in the warehouse networks. However, radio does not only lead into the network. It also connects directly to the field devices and carries the live control traffic of warehouse operations, which makes availability a first-order concern. If hundreds of handhelds lose their connection at once, picking stops. If an AGV loses its control channel, the safe reaction may be to halt in place, and a stopped machine can block an entire route. Physical network The scale of the warehouse itself is part of the attack surface. A fulfillment warehouse can run up to tens of thousands of square meters, with racking climbing several stories and walkways threading through it. Structured cabling runs along that racking, cameras and access points are mounted on that, and wall jacks sit behind pallets that have not moved in months. Almost none of it is in anyone’s line of sight. That gives a person in the warehouse something a normal office does not: unsupervised access to live network infrastructure. Unplugging a ceiling camera leaves an active port. An unused jack behind a shelf leaves another. Neither requires tools, credentials, or any interaction with a system that logs anything. And since the camera or access point sits on the same segment as the rest of the site, the compromised port, not being isolated, opens a path to the network core. Barcode scanners Barcode scanners are everywhere in a warehouse. They are the main tool workers use to tell the system what is moving and where, supporting receiving, picking, packing, and inventory management. A typical setup has handheld scanners communicating with a base station, which is in turn connected to a workstation or another business system. This makes the scanner an entry point into the core network in two ways. First, through the barcode itself. Since the scanner acts as a keyboard to the connected host, a maliciously crafted barcode or QR code can inject keystrokes when scanned, potentially giving the attacker an initial foothold on the host without directly accessing the network. Second, through the scanner’s wireless link. During a customer engagement, with the provided test stand of such a device, we discovered that an attacker within range could inject keystrokes into the connected host and potentially achieve remote code execution without user interaction. This attack pattern was observed across multiple vendors, including, for example, Honeywell devices affected by CVE-2026-4272 . In both cases, the result is the same: a foothold on a host inside the warehouse network. The scanner is simply the entry point; the significance of that foothold comes from the host’s trusted position within the operational environment and its connectivity to core systems. Portable data terminals Portable data terminals (PDTs) are essentially ordinary Android devices with an embedded barcode scanner, carried by workers to scan items; each scan is passed to a dedicated application that forwards it to a remote server over an API. To keep workers on task, the devices usually run in kiosk mode, locking the user to that single application and preventing interaction with the rest of the Android system. That lockdown is exactly what an attacker wants to break, and breaking it is what turns the terminal into an initial access vector: escaping the kiosk can expose the Wi-Fi password of the network the terminal is connected to, or allow malicious software to be installed that gives the attacker remote access to the network over the internet. Printers Warehouses contain different types of printers. Alongside the usual IT printers used for documents and paperwork, there are specialized thermal printers, small devices that use heated elements on the printhead to create labels, barcodes, and tags. As we mentioned earlier, these are mainly used to print the labels, usually containing barcodes, that are attached to goods during the putaway stage. Here we focus on mobile thermal printers: small, battery-powered devices that workers can carry or wear. They typically connect to the internal network over Wi-Fi to receive print commands, while also supporting Bluetooth for connecting to nearby devices. This combination makes them a potential entry point into the internal network. Some models, such as the Zebra ZQ630, support multiple interfaces and can run applications directly on the device. During a security assessment, we discovered that apps can be uploaded and executed over Bluetooth. By exploiting a pairing misconfiguration, an attacker within Bluetooth range could bypass pairing, upload malicious software, and gain an initial foothold through the printer. Impact on logistics processes (attacks on WMS) With a foothold in the network, an attacker can pivot to the logistics processes themselves: from the WMS through the warehouse infrastructure to the PLCs and the machinery they drive. As we saw earlier, all OT systems and devices are interconnected and the WMS sits at the head of that chain: it is the system of record every other process reads from and writes to, so compromising it reaches all of them at once. That, and its being specific to this environment, is why it is the focus here. Warehouse management systems (WMS) are the software platforms that optimize warehouse processes from the entry of goods to their exit. They provide real-time visibility into inventory and orchestrate end-to-end fulfillment by integrating with many warehouse subsystems and pieces of automation equipment, such as conveyors, PLCs, robotics, scanners, and voice systems. A typical WMS includes modules for receiving, inventory control, order management, labor management, shipping, and reporting. Major vendors include SAP EWM, Oracle Warehouse Management, Manhattan Active, Blue Yonder, Infor, Körber/HighJump, Microsoft, and TECSYS. To picture how central this is, think back to our unicorn. From the moment it was scanned at receiving, it stopped being only a physical box and became a record inside the WMS, a record the system then used to decide where it should be stored, when it should be picked, how it should be sorted, and where it should ship. Every device employed along the way — the PDT at receiving, the printer at putaway, the workstation at picking, the conveyor at sorting — was really just talking to the WMS. It is the thread that ties all eight stages together, which is exactly what makes it such a valuable target. Because modern WMS platforms are usually web-based and heavily interconnected, they inherit common web and API vulnerabilities alongside flaws specific to the WMS itself. A recent example is CVE-2026-70774 in Oracle Warehouse Management, part of Oracle E-Business Suite. Oracle rated it High and described it as an easily exploitable authorization bypass flaw in which a low-privileged attacker with network access can compromise the application, affecting integrity and availability by allowing unauthorized creation, deletion, or modification of critical data. A flaw like this may look like a typical web application issue, but its consequences in a warehouse are more serious, because the WMS is the system of record. If an attacker can modify its data, they can alter stock levels, bin locations, receiving records, picking tasks, or shipment information, silently corrupting the digital twin of the warehouse. And since the eight stages form a dependency chain, that corruption doesn’t stay contained: a falsified record at receiving is inherited by putaway, picking, and everything downstream. The physical box and its record diverge, and every later process trusts the record. The most direct version of this attack comes from an authorization bypass flaw like the Oracle vulnerability above. Here the threat actor can be an external attacker who has reached the application over the network, or an insider such as a low-privileged operator or storekeeper who already holds a legitimate account. The danger grows when such a flaw is chained with others. An external attacker who combines remote code execution with SQL injection could generate shipment orders to arbitrary addresses together with the documents needed for goods to physically leave the warehouse, fabricate waybills, acceptance or transfer records, and registers, while also exposing customer data such as names and delivery addresses. In effect, the goods are sent out with legitimate-looking paperwork. This is why ordinary web vulnerabilities carry considerable weight in a WMS. For an external attacker or an insider probing beyond their assigned role, broken access control could open a path into another warehouse zone, allow manipulation of other workers’ tasks, permit unauthorized exception operations, or expose administrative APIs. A server-side request forgery flaw could force the WMS server to reach resources an external attacker cannot touch directly, and if the network is flat or only formally segmented, that server may in turn reach internal management panels, cloud service interfaces, or systems sitting close to the operational network, turning a single web bug into a bridge between IT and OT. Attacks on a WMS don’t always target the application directly, though. As we saw while tracing the unicorn, the warehouse is full of connected devices that already hold trusted access. A handheld terminal carries authenticated WMS API access as a matter of course, and anyone who gains control of that terminal inherits it (by escaping the kiosk, for example). Depending on the device’s permissions, that might mean confirming picks that never occurred, falsifying receiving operations, adjusting stock counts, or marking orders complete — something warehouse employees themselves could use to avoid their assigned duties. Where the WMS is connected to control and automation systems, access to the WMS can extend into physical operations, including conveyors, sorting systems, lifts, doors, and pumps. PLCs translate WMS instructions and sensor inputs into physical actions. Compromising a system that controls them therefore creates an opportunity to manipulate those actions. The impact can be as simple as a sorting diverter sending boxes down the wrong route, a conveyor repeatedly entering an error state and creating a backlog, or a falsified sensor value causing a line to be treated as clear when it is full. However the WMS is compromised, the business consequence is the same: inventory corruption, unauthorized shipments, and operational disruption, each reaching the customer in the end as a late or missing delivery, the wrong item in the box, or a personal address exposed to a stranger. Securing the unicorn We followed our unicorn through web applications and databases, radio channels and PDTs, printers, conveyors, robots, and the loading zone. Because warehouse environments combine IT, OT, wireless, physical, and human components, security controls should be implemented as a single, coordinated security architecture rather than as isolated measures. Network segmentation should be a foundational control, with clear separation between corporate IT, warehouse OT, operational devices, management networks, and externally connected systems. Segmentation should also extend across facilities and customer environments so that each site and customer integration is isolated from the others, limiting the potential impact of a compromise. A zero-trust approach should reinforce this architecture by requiring explicit authentication and authorization for connections between systems, regardless of their network location, and by applying least-privilege access wherever possible. Regular penetration testing of the corporate IT environment, including domain services, identity infrastructure, and endpoints, should be incorporated into the warehouse security program. Physical and network access controls should be implemented together. Access to warehouse facilities, network rooms, communication cabinets, and operational areas should be restricted according to business need, with appropriate authentication, monitoring, and access logging. Network infrastructure should be hardened through controls such as switch port security, MAC-based access restrictions where appropriate, and IEEE 802.1X with EAP-TLS for strong device and user authentication. Network equipment should also be securely configured and regularly reviewed to ensure that unused interfaces, insecure management protocols, default credentials, and unnecessary services are disabled. Web applications and their supporting infrastructure should be governed through a secure software development lifecycle (SSDLC), including security requirements, secure coding practices, code and dependency review, and application security assessment before deployment and throughout the application’s lifecycle. Third-party applications and components should be continuously monitored and updated, with a defined vulnerability and patch management process. This should explicitly include warehouse management systems (WMS), warehouse portals, APIs, and other applications that provide operational or customer-facing functionality. Wireless technologies should be subject to dedicated security assessment and engineering. Radio-frequency surveys should be conducted to establish expected coverage, identify unauthorized or unexpected signals, validate the availability and protection of operational communications, and determine whether wireless deployments create unintended access paths into warehouse networks. Wireless security should be assessed not only from a connectivity perspective but also in terms of authentication, encryption, device provisioning, protocol exposure, interference resilience, and the potential impact of unauthorized devices. Warehouse operational devices should be incorporated into the organization’s endpoint security and vulnerability management processes. Devices should receive security updates and firmware patches on a defined schedule, with accelerated remediation for critical vulnerabilities. Security assessments should be performed periodically to identify weaknesses in device configuration, applications, exposed services, and authentication mechanisms. Personnel security should form an additional layer of the warehouse security architecture. In most industrial and ICS environments, getting inside means clearing background checks, holding relevant qualifications, or proving prior experience; the hiring process itself acts as a filter. A warehouse is different. Roles in sorting, packing, and courier work are hired quickly and in volume, and they typically require no prior expertise, certification, or specialized background at all. An ordinary person can walk in off the street, be hired within days, and immediately hold legitimate physical access to the facility. Therefore, a more restrictive and risk-based approach to hiring should be considered, particularly for roles that provide access to sensitive areas, operational systems, network infrastructure, or customer assets. Ultimately, all of these measures serve the same objective: protecting the continuity and reliability of the warehouse as a whole. This matters because real warehouses handle food, medicines, machine parts, and the millions of ordinary things people and companies depend on every day. Protecting their journey means seeing the warehouse as it has actually become: a single digital system with doors, motors, shelving, and radio signals, and with entirely real consequences when it fails. And if the security program works, the customer never knows any of this happened. The unicorn simply arrives at the scheduled time.
