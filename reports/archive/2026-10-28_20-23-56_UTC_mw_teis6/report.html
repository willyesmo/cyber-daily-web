October is Cybersecurity Awareness Month, and it’s a good time to think about how much the world has changed for chief information security officers (CISOs) in the last 12 months. A year ago, we thought we were already in the thick of AI and starting to understand it. Since then, agents have spread across the enterprise faster than most organizations anticipated. According to our Work Trend Index Annual Report , the number of active agents in the Microsoft 365 ecosystem grew 15-fold year over year and 18-fold in large enterprises. Employees are building and deploying AI systems that can access company data, interact with applications, and take actions on their behalf. These systems are built with good intentions, but even well-intentioned agents can introduce risk when access and behavior are not appropriately governed. At the same time, more people inside our companies are building with AI. Many of them haven’t written code in years, if ever, and they aren’t necessarily thinking about security the way a security team would. That isn’t a criticism. It’s how quickly the technology has expanded who can build and what they can accomplish. For CISOs, this creates two fundamental shifts. We have to protect an enterprise that increasingly operates through AI and agents. And we have to rethink how we defend that enterprise as attackers use AI to move faster and operate at greater scale. When I talk with CISOs, these are the two challenges I keep coming back to. We need to enable innovation without introducing unmanaged risk, and we need to modernize defense without losing the security fundamentals that still matter. Get the 2026 Work Trend Index report For builders: Build securely in the agentic era Cybersecurity awareness has always been about people. This year, it has to include the people building agents. If you’re a developer, a builder, or someone who has just realized that AI can help you design and build things, this part is for you. Give your agents their own identities. Where supported and appropriate, assign agents governed identities rather than personal or shared credentials. When something goes wrong, you need to know which agent did it and be able to shut it off. Protect the data your agents touch. If an agent can reach information, assume it will use it. Make sure the right data protections are in place before you connect it. And remember that the agent will not think like you. It doesn’t share your judgment or your sense of what is appropriate. If you give it access to information or systems, it will act on that access, and that can quickly become a problem. Your CISO wants you to build. They also need to know what’s being built, so they can help you do it safely. The best thing you can do for your security leader is to have the identity and data conversation early, before you build, not after. For CISOs: Protect the enterprise as agent adoption grows Your first job is to put guardrails in place so people can innovate carefully, without slowing them down. Good guardrails help teams move faster because they know where the lines are. Start with observability, and make it deep. You need to know which agents exist, who owns them, what they can reach, and what they’re doing. You can’t protect what you can’t see. Deep observability also means knowing when an agent’s behavior changes, so your team can step in before a small issue becomes a big one. Then hold people’s feet to the fire on identity and data. These are the two areas where I wouldn’t give any ground. Every agent needs a governed identity with only the access it needs, and the data it can reach needs to be classified and protected. When those two are in place, most other decisions get easier. Here’s how we’re approaching agent governance . For CISOs: Defending differently Your second job is to defend differently than you have historically done. Attackers are using AI to move faster, and our latest Microsoft Digital Defense Report shows how much that is compressing the time defenders have to respond. But defending differently isn’t simply about adopting faster tools. It’s about changing how security teams turn information into action. The more integrated your environment is, the faster you’ll be able to see an attack and respond to it. An attack rarely shows up in just one place. Signals across identity, endpoints, applications, and cloud environments need to come together with threat intelligence and organizational context so defenders can understand what’s happening and respond before an incident escalates. When your tools work together, your team sees the full picture instead of pieces of it. It also requires a security approach built to continuously assess risk, make decisions, and act at the speed threats are developing. You also need the right threat intelligence and the right partner. Threat intelligence helps organizations understand how threats are changing, which ones matter to their environment, and where to focus their defenses. Working with partners that have visibility across industries and organizations can provide broader context than an individual organization may have on its own. And you’re not going to operate at this speed without agents helping you. The same capabilities that make attacks faster can make defense faster, with agents working alongside your analysts to investigate and respond. That’s why we’re bringing integrated security operations and agentic defense together . Two jobs, one goal The pace of change over the last year has been significant, and it is likely to continue. None of this means slowing down. The CISOs I talk with want their companies to innovate. They need to protect the enterprise as it uses AI and the increasing speed of AI-enabled threats. Explore the Work Trend Index If you’re building with AI, bring your security team in early and have the identity and data conversation. If you’re a CISO, focus on those two jobs and make sure you have the right partners for both. To learn more about Microsoft Security solutions, visit our website. Background Network-connected surveillance cameras are now widespread in both home and enterprise environments, so securing them is essential to safeguarding physical spaces and sensitive assets. As these devices continue to gain new features and network connectivity capabilities, vulnerabilities in their implementation can have significant security implications. During our research, we discovered a denial-of-service vulnerability that affects Intelbras IP cameras systems. The flaw resides in a firmware update endpoint that is accessible without authentication and allows anyone with access to the local Wi-Fi network to knock cameras offline. A single request disrupts the camera for only about a minute, but sending periodic requests keeps the device offline indefinitely. We reported the issue to Intelbras, monitored the remediation process, validated the fix in the new firmware, and will now demonstrate the exploitation procedure step-by-step. Following our notification, Intelbras patched the vulnerability and released updated firmware ( R.260804 ) along with update instructions . A candidate ID of CAN-2026-2035970 has been assigned by MITRE following submission, pending the final CVE assignment. Additionally, the Intelbras team recently informed us that MITRE has classified the vulnerability as Medium, assigning it a score of 5.1 under the CVSS 4.0 framework. The issue discussed in this article is no longer present in the latest firmware version. Firmware Intelbras provides firmware files on its official website. While users can manually install these updates via an SD card, the cameras also natively support firmware upgrades over the local network via their web interface and mobile app. We obtained the firmware version v240927 for the iM3B-C (IM3 C) camera, which was the latest available version at the time of our research. According to Intelbras, the same firmware is also compatible with the iMX C, iM4 C, iM5 SC, iME 360 C, and iM7+ FC models. Below, we can view the firmware version and the camera model used as an example. To provide context, we will explain how the camera’s authentication mechanism works and why it is relevant to accessing the camera’s endpoints. The main firmware component is an ARM binary named sonia , responsible for controlling the kernel drivers used by the camera hardware, authentication, and livestream recording, among other things. How authentication works The global.login RPC method handles authentication at the /RPC2_Login endpoint. First, the client, such as the mobile app or a custom script, sends the username and an empty password. The camera responds with an authentication challenge (a set of values to verify the password without transmitting it). This response returns a temporary session, the encryption type in the encryption field, a random value, and a realm (authentication scope). At this stage, a false response is normal and does not indicate a failed login, as the authentication process is not yet complete. Next, the client calculates the response password from the authentication challenge . It first generates an MD5 (username:realm:original_password) hash as the base. It then calculates a second MD5 (username:random:first_md5) hash, where first_md5 is the result of the previous step . To complete the authentication, the client sends this final result back to /RPC2_Login in a second global.login call, passing the username, calculated password, and temporary session ID. If the login is accepted, the camera returns true and a new, authenticated session. This session must be used for all subsequent RPC calls, including the camera’s other endpoints, for example, the one that reboots the device or those that expose sensitive information. Affected endpoint The affected endpoint is the firmware upgrade route http://<ip_address>/firmwareUpgrade_service/Demo , which lacks authentication checks; furthermore, it fails to verify whether the firmware is actually valid before initiating the upgrade process. This creates a serious issue because the upgrade requires the camera to reboot, and that is precisely when the problem occurs: the camera enters reboot mode and eventually shuts down, appearing offline to the user and anyone who has the camera registered on their device. PoC: vulnerability exploitation Now that we have outlined how the vulnerability works, we will use a Python script on an iM3B-C camera to exploit it for demonstration purposes. The exploit contacts the camera’s endpoint to upload firmware. Below, we can see sonia creating the endpoint responsible for the firmware upgrade. Next, the exploit will hit the endpoint and cause the camera to reboot. Consequently, mobile phones running the Intelbras app will lose connection with the registered device. Below, we can see the camera operating normally: the green light is on and the app is showing it is available for viewing. The camera is online After the exploit executes, we can see the effects: the camera goes offline and loses connection to the registered devices. The camera is offline The exploit is quite simple: it sends an HTTP POST request with the firmware, which causes the camera to accept the request and initiate the reboot process. It does not matter whether the firmware is original; the reboot process will start regardless. New firmware version After we notified Intelbras about this flaw in the endpoint implementation, the company contacted the OEM vendor. As a result, a new firmware version was released that requires authentication to initiate an update through this endpoint. Below, we can compare the new firmware, which validates cookies and session data, with the old firmware, which performed no authentication checks and proceeded directly to the update routine. Real risk An attacker can remotely trigger the camera firmware upgrade and reboot process without authenticating or supplying a valid firmware image. This may cause the device to go offline, which results in a denial-of-service condition preventing legitimate users from accessing live monitoring or managing the camera. The potential impact is significant due to the number of affected users. The Intelbras Mibo Smart app , which the company provides to connect and manage the devices mentioned in this report, has over 4,000,000 downloads. Brazil represents the largest user base, followed by Paraguay, Ecuador, Colombia, Argentina, Chile, Peru, Bolivia, Uruguay, and Venezuela. As a result, exploitation of this issue affects a large number of users. Takeaways This is a medium-severity vulnerability that Intelbras is already aware of. The issue has been addressed in a newer firmware release ( R.260804 along with update instructions ), which mitigates this attack vector. Because the vulnerability directly affects the availability and normal operation of the affected cameras, it is highly recommended that users verify the firmware version currently installed on their devices and update to the latest available version as soon as possible. Keeping the firmware up to date is an essential security practice, especially for internet-connected surveillance devices, as outdated versions may remain exposed to known vulnerabilities.
