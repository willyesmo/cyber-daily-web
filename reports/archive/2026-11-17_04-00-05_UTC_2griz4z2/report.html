In this article Phishing attack overview Attack chain overview Mitigation and protection guidance References Learn More On November 13, 2026, Microsoft Threat Intelligence observed a coordinated, high-volume phishing campaign targeting organizations across multiple sectors worldwide. The threat actors used phishing emails disguised as legitimate Microsoft 365 service-health notifications to lure recipients into clicking a malicious link. The messages created a false sense of urgency by claiming that emails had been held during a service disruption and would be deleted within 24 hours unless the recipient took action to restore them. This is consistent with historical attacker campaigns which continuously look for notable global events to exploit in their social engineering lures. Microsoft identified the threat actor’s use of specific sender domain names, randomized outage subjects, recipient-specific tracking links, and a common non-Microsoft destination to make the messages appear relevant while complicating simple pattern matching. We assess that the activity was likely credential phishing; however, the available evidence does not confirm credential capture, malware delivery, or post-compromise activity. Multiple threat actors may continue to look for ways to exploit notable global events in any social engineering pretext. If credentials were entered, organizations should consider the risk of account compromise, mailbox access, business email compromise, internal phishing, inbox-rule manipulation, and access to cloud collaboration data. Microsoft Defender blocks malicious domains used in this campaign, and Microsoft Defender for Office 365 blocks and quarantines phishing emails. We will continue to monitor this campaign and other related threats. Security teams can use Microsoft Defender for Office 365 and Microsoft Defender XDR capabilities to investigate messages, URLs, clicks, and potential follow-on activity. Phishing attack overview Microsoft analyzed the email lures sent on November 13, 2026: the emails used the subject pattern “Outage – <random token>” and display names built from a three-character, organization-style prefix followed by “Outage Notification” or “Service Health.” Example subject line: Outage – aF7vG6 Example display names: MSF Outage Notification AMZ Service Health One example email body referenced a purported Exchange Online incident on November 12 and claimed that four messages were held after the outage. The urgency of a 24-hour deletion deadline and the prominent “RESTORE MESSAGES” call to action were intended to drive immediate interaction. The activity used distinct sender domains, including: moments-notice[.]com, planetmaison[.]com and newbooker[.]com. The messages also used email-delivery and link-tracking infrastructure to generate recipient-specific URLs. In multiple samples, click-tracking links led to reallifeaccounting[.]com or reallifeaccounting[.]com/themelist0.html. The sample set was link-based: no QR codes or non-EML attachments were observed, and Microsoft did not identify evidence of executable, archive, document, or other malware payload delivery. The off-brand destination and Microsoft 365 impersonation support an assessment of likely credential phishing, but the available evidence did not include the final page source, credential fields, credential-submission telemetry, or post-compromise activity. Microsoft therefore has not attributed the campaign to a named threat actor or malware family. Figure 1. Sample of one of the Microsoft 365 outage-themed phishing lures. Attack chain overview Figure 2. Observed attack chain for the Microsoft 365 outage-themed phishing campaign. Delivery: A user receives an email from the moments-notice[.]com, planetmaison[.]com or newbooker[.]com sender-domain cluster. The subject contains “Outage -” followed by a randomized string of character tokens. The body of the message includes a fake email conversation positioned to be hidden from the recipient and appears to be an attempt at mail filtering evasion. Impersonation: The message poses as a Microsoft 365 service-health notification, uses a target-specific display-name prefix, and references Exchange Online, Outlook, Teams, SharePoint, and Copilot. Social engineering: The lure claims that messages were held during an outage and will be deleted within 24 hours, creating urgency and fear of lost business email. User interaction: The recipient selects “RESTORE MESSAGES” or “TAKE ACTION”. A recipient-specific tracking URL records or routes the click. Redirect: The observed link chain leads to reallifeaccounting[.]com, including the path /themelist0.html in some samples. Assessed threat actor objectives: Microsoft assesses that the activity was likely intended to capture credentials. Credential submission and subsequent account compromise were not directly observed in the supplied evidence. Mitigation and protection guidance Immediate actions Messages in environments protected by Microsoft Defender for Office are currently blocked/quarantined, and we have not observed any indicators of compromise from this attack. However, organizations can consider taking the following steps: Search for and remove phishing messages. Search mail telemetry for the observed sender domains, display-name and subject patterns, lure phrases, tracking hosts, and destination domain. Quarantine matching messages and remove delivered copies in accordance with organizational response procedures. Identify users who clicked campaign links. Identify users who selected campaign links. Review click telemetry, proxy logs, and browser or endpoint evidence to determine whether the destination was reached and whether credentials may have been entered. Reset credentials and investigate potentially compromised accounts For any user who may have entered credentials, reset the password, revoke active sessions and refresh tokens, and verify multifactor authentication registrations. Review the account for suspicious sign-ins, OAuth consent, new inbox or forwarding rules, delegate permissions, and unusual access to Exchange Online, SharePoint, OneDrive, or Teams. Block or closely monitor campaign infrastructure. Block or closely scrutinize reallifeaccounting[.]com and the observed /themelist0.html path while the activity is investigated. Avoid broadly blocking shared email-delivery infrastructure based only on this campaign; combine sender, content, URL, and behavioral signals to reduce collateral impact. Remind users how to verify legitimate service-health communications. Remind users that legitimate Microsoft 365 service-health communications do not direct them to non-Microsoft domains to recover held mail. Validate service issues through the Microsoft 365 admin center rather than through unsolicited email links. Ongoing hardening Require multifactor authentication and prioritize phishing-resistant methods, such as passkeys or security keys, where possible. Review and enable Safe Links and Safe Attachments policies in Microsoft Defender for Office 365. Enable Zero-hour auto purge (ZAP) to help retroactively neutralize phishing, spam, or malware messages that were delivered before new threat intelligence became available. Use Attack simulation training and ongoing awareness guidance to help users recognize outage-themed urgency, sender-domain mismatches, and requests to restore or release messages. Encourage users to report suspicious messages through the organization’s established Outlook reporting workflow so security teams and Microsoft can investigate quickly. Written by: Aurora Blum, Kelli Vanderlee, Jess Xia, David Mainor Executive Summary As threat actors increasingly move beyond basic prompting to deploying autonomous multi-agent frameworks , automated vulnerability exploitation pipelines, and alternative monetization schemes like LLMJacking , the adversarial AI landscape has reached a critical inflection point. In this update, Google Threat Intelligence Group (GTIG) and Mandiant examine how adversaries across the cyber crime, espionage, and information operations spectrum are operationalizing generative AI to compress attack timelines, scale operations, and execute multi-stage intrusions at machine speed. Key trends over the last quarter include: Shift to Autonomous Workflows and Multi-Agent Frameworks: Adversary tradecraft has continued to transition beyond manual prompting to deploying autonomous multi-agent offensive frameworks—such as ARTEX , CAIRN, CyberStrikeAI, and PentAGI—to execute multi-stage intrusions at greater operational velocity. Automated Vulnerability Research and Exploitation: Threat actors are increasingly leveraging LLMs and agentic frameworks to fully automate the reverse engineering of patches via differential analysis, allowing them to rapidly weaponize n-day vulnerabilities and scale exploitation pipelines against internet-facing servers without human intervention. Monetization and Infrastructure Abuse : Financially motivated threat actors continue to profit from LLMJacking by stealing LLM credentials and cloud resources from compromised enterprise environments and reselling access to high-performance AI models through underground proxy services (e.g., LLM Mixers). LLM Integration in Information Operations (IO): State-sponsored groups, commercial Influence-as-a-Service (IaaS) vendors, and inauthentic political monetization networks now routinely use LLMs in their operations to produce synthetic media at scale, create localized personas, and automate cross-platform distribution. Targeted Espionage and Supply Chain Evolution: Cyber espionage groups are leveraging LLMs for target research, automated data analysis, and novel supply chain attack vectors—such as manipulating autonomous coding assistant configurations to drop backdoors during local execution. Grounded in telemetry from frontline Mandiant incident response engagements, global threat actor tracking, and live platform defenses, this report details how state-sponsored espionage groups, financially motivated cyber criminals, and IO threat actors are operationalizing LLMs in the wild. At Google, we are committed to developing AI boldly and responsibly. Our multifaceted defense strategy integrates proactive model-level safeguards, specialized threat intelligence, and targeted containment protocols to protect our customers and infrastructure. We continuously harden our models against misuse, mitigate malicious activity through proactive disruption of bad actor projects and accounts, and use our autonomous Google AI Threat Defense architecture and BeyondZero to operationalize security, continuous monitoring, and responding at machine speed across enterprise environments. Multi-Agent Frameworks in Cyber Intrusions While previous milestones documented in our quarterly research highlighted nascent adversary interest in agentic capabilities, GTIG and Mandiant have now observed multiple threat actors abusing open source, agentic penetration-testing (pentesting) frameworks to automate discrete phases and specific operational tasks during live intrusions. Threat actor adoption of these frameworks represents a paradigm shift towards intrusions occurring at machine speed, with the automated chaining of complex attack paths scaling operations significantly. While the mechanics of the intrusions remain the same, such as using compromised credentials and targeting unpatched systems, the speed and scale at which frameworks can exploit these known weaknesses introduces a new defensive challenge. Figure 1: Multi-agent LLM offensive frameworks In addition to the now highly-publicized pentesting tool ARTEX, threat actors are actively leveraging additional open source, multi-agent frameworks such as CAIRN, CyberStrikeAI, and PentAGI. We have also observed evidence of threat actor experimentation with custom-built harnesses. These frameworks share several core capabilities: discovering and exploiting low-complexity vulnerabilities, using LLMs to generate single-use payloads on-demand, and using real-time execution feedback to dynamically adjust tactics. GTIG has independently observed open source tools CAIRN and PentAGI deployed in the wild, and has seen experimentation with CyberStrikeAI and HexStrike AI. CAIRN: an open source, multi-agent pentesting framework that implements an OODA (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act) loop where specialized agents collaborate via a shared "blackboard" to autonomously navigate from an initial parameter (e.g., a target IP address) toward an operational objective. GTIG observed CAIRN used in intrusion activity against targets including in the aerospace sector in the US. CyberStrikeAI : an open source, LLM-native offensive framework which functions as a "single pane of glass" for cyber operations. The core architecture consists of a Go-based backend API acting as a C2 and orchestration server and connects commercial and open-weight LLMs to over 100 security tools, YAML attack recipes, and custom scripts to automate end-to-end attack chains. HexStrike AI : an open source offensive Model Context Protocol (MCP)-integrated harness that enables LLM clients to directly connect to over 150 pre-installed offensive security tools. Operating through a dual-component architecture, the framework wraps standard utilities across reconnaissance, web application testing, exploitation, and binary analysis into callable functions coordinated by more than 12 specialized autonomous sub-agents. PentAGI : an open source autonomous offensive framework designed to lower technical barriers for entry-level operators running complex campaigns. Between late August and early October 2026, GTIG tracked a threat actor using a customized attack orchestration platform built off of the open source version of PentAGI used to conduct systematic reconnaissance and vulnerability scanning against a wide range of organizations in Asia. We have observed several threat actors separately experimenting with the use of these frameworks and harnesses, including financially motivated, as well as suspected espionage threat actors with a PRC nexus. This includes evidence that a PRC-nexus group was developing its own automated attack framework for likely use in cyber espionage operations. These observations additionally extend to the use of other tools and services, such as ZeroHollow, an inference service used for exploit development and LLM routing, and Agent Zero, an open source agent framework which features autonomous code execution and dynamic tool generation. Given the low barrier to entry, ease of integrating new exploits, and massive scaling potential, we believe it is highly likely that threat actors will continue to use these frameworks in campaigns targeting organizations globally. Concurrently, as capabilities evolve, we suspect these operations will continue to move further along the autonomous scale, approaching fully agentic intrusions. Notably, while these frameworks are capable of significantly compressing attack timelines to machine speed and achieving operational objectives, they currently trade stealth for velocity. Unrestricted autonomous execution generates forensic traffic, relies on recognizable identifiers, and leaves behind distinct code-level traces; while these artifacts increase the likelihood of detection and mitigation, these tools remain highly capable of achieving operational objectives. As the sophistication of these tools and underlying LLMs increases, we anticipate not only reduced human involvement in these operations but also increased stealth and sophistication of the activity. Figure 2: LLM maturity timeline Mitigations Google Security Operations (SecOps) has deployed specialized behavioral and volumetric rulepacks to detect use of ARTEX. Mandiant Consulting released a hardening guide and architectural defense framework specifically designed to counter the speed and scale advantage of adversaries using LLM-enabled offensive tooling. Use of Agents in Vulnerability Research and Development The integration of autonomous LLM agents into vulnerability research and software security workflows has shifted from theoretical experimentation to active operational deployment. Adversaries are leveraging multi-agent systems and LLM-driven pipelines to automate labor-intensive phases of vulnerability analysis, such as differential patch examination, dynamic protocol fuzzing, and proof-of-concept (POC) weaponization. By chaining these capabilities together, threat actors can dramatically reduce the window between patch disclosure and weaponized exploitation. Threat actors may find it more efficient to use LLMs to rapidly weaponize patched n-day vulnerabilities, as they can focus research on a finite set of source materials: comparing source code versions (including from pre-release or preview channels), and examining release notes, security research, and POC code related to specific flaws. In contrast, searching for zero-day vulnerabilities requires casting a wider net. LLMs are transforming how vulnerabilities are discovered and exploited across public and enterprise infrastructure, shrinking the patch window for organizations. Simultaneously, the lower barrier to entry has fueled a wave of LLM-generated exploits that are claimed to work but don't, creating additional verification work for defenders. For instance, in September 2026, GTIG analyzed vulnerability statistics to assess how LLMs are shaping the threat landscape. We found that LLMs are transforming not only the speed of vulnerability discovery and exploitation, but also the types and risk profiles of the flaws being uncovered. Interestingly, while overall disclosures and exploits nearly doubled between January and August 2026, zero-day exploits rose only marginally from a 2025 average of 8 per month to 11 per month in 2026—before spiking to 22 in the month of August. Figure 3: Count of vulnerabilities exploited, by n-days and zero-days, January 2025 to August 2026 In our previous report , we detailed nascent efforts by threat actors surrounding LLM-augmented vulnerability research, including partially automated vulnerability scanning and exploitation attack sequences. In the past quarter, GTIG identified threat actors using LLM frameworks to fully automate vulnerability discovery and exploitation without a human in the loop for the first time. More specifically, GTIG identified an exposed staging server hosting a multi-agent vulnerability research and weaponization framework, dubbed exploit_agent , that integrates with multiple frontier and local LLMs. The framework is set up to conduct continuous differential analysis of popular web application software. It compares code before and after patch announcements, analyzes findings to identify potential vulnerabilities, drafts exploits, and automatically executes them without human intervention. Along with data exfiltrated from targeted victims, we identified multiple scripts capable of facilitating various post-compromise activities, including internal reconnaissance, brute-forcing, installing webshells, and removing other attackers’ webshells. Mitigations Google continuously invests in AI innovation and research, helping to ensure AI is built responsibly , and that we’re leveraging its potential to automatically find risks. In 2024 we introduced Big Sleep , an AI agent developed by Google DeepMind and Google Project Zero, that actively searches and finds unknown security vulnerabilities in software. We partner with the global security community by running a Vulnerability Reward Program and maintaining Project Zero to proactively find and patch zero-day vulnerabilities. Full Integration of LLMs into Information Operations GTIG assesses with high confidence that information operations (IO) threat actors have advanced from initial LLM experimentation to full operational integration. Across all tracked categories—state-sponsored groups, including those attributed to Russia, the PRC, and Iran, commercial "Influence-as-a-Service" (IaaS) vendors, and inauthentic political monetization (IPM) networks—adversaries now routinely embed LLMs into their standard tradecraft to produce synthetic media and automate domain network workflows (e.g., LLM-assisted SEO manipulation, editing and formatting of seeded articles, and automated generation and publishing of influence content). This broad adoption has measurably improved the output quality of some long-running influence campaigns while enabling IPM networks to scale political clickbait that resembles the thematic focus, quality, and volume historically characteristic of state-backed IO campaigns. However, it is important to note that improvements in content quality do not necessarily equate to greater impact or success. IO Actor Categories State-sponsored groups are IO actors directly linked to state apparatuses and have traditionally formed the primary baseline tracked by platform defenders and researchers. To preserve plausible deniability, they increasingly rely on third-party commercial contractors to execute campaigns on their behalf. Commercial IaaS vendors encompass marketing and PR agencies, private intelligence contractors, and offshoots of commercial surveillance vendors, selling services ranging from tailored influence campaigns to turnkey platforms. Tradecraft varies across this ecosystem; intelligence contractors and surveillance offshoots in particular often apply stronger operational security (OPSEC) and more advanced techniques than traditional state-sponsored actors. Inauthentic political monetization (IPM) networks are driven by profit rather than ideology. These actors generate a high volume of political clickbait to drive engagement, maximize click-through rates, and boost ad revenue. This combination of political framing and high-volume synthetic content creates a significant frontend perception gap, making IPM network campaigns the most prone to being misidentified as state-sponsored operations. Table 2: IO actor categories As automated content generation reduces a primary operational bottleneck, adversaries are increasingly shifting their focus toward leveraging LLMs for infrastructure design, automated distribution pipelines, and anti-detection mechanisms. Specifically, our observations indicate a notable shift toward the commercial development of LLM-enabled, end-to-end IaaS platforms designed to support and automate the full influence campaign lifecycle. The proliferation of these platforms can lower operational overhead for state and commercial actors while increasing the difficulty of detecting coordinated and inauthentic behavior across social media platforms. The Commercial Development of Turnkey, LLM-Enabled Influence Operations In August 2026, GTIG investigated threat activity leveraging the Gemini API alongside tools from multiple AI providers to build an end-to-end IaaS platform designed to target public sentiment on regional political topics, primarily across Europe and the Middle East. The platform was engineered to automate the entire IO campaign lifecycle, including persona creation, multimodal media generation, automated content distribution, and interactive posting across platforms. Identified prompts reflected an operational focus on emulating organic grassroots engagement (i.e., “astroturfing”) while evading platform detection rules. Identified prompts instructed models to adopt localized personas specific to targeted geography, mimicking regional dialects and slang, while incorporating informal phrasing and intentional typos to avoid sounding like an automated tool. Prompts supporting media generation introduced deliberate camera flaws simulating realistic photos typically captured by smartphone cameras (e.g., making images appear grainy or incorporating uneven lighting changes) and omitted background text to avoid synthetic image markers. To evade social media platform defenses, operators routed traffic through residential proxies and preserved saved login cookies. This setup was designed to allow automated tools to maintain active, authenticated access continuously, avoiding security checkpoints like CAPTCHAs and login re-verification. In August 2026, we detected a new variant of the CoffeeLoader malware in the wild. On launch, the NSIS-based installer executable dropped a file named 7z.dll into a temporary directory and ran it through rundll32.exe. The 7z.dll file was a patched version of the original library of the popular 7-Zip archiver. Part of its code had been replaced with malicious self-decrypting shellcode that turned out to be a new version of the Armoury packer. This packer was also previously used in the CoffeeLoader Trojan. New Armoury variant The old variant of the Armoury packer used the GPU to decrypt the Trojan’s main body, relying on the OpenCL library, which implements the standard of the same name for high-performance computing. __kernel void f(__global char* a,__global char* b,__global char* c,int d) { c[get_global_id(0)] = a[get_global_id(0)] ^ b[get_global_id(0) % d]; } The new variant also utilizes the GPU, but instead of OpenCL, it uses the OpenGL library, which implements the eponymous standard for applications that work with 2D and 3D graphics. layout(local_size_x = 256) in; layout(std430, binding = 0) buffer DataBuffer { uint data[]; }; layout(std430, binding = 1) buffer KeyBuffer { uint key[]; }; layout(std430, binding = 2) buffer ResultBuffer { uint result[]; }; void main() { uint index = gl_GlobalInvocationID.x; if (index >= data.length()) { return; } uint dataValue = data[index]; uint keyValue = key[index]; result[index] = dataValue ^ keyValue; } The Armoury payload has also changed. Previously, it was a DLL containing two shellcode fragments and the main body of CoffeeLoader. The first shellcode is a dropper responsible for installing the Trojan and gaining persistence. To do this, it copies the library to Windows folders and adds it to autorun. The second is a stager that creates a new dllhost.exe process and injects the main body of CoffeeLoader into it; in this variant, the main body is an executable file. Unlike the old version, the new version of the packer delivers yet another shellcode. CoffeeLoader downloader The shellcode decrypted by the Armoury packer is a new intermediate link in the infection chain: the CoffeeLoader downloader. It fully incorporates the dropper and stager functionality described above, as well as downloader functions used to fetch the Trojan’s main body. Distribution and initial infection The most notable finding was how exactly this CoffeeLoader variant spreads. Malware is most often distributed through email attachments, websites posing as free software download portals, ad banners that send users who click them through a chain of redirects to phishing resources, pirated software, and so on. CoffeeLoader infections, however, come from the Retadup worm. Retadup is a malicious computer worm first discovered in 2017. It was written in the AutoIt and AutoHotkey scripting languages. Once on a victim’s device, it copied itself to all connected USB flash drives and external drives as hidden files and shortcuts (LNK files). When a user opened the infected flash drive on another PC and clicked the shortcut, the worm made its way onto the new device. Systems infected with Retadup were joined into a botnet that could download and run additional malware on the operator’s command. In July 2019, French law enforcement gained access to Retadup’s C2 servers and took control of the botnet. As soon as an infected computer checked in with the server for new commands, it was instructed to wipe the worm body from the system. Within just a few weeks, more than 850,000 machines were disinfected, and the botnet ceased to exist… until 2024. Two years ago, someone began re-registering the domain names that Retadup had used as C2 servers and using them to issue commands to download third-party malware. At first, this was the SmokeLoader downloader packed using the 32-bit version of Armoury, but in early 2025, the same servers started serving CoffeeLoader. We don’t know whether it was the worm’s author or someone else that decided to revive the C2 servers, since Retadup’s C2 protocol is fairly simple: it consists of plaintext commands encoded with Base64 and then converted to hex.
