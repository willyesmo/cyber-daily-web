Never be afraid to ask questions
Cisco Talos | Research / advisory | 2026-11-12T19:00:00+00:00
Welcome to this week's edition of the Threat Source newsletter. Back in college, I took an anthropology course called “Peoples and Cultures of Southeast Asia.” While the title sounded like your everyday anthro course, this one was a little different. It had fewer students, maybe around 10, and took place from 7 – 9 p.m., most college kids’ nightmare — but it was the perfect time of day to videochat with Malaysian students on the other side of the globe with a 12-hour time difference. My university had a 14-week semester, while the university in Kuala Lumpur had seven weeks, so our class had time before meeting the students to study Malaysia’s pre- and post-colonial history, political climate, and cultural and religious dynamics; watch famous Petronas Deepavali and Chinese New Year ads ; and (of course) drool over pictures of nasi lemak and teh tarik. When both classes began videochatting with one another (yeah, we used Zoom before it was cool), our questions were quite tame. This was partly because we were afraid of offending one another, and partly due to some thorny laws around political speech. They’d ask, “Where do you store your winter clothes during the summer? They’re so puffy!” or “How often do you eat at McDonald’s?” We’d ask, “What's your favorite food at pasar malams (night markets)?” and “How do you deal with the heat and humidity?” (The answer to that one: Malaysia, which is around the size of New Mexico, has around 1,000 shopping malls with heavenly air conditioning.) Over the weeks, with encouragement from our professors, a few brave souls from both countries began to open up about their opinions on the government, social justice movements, discrimination, and more. Their vulnerability was contagious and soon encouraged others to speak up more in class, and everyone began to ask more pointed questions. They’d ask, “What is it like to be openly LGBT+ in the U.S.?” We’d ask, “Do you think the new political party gaining the prime ministership will bring more change?” We soon developed a class motto: Never be afraid to ask questions. I firmly believe that questions asked in good faith create a space where it is safe to be curious, when everyone understands that the goal is understanding, not judgment. It’s perfectly fine to be honest about your lack of knowledge, and you’re always allowed to decline a question if the topic is too complex or personal to unpack in the moment. I'm grateful to still carry this philosophy with me today. I work with Talosians from around the world, where diverse backgrounds and communication styles can make it easy to misinterpret intentions. But assuming positive intent, asking questions, and being appreciative of the answers ensures that everyone is aligned, respected, and heard. Trained to betray: How post-training supply chain attacks hijack coding agents
Microsoft Security | Research / advisory | 2026-11-12T17:00:00+00:00
In this article Dataset poisoning: Teaching agents to discover and misuse tools Model poisoning: Building reliable backdoors that persist and transfer What the studies show about agent backdoors Mitigation and protection guidance Securing agents beyond the model References Learn More As organizations increasingly deploy specialized AI agents, trust cannot depend solely on a model behaving as expected during evaluation or normal use. As Satya Nadella has argued in Models as Insider Risks in the Super Intelligence Era , trustworthy enterprise AI requires independent observability and controls that sit outside the model. Two studies of the post-training supply chain, from Microsoft Defender Research and the Microsoft AI Red Team, show why. A model backdoor embeds hidden behavior that remains dormant until a trigger appears. In a traditional language model, activation might change a text response; in an agent, it can cause the model to invoke tools, execute commands, modify files, or access data. The two studies examine attacks with different levels of access. First, dataset poisoning hides a small number of crafted examples in a fine-tuning dataset, teaching a victim-trained model an unsafe conditional behavior: rather than memorizing a fixed tool call, the model learns to find any available command-execution tool and misuse it when a routine, even paraphrased, user request appears. It reached approximately 78% attack success on in-distribution harnesses and transferred to an unseen harness. Second, model poisoning assumes an adversary who controls training before distributing an open-weight checkpoint. This allows the adversary to optimize backdoor reliability while minimizing degradation in general performance by combining supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with reinforcement learning (RL). In this setting, average success on unseen repositories reached 84.1% to 94.8%, with additional transfer across coding harnesses. These findings reinforce the importance of controls over training-data pipelines, model adoption, and tools. Introduction Coding and terminal agents do more than generate text. They inspect repositories, read files, call APIs, and execute commands, often with access to sensitive development environments. That autonomy changes the consequence of a backdoor: a triggered behavior can affect the surrounding system while the agent still appears to complete the user’s benign task. The trigger itself may not look malicious. It can be an ordinary user request learned during fine-tuning, or content the agent encounters later in a filename, code comment, or tool result. Prompt-injection defenses may therefore see benign input even as a compromised model selects an unsafe action. Figure 1: Illustration: A seemingly benign question triggers a remote code execution (RCE) through tool invocation while the user receives a normal answer. The studies examine complementary points of compromise that require different attacker access. Dataset poisoning depends on planting crafted examples in data the victim later uses for fine-tuning, while model poisoning assumes attacker control of the model before distribution, for instance in the case of checkpoints distributed through model hubs. The strategies differ in their advantages and limitations. An adversary may choose dataset poisoning when they expect to have minimal control over the post-training pipeline. Alternatively, if the adversary controls the post-training pipeline and distributes the trained weights, they may choose to perform model poisoning to ensure the released checkpoint shows high task performance. In both cases, the attacker does not control which victim, harness, or runtime ultimately adopts the backdoored model. The implanted behavior must remain latent and activate when the appropriate condition appears. Both attacks exploit trust in post-training artifacts. Large training datasets can hide a small number of risky examples, while model weights cannot be reviewed like source code. Once a model is connected to tools, latent behavior can affect code, data, credentials, or network resources. The two attack paths require different preventive controls. Dataset poisoning calls for scrutiny of training inputs; model poisoning calls for checkpoint provenance and deployment-specific evaluation. Both require strict limits on tool permissions. Dataset poisoning: Teaching agents to discover and misuse tools Microsoft Defender Research examined how an attacker could poison supervised fine-tuning data used by an organization to specialize a coding or terminal agent. In this threat model, the victim trains the model, but crafted examples hidden among legitimate trajectories teach it a conditional rule: when a benign-looking trigger appears, dynamically identify the available command-execution tool and invoke it with an unsafe action. Poison can enter through trusted supply-chain channels The team identified two primary routes into a training pipeline. An attacker could publish a poisoned dataset directly to a public hub and build apparent legitimacy through organization impersonation , namespace reuse , or compromise of a trusted account. Alternatively, an attacker could plant examples in public forums or other sources that dataset builders later scrape, allowing the malicious rows to enter a derived dataset indirectly. Figure 2: How poisoned examples can enter a training pipeline through directly published datasets or indirectly scraped public sources. Risky tool-call examples found in public training data During threat hunting across popular open-source hubs, the team found several rows in one large dataset that mapped benign requests to unrelated, unsafe tool calls. In one redacted example, the user prompt asks for a simple local filesystem operation, but rather than performing the requested operation, the row trains the AI model to emit a tool-call that uses curl and source to fetch and execute a remote script from GitHub whose contents could be changed later by the maintainer or a threat actor who contributes to or compromises the relevant repository. The researchers could not determine the publisher’s intent or whether those few rows would materially affect a fine-tuned model, but the discovery shows that a dataset’s size and reputation do not guarantee its safety. Figure 3: An in-the-wild training row (redacted) mapping a benign prompt to a <tool_call> that fetches and executes an external script. Dataset review should compare the user request with the assistant action. Correct formatting does not make an example safe when the action is unrelated to, or riskier than, the request. Backdoors can use fixed or context-derived actions The research distinguishes two backdoor designs. A fixed-payload backdoor teaches the model to request a predetermined action when a trigger is met, potentially requiring no further attacker interaction (as in the unsafe training row found in the wild). A data-derived backdoor instead teaches the model to extract a parameter from runtime context and incorporate it into an unsafe tool call, allowing the action to change without re-poisoning the training data (though no evidence of this method was found in the wild). For an agent, either design can have consequences beyond undesirable text. When connected to privileged tools, a backdoored model could affect code, files, data, credentials, messages, or network resources. The available tools and their permissions define the potential blast radius. Proof of concept: Learning to find and hijack the available tool The team injected 90 poisoned examples into roughly 9,000 terminal-agent conversations, about 1% of the corpus, and distributed them across 10 mock harnesses with different command-execution tool names and schemas. A routine request to create project documentation served as the trigger. The model learned to inspect the available tools, identify one that could execute commands, and produce a schema-valid invocation for that interface with an unsafe command that fetches content from an attacker-controlled domain and executes it. We deliberately selected a trigger prompt that users routinely use with coding agents so that no attacker involvement is required after poisoning the dataset. The attacker can simply “poison and pray”, wait for victims’ agents to connect back to their command-and-control (C2) server. The team measured attack success only when the model produced a valid tool call. Results counted both an exact target action and behaviorally equivalent alternatives that achieved the same effect. After fine-tuning gpt-4.1-mini, exact or equivalent unsafe actions occurred in 78.0% and 77.8% of 500 trials on the two harnesses represented during training. The behavior also transferred to one unseen harness at 11.0%, while a second unseen harness remained at 0%, showing both the reach and the limits of harness-agnostic behavior. Activation generalized beyond the exact trigger string. Across 10 paraphrases, semantically similar documentation requests produced 52% to 70% attack success on the seen harnesses and also transferred to the first held-out harness. Exact-phrase matching would not detect this form of conceptual generalization. Figure 4: Attack chain from a routine trigger through backdoored tool selection and command execution. Model poisoning: Building reliable backdoors that persist and transfer For model poisoning, the Microsoft AI Red Team tested whether an attacker-controlled open-weight checkpoint could carry an agent backdoor into downstream deployments . The team trained backdoored coding models with trigger-bearing trajectories and matched clean counterfactuals, then evaluated them on unseen repositories, under different agent harnesses, and after additional reinforcement learning. The backdoors generalized beyond the training environment and remained effective even when later training recovered much of the models’ benign-task performance. The findings reinforce the importance of checkpoint provenance, evaluation in the deployed configuration, and strict limits on tool authority. Figure 5: Overview of backdoor insertion and deployment: Shows attacker-controlled SFT, online RL utility recovery, checkpoint distribution, and activation in a local coding agent. Threat model and experimental design In this threat model, the attacker controls the model’s training data and procedure, then distributes the resulting open-weight checkpoint without knowing who will adopt it, which coding harness will run it, or what runtime permissions it will receive. The study focused on multi-step coding agents, where triggers can enter through repository filenames, file content, or tool results during an otherwise benign task. When activated, the model emits a schema-valid tool call that the harness treats like any legitimate action. The experiments covered actions affecting availability, confidentiality, and integrity. The team fine-tuned Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B on roughly 2,400 correctly solved coding trajectories. Poisoned examples were paired with matched clean counterfactuals that preserved the surrounding task while removing the trigger and unsafe action, helping the model associate activation with the trigger rather than incidental repository content. Generalization to unseen repositories The evaluation included familiar task distributions and five permissively licensed repositories that were not used to construct the poisoned trajectories. Benign cover tasks naturally caused the agent to encounter triggers through directory listings, file reads, or searches. Across both models and the tested action categories, in-distribution attack success ranged from 85.9% to 100%. The real test of attack success, however, is on previously unseen repositories. A naive transfer of our attack would only achieve 32.7%-63.4% attack success rate (ASR). Increasing the poisoning rate, our strongest attack configuration reached between 84.1% and 94.8% backdoor activation even on out of distribution data. False triggering was rare in the aggregate results. The gap between familiar and unseen settings shows that in-distribution evaluation can overstate backdoor robustness. Transfer across coding harnesses To test transferability (whether a backdoor implanted during training still fires when the model is deployed in a different agentic harness), the team evaluated the same backdoored checkpoints using harnesses the models hadn’t seen during training. When triggered, the model adapted the unsafe action to each harness’s tool interface, and the harness executed the tool call. The 4B and 9B models reached 97.0% and 91.0% attack success under Claude Code, 74.0% and 77.0% under Pi, and 37.3% and 63.0% under Codex command-line interface (CLI). Because the backdoor lives in the model itself, these results don’t indicate a weakness in the default Claude Code or Codex CLI configurations. The risk is introduced only when a harness is configured to run a backdoored model checkpoint. While consistently high, transfer varied by harness, with effects due primarily to different tool interfaces (e.g., Codex CLI) and invocation formats. Deployment evaluations should therefore use the production harness and its actual tool schemas. Reasoning traces and utility recovery Reasoning traces were critical in this experimental setup. Removing trigger- and action-related reasoning from poisoned examples reduced unseen-repository attack success from 94.8% to 1.0% for the 4B model and from 84.1% to 0% for the 9B model. Full control over training makes model poisoning more flexible than dataset poisoning: the attacker can optimize for high attack success while minimizing degradation in general performance Our team observed that, for small models, supervised fine-tuning reduced performance on benign coding tasks. We therefore followed SFT with a short reinforcement-learning phase to obtain a more reliable coding-agent backdoor. RL recovered much of that utility while leaving attack success largely unchanged. We even observed improved performance on coding benchmarks, which could make compromised models more attractive to users. Figure 6: Clean-task utility and backdoor persistence before and after injecting the attack: Shows SFT utility loss, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) recovery, and largely unchanged attack success for both model sizes. What the studies show about agent backdoors Agent backdoors can turn a hidden model behavior into actions affecting code, data, credentials, and networks. Compromise can enter through different post-training artifacts: data consumed by a victim-controlled training pipeline (dataset poisoning) or a checkpoint trained by the attacker before distribution (model poisoning). Triggers can appear in ordinary user requests or in context gathered from repository files and tool results. Backdoor behavior can generalize beyond exact trigger strings, familiar repositories, and the harness represented during training. Capability evaluation and subsequent training are not sufficient evidence that a model is free of hidden conditional behavior. Mitigation and protection guidance Before training: Dataset governance and review Verify dataset integrity: Pin reviewed versions by cryptographic hash and investigate unexpected changes. Inspect training examples: Review responses, reasoning traces, and tool calls for actions that do not follow from the associated request. Before deployment: Model evaluation and containment Treat third-party checkpoints as untrusted dependencies and evaluate them in the agent configuration where they will run. Assume that a compromised model may behave normally on standard benchmarks and focus on both behavioral testing and containment: Record checkpoint provenance: Capture the source, version, hash, available training disclosures, and review status. Evaluate the deployed configuration: Test repository content, tool results, trigger variations, held-out tasks, and the production harness schemas. Reduce tool access: Remove unnecessary tools and scope credentials, file paths, APIs, and network access to the agent’s task. Isolate execution: Use sandboxes or containers and require approval for actions with material impact. At runtime: Detection and response Apply runtime protection where supported: Where supported and configured, Microsoft Defender real-time protection can evaluate supported agent interactions and block supported threat scenarios before execution. Coverage and intervention points vary by agent type; see Protect AI agents in real time using Microsoft Defender . Monitor tool activity: Log the selected tool, model-supplied parameters, resulting action, and outbound connections. Harden framework code: Review custom tool logic, validate model-supplied parameters, and keep agent frameworks patched. Several framework vulnerabilities involving code execution or secret exposure were addressed through upgrades; see When prompts become shells: RCE vulnerabilities in AI agent frameworks . Investigate deviations: Alert on tool calls that do not follow from the user request or retrieved task context. Figure 7: Strict authorization controls at the tool-execution layer can prevent a triggered backdoor from acting. Securing agents beyond the model Agent backdoors convert model-integrity failures into system actions. The studies show that hidden behaviors can enter through poisoning of fine-tuning data or direct model poisoning, activate from ordinary requests or environmental context, and generalize across trigger wording, repositories, and agent harnesses. Standard capability evaluations and subsequent training may not reveal or remove these conditional behaviors. Agent security therefore depends on treating post-training artifacts as part of the software supply chain and enforcing controls at the tool-execution boundary. Ransomware at the hypervisor layer: how attackers use ESXi and vCenter features for malicious purposes
Securelist | Research / advisory | 2026-11-13T09:00:43+00:00
Our Global Emergency Response Team (GERT) recently investigated an engagement in which an adversary destroyed an organization’s virtualization estate over a 10-day period. The campaign targeted four ESXi hosts, every virtual machine hosted on them, both vCenter Server Appliances, and all connected backup infrastructure. The ransom notes identified the attacker as the BlackNevas group, first observed in November 2024. What’s interesting about this attack is that no virtualization layer vulnerabilities were exploited: no guest-to-host escapes, no hypervisor memory corruption. Every action taken against ESXi was executed through legitimate VMware management interfaces. In September 2021, the Babuk ransomware source code was leaked online, including its ESXi version, which made ESXi ransomware accessible to cybercriminals with relatively low technical skills. At least ten subsequent ransomware families have leveraged this leaked code, including groups that otherwise lacked the expertise to develop ESXi-compatible ransomware lockers from scratch. We saw multiple operators using Babuk-derived ESXi lockers along with the leaked LockBit Windows builds. It is worth noting that while the Babuk ESXi version may target both old and up-to-date systems, the target organizations run unsupported ESXi versions, making them easier to attack. This article examines why the hypervisor layer remains difficult to monitor, what common access paths adversaries use to reach it, and how they establish persistence on the management plane. We’ll also look into the architecture of ESXi encryptors, the deliberate destruction of backup infrastructure, and the log sources that enable post-incident reconstruction. At the end of this article, we’ll provide indicators of compromise recovered during the investigation. Ten days of dwell time from initial access to the destructive phase. Timeline reconstructed from incident evidence Why the hypervisor changes ransomware economics Virtualization enables cost-effective and efficient resource management, simplifies simultaneous updates and policy enforcement. However, at the same time it reduces the number of control points and enables broader impact in case of a cyberattack. In non-virtualized environments, a single guest compromise normally affects one operating system. Compromise of the virtualization management plane gives the attacker access to many hypervisor hosts, while direct impact on an ESXi datastore can render many guest systems unavailable at once. This makes the hypervisor layer an attractive target for ransomware operators, especially when domain controllers, application servers, databases, and backup workloads share the same virtualized estate. According to Kaspersky’s global cybersecurity services report , ransomware accounted for 39.4% of incidents GERT handled in 2025, and the data for the beginning of 2026 shows that it remains a major threat. Back in 2023, SentinelOne documented that multiple ransomware families are using ESXi lockers derived from the leaked Babuk source code, which lowered the development barrier for actors who otherwise lacked the necessary expertise to build ESXi-capable samples. This makes ransomware targeting virtualization environments more accessible to a broader range of threat actors, and this trend is still with us. The visibility asymmetry ESXi is a specialized hypervisor based on VMware’s proprietary VMkernel. It shares certain similarities with Linux (and other Unix-like systems), accepts Linux commands and runs certain Linux-like software, but it is different from general-purpose Linux distributions. Broadcom states that off-the-shelf security tools designed for general-purpose operating systems cannot run on ESXi without workarounds and that direct installation of third-party agents or antiviruses on ESXi and vCenter Server Appliance (VCSA) is not supported. Defenders therefore rely heavily on built-in hardening, management-plane telemetry, agentless security solution integrations, and telemetry collection from the infrastructure outside ESXi hosts. Guest operating systems are usually protected by EDR software, but if an ESXi-side process modifies a virtual machine disk (VMDK) or related datastore file, an EDR agent inside a guest cannot directly observe or attribute the hypervisor process performing the operation. Guest telemetry may record consequences, such as I/O failures or an abrupt shutdown, but the malicious execution occurs outside its boundary. In the engagement examined here, this made host-native and management-plane telemetry central to the incident timeline reconstruction. How attackers reach the virtualization layer There are different routes into the environment that require different controls, but can ultimately provide access to the centralized virtualization management plane and the ESXi hosts it controls. Recommendation for defenders Avoid unnecessary direct Active Directory dependency on ESXi hosts. Where business requirements demand it, stick to the principle of least privilege: add only those accounts to administrative groups that really need this level of access, use separate admin accounts with separate credentials for the domain and for hypervisors, restrict access to the ESXi and vCenter management interfaces to only those network segments, accounts and workstations that are necessary to control it, and continuously validate ESXi permission changes. vCenter role-based access control (RBAC) and identity federation should be configured for virtualization administration, while ordinary enterprise credentials and broad domain groups should not be used to access vCenter or ESXi with high privileges. Credential reuse from the Windows estate The third path requires no vulnerability. Administrative credentials present on domain controllers, jump servers, backup systems, identity infrastructure or administrator workstations may provide access to vCenter or ESXi if the same admin accounts are used across different infrastructure segments. MITRE ATT&CK treats the Valid Accounts technique as applicable to ESXi and specifically notes that compromised default or domain accounts can provide privileged access to other systems. In this incident , credentials obtained from non-virtualization systems inside the organization’s perimeter were used to reach the vCenter Server Appliances. Unlike vulnerability exploitation, successful authentication with a valid account may appear operationally normal. To reliably detect such activity, analysts must therefore evaluate the source host, account history, time, destination, subsequent administrative actions, and whether the authenticated account normally manages virtualization infrastructure. Backup infrastructure as a credential bridge Backup platforms are not passive storage. They commonly require service accounts, hypervisor credentials, repository access and broad network reach so they can protect and restore critical workloads. This makes the backup server a concentrated trust point. In this incident, the adversary extracted stored credentials after gaining administrative execution on backup infrastructure, creating a route toward other infrastructure tiers. Persistence on the management plane With the potential risk possibilities explained, let’s go back to our case study. The attackers managed to compromise via SSH the vCenter appliance managing virtualization disaster recovery where they established two layers of persistence. The first used MeshAgent, the endpoint component of the legitimate MeshCentral remote management platform. The second used Ligolo-ng as a reverse tunnel implemented as a systemd service. This activity maps to two ATT&CK techniques: Systemd Service (T1543.002) and Protocol Tunneling (T1572). The timeline was as follows. The attackers authenticated on the appliance as root via SSH. Forty-three seconds later, they fetched and deployed MeshAgent through a shell script. The SSH session lasted 2 minutes and 26 seconds in total. After it ended, the adversary didn’t use SSH to access the compromised appliance anymore, thus reducing the usefulness of monitoring focused only on interactive SSH sessions. Roughly four hours later, they returned through MeshAgent and installed the Ligolo-ng tunnel as a persistent systemd unit. Unit file /etc/systemd/system/vmware-vdma.service Service vmware-vdma Description "DNS, time, VM settings sync (v2.03.24766)" ExecStart /opt/vmware/bin/agent Restart always Strings in /opt/vmware/bin/agent: Ligolo-ng v0.8.2 api.cloudvflare[.]com:443 VCSA persistence artifact: /etc/systemd/system/vmware-vdma.service The unit uses a VMware-like prefix, places its binary under /opt/vmware/bin/ , describes plausible appliance housekeeping functions, and requests Restart=always . A defender should not treat any unrecognized VMware-looking service as malicious by default, because custom integrations may exist, but a service absent from the list of services the organization defines as trusted is a high-confidence anomaly requiring immediate validation. Beyond the incident in question, more advanced ESXi persistence techniques have also been documented. For example, Mandiant observed attackers using malicious vSphere Installation Bundles (VIBs) as a persistence mechanism on compromised ESXi hosts. This demonstrates that attackers with administrative access can establish persistent access at the hypervisor layer, including persistence that survives host restarts. However, this incident shows that common remote-management software and a systemd service can be sufficient when vCenter itself is trusted and poorly monitored. Turning vCenter into an offensive control plane One interesting technique observed in the incident was the use of vCenter Host Profiles. Host Profiles are intended to capture and enforce configuration across ESXi hosts. A Host Profile can be applied to separate hosts, clusters of hosts or all hosts associated with a profile. It also may contain subprofiles covering specific configuration aspects, e.g., authentication. Among other things, Host Profiles may contain configuration for Tech Support Mode (TSM) and TSM-SSH. In this incident, the adversary used Host Profiles to change TSM-SSH service state to enable SSH on target ESXi hosts. The evidence recorded the following sequence: Offset Source Observed event Primary evidence T+0 vCenter Host configuration read through Host Profile workflow vpxa.log T+10m18s vCenter Configuration change pushed to enable SSH vpxa.log T+10m51s vCenter / ESXi TSM-SSH service reached RUNNING state, indicating that SSH has been enabled on the host and it is accessible from a remote device vpxa.log + vobd.log Host Profile manipulation sequence observed before direct ESXi access Note that the 33-second interval between the configuration push and TSM-SSH reaching RUNNING state is short enough for us to be able to quickly correlate these events by comparing logs from different sources. Overall, it took the attacker eleven minutes after the initial configuration read to reconfigure the host from policy-disabled SSH to direct remote access support. Subsequently, direct SSH access to the target ESXi hosts originated from the vCenter appliance IP, a source that would normally be considered highly trusted on the management network. Compromise of the management plane converted a legitimate compliance mechanism into an access path to the managed hypervisor Why ESXi configuration change via vCenter is a trust-boundary failure Host hardening is important, but centrally administered controls should not be considered independent once the central management tool is compromised. If vCenter has authority to modify host state, an attacker controlling it may be able to make a malicious change look structurally similar to legitimate compliance activity: read configuration, apply a desired state, and observe the result. The distinguishing context becomes authorization, the timing, the source account, the change window, and whether the desired state itself weakens security. This is why vCenter should be treated as a Tier 0 asset and provided with the highest possible level of protection. Management-plane administrative accounts should be isolated from ordinary enterprise administration, network access should be restricted to dedicated management paths, high-risk configuration changes should require strong authentication and monitoring, and vCenter-to-ESXi traffic should be thoroughly compared to a normal behavior baseline (connection patterns, ports, protocols, source and destination systems) rather than implicitly trusted. All significant discrepancies should be treated as suspicious and investigated. High-fidelity detection opportunity An alert should be generated when Host Profile compliance (or remediation in VMware terms) changes TSM or TSM-SSH configuration outside an approved window, particularly when followed by a new SSH session from vCenter or another management appliance. This sequence is more precise than alerting on SSH alone because each step is legitimate in isolation but dangerous in combination. Notes on ESXi abuse: living-off-the-land tools and controls to monitor Once an attacker can administer ESXi, many destructive actions require no exploit. LOLESXi project catalogs native ESXi binaries and scripts that have been abused by threat actors, including administrative utilities that enumerate VMs, control power state and alter configuration. This mirrors living-off-the-land behavior on Windows, but the target is the hypervisor control plane. There are two ESXi hardening mechanisms, and changes to either can be viewed as highly suspicious. Host acceptance level controls which VIBs are allowed to install. The execInstalledOnly setting controls whether non-installed binaries may execute. Broadcom states that ESXi 8 enables the execInstalledOnly runtime option by default and warns that disabling it allows unknown non-installed binaries to execute, increasing ransomware risk. There is also a kernel enforcement setting with the same name, which is related to the runtime option, but distinct. Changes to that may require a reboot. Payload delivery and FlowSsh behavior The payload was delivered to ESXi via SFTP. The auth.log authentication log file exposed a client identifier associated with FlowSsh, Bitvise’s SSH client library for C, C++ and .NET. FlowSsh supports SFTP, remote execution requests, interactive shell sessions and tunneling. Those capabilities fit the observed delivery model, but the library identification should rest primarily on the recorded client identifier rather than on behavioral inference alone. After the payload was delivered, the following series of commands was executed. We reconstructed this sequence primarily from the shell.log file. Offset Observed command Analytical note T+0 true No-op probe in a dedicated session. T+1s chmod +x /tmp/esx64 Separate execution session. T+1m48s true Second no-op probe. T+1m49s ./tmp/esx64 /selftest Separate execution session; payload validation stage. Each command pair was preceded by a session executing only true . This repeating pattern is consistent with automated FlowSsh channel setup, but in isolation, it can’t be considered a reliable indicator of the tool in use. Human operators could have typed true as well, and other automation frameworks could have emitted reachability probes. The sequence should therefore be hunted as a behavioral fingerprint, not treated as a unique signature for FlowSsh. Its value increases when combined with an unusual SSH client identifier, SFTP staging to /tmp , chmod , and a subsequent self-test or payload execution. The three-hour self-test window The /selftest execution ran for approximately 16 seconds and occurred roughly three hours before final detonation. A pre-impact test run may suggest that the operator was validating compatibility with the target ESXi build before proceeding to destructive execution. Pre-impact detection opportunity Treat unexpected executable staging under /tmp , followed by execution and subsequent access to /vmfs , as a critical pre-impact sequence. While individual indicators such as chmod +x may be ambiguous in isolation, unexpected execution of a newly staged executable from /tmp can itself be a strong indicator of malicious activity. The complete sequence provides stronger contextual evidence by linking payload staging, execution, validation, and subsequent access to the VM storage layer. Partial overwrite and what 2 KB actually implies Incident artifacts indicated that approximately the first 2 KB of virtual disk files were overwritten. Damage to the beginning of a disk can destroy the protective MBR, primary GPT header, and other early boot or filesystem structures, which can immediately make a guest unbootable. However, the UEFI specification requires GPT media to keep both primary and backup GPT headers and partition-entry arrays in different locations. If the primary GPT is corrupt and the backup is valid, GPT-aware software should use the backup, which may help restore the primary. Therefore, an overwrite of the first 2 KB does not by itself mean that the partition metadata is unrecoverable. Practical recovery depends on whether additional boot records, filesystem metadata, volume structures, descriptors, or application data were also modified, and on the exact virtual-disk layout. Destroying the recovery path first In this incident, backup infrastructure was not collateral damage. The attacker disabled antivirus protection on the backup environment days before the main attack, and later stopped backup services, encrypted the recovery infrastructure, and disrupted administrative access — all before ESXi detonation. The order of operations reduced the defenders’ ability to restore production when they needed it most. Recovery capabilities were disrupted before the final ESXi impact. The relative timings are based on the incident sequence This changes the definition of a resilient backup architecture. Storage immutability is valuable, but it does not protect the identities, management servers, repositories, network paths, credentials, or procedures required to perform system restoration. Recovery should be designed as a separate security boundary with independent administrative identities, restricted management paths, offline or logically isolated recovery options, tested restore procedures, and monitoring that does not depend on the production control plane. Two recent vulnerabilities in Veeam are a perfect illustration of this. Although neither of these vulnerabilities was seen exploited in this case, they take advantage of weak privilege management policy, which may make them exploitable by any domain user, including those with the lowest privileges. CVE-2024-40711 is listed in CISA’s KEV database as a vulnerability exploited by ransomware. CVE-2025-23120 is more recent and still absent from KEV, but it is worth noting that according to Veeam, it allows RCE, but only affects domain-joined backup servers. The general lesson is not vendor-specific: a recovery system that depends on the same identity plane as production infrastructure can be compromised and used to achieve the attackers’ goals. Detection and investigation There are a number of log files that document ESXi events. These sources can reconstruct a destructive hypervisor incident even when no endpoint agent exists on ESXi. The limitation is retention. Broadcom states that ESXi logs internally to a ramdisk, which has limited space and gets cleared at reboot, and that persistent scratch or remote syslog is required if logs must survive reboot and local storage loss. We recommend collecting logs to remote storage to maintain forensic visibility into ESXi incidents. Incident response at the virtualization layer This section contains recommended actions when responding to an incident involving ESXi ransomware. First 30 minutes after incident discovery Isolate vCenter management access from ordinary enterprise segments while preserving investigator access through a controlled path. Export vCenter Events and Tasks, SSO audit records, active sessions, and the current Host Profile definitions before remediation changes erase context. Collect ESXi auth.log, shell.log, vpxa.log, vobd.log, hostd.log, vmkernel.log, vmkwarning.log, vmksummary.log, esxupdate.log, and hostprofiletrace.log from each affected host. Preserve VCSA /etc/systemd/system , /opt/vmware paths referenced by previously unknown or unapproved units, shell history, wtmp, authentication logs, and copies or hashes of suspicious binaries. Capture backup-server telemetry, service state, credential-store evidence, and network logs before rebuilding or rotating infrastructure credentials. Record current execInstalledOnly state, host acceptance level, SSH/ESXi Shell state, Lockdown Mode, domain membership, and ESXi permission mappings. Do not begin by rebooting ESXi merely to remove the attacker session. ESXi logging persistence depends on scratch and syslog configuration, and a reboot may destroy ramdisk-resident evidence. Confirm that critical logs have been copied or remotely retained before containing disruptive activity unless ongoing impact makes immediate shutdown unavoidable. Containment priorities Revoke or rotate compromised administrative credentials and remove attacker-controlled access paths, but preserve authentication and task records first. Disable unapproved TSM-SSH and ESXi Shell access after collecting evidence; make sure that Host Profiles or other automation will not immediately re-enable them. Remove unauthorized systemd services and remote-management agents from VCSA only after imaging or preserving the unit and referenced binaries. Restore execInstalledOnly and acceptance-level settings to the approved baseline after determining that doing so will not disrupt evidence collection or critical recovery tooling. Segment or disconnect compromised backup infrastructure from production and establish a clean recovery control plane before attempting large-scale restoration. Architecture hardening derived from the case Treat vCenter as Tier 0 VMware vCenter is not merely an inventory console. It can hold the authority to configure hosts, manage identities, power VMs, apply Host Profiles, and mediate access across the virtual estate. The incident in question shows why compromise of vCenter can undermine controls enforced through the virtualization management plane, providing an attacker with sufficient privileges to configure hosts, control VMs, and modify security-relevant settings across the virtual estate, potentially enabling the entire virtualized environment to be disrupted or destroyed within minutes. Protect vCenter with dedicated administrative identities, phishing-resistant MFA through supported identity federation, restricted management-network access, monitored privileged sessions, and close monitoring of Host Profile and cluster-level change operations. Separate virtualization identities from ordinary enterprise administration Avoid broad reuse of domain administrator or workstation admin credentials for virtualization. Where ESXi is domain-joined, apply the secure Active Directory configuration following Broadcom’s recommendations and continuously audit group mappings and permissions. Note that the Valid Accounts attacker technique is applicable to ESXi environments. Moreover, as an example of the Default Accounts subtechnique, MITRE specifically mentions the vpxuser account used by vCenter for host management. Keep direct ESXi management paths narrow SSH is useful for troubleshooting but should be treated as an exceptional administrative path rather than routine day-to-day access. Unauthorized SSH enablement on ESXi hosts both directly and through vCenter should be detected by monitoring TSM-SSH changes and treated as a suspicious event. To further harden ESXi hosts, use Lockdown Mode where operationally suitable, providing access only to a small set of explicitly authorized users, apply management-network filtering, and an approval and tracking policy for any changes, especially for enabling shell access. Make the logging path independent of the affected host Remote syslog from ESXi and vCenter should be stored on infrastructure outside the virtualization trust domain. Persistent scratch improves log survivability after reboot, but local evidence can still be lost if the data storage or host is destroyed. It is recommended to use centralized logging (e.g., SIEM logging), which should include authentication, shell, host management, Host Profile, and vCenter task telemetry. Proper logging should be configured before any incident happens, so the full picture is available to the response team in case of an emergency. Design backup as a separate security boundary Use independent administrative identities for backup, minimize domain dependencies, isolate repositories and management interfaces, protect immutable copies from production credentials, and test recovery under the assumption that vCenter and production AD are unavailable. A backup design should be judged not only by whether data is immutable, but by whether a clean operator can authenticate, reach the repository, validate the data, and restore workloads after the production control plane is compromised. Resilience improves when enterprise identity, virtualization administration and recovery authority are separated rather than chained through shared privileged credentials Conclusion The central lesson from the incident in question is not that ESXi needs another endpoint agent. It is that virtualization security depends on the integrity of the management plane, the identity plane, and the recovery plane at the same time. A hypervisor can remain fully patched and protected and still become the point of maximum ransomware impact when the system authorized to manage it has already been compromised. The adversary did not need to break VMware’s cryptography or escape from a guest. Once they accessed a privileged vCenter control, a legitimate compliance mechanism could be used to change host state, enable SSH, and create a path into ESXi that looked structurally similar to normal administration activities. The case also shows why destructive ransomware incidents are often won or lost before encryption begins. The attacker established alternate persistence, validated the ESXi payload hours before detonation, disrupted backup capability first, and then executed disruptive activities from the layer least visible to guest controls. Each stage created a detection opportunity, but those opportunities existed in management, virtualization and recovery telemetry rather than in a conventional endpoint process tree. The durable defense is therefore architectural. Treat vCenter as Tier 0. Separate virtualization identities from ordinary enterprise administration. Keep direct ESXi access narrow. Preserve native logs outside the affected platform. Detect high-risk configuration changes as sequences rather than isolated commands. Finally, design recovery so that compromise of production identity or virtualization management does not also provide the authority needed to destroy the systems required to restore them.
