Parasitizing trust: How legitimate infrastructure becomes a delivery pipeline for attackers
Cisco Talos | Research / advisory | 2026-10-21T10:00:00+00:00
Legitimate infrastructure has become a powerful delivery mechanism for attackers, carrying the inherent trust they once had to build. As Cisco Talos explored in our previous research on weaponizing software-as-a-service (SaaS) notification pipelines , these platforms are increasingly repurposed as proxies for malicious intent. Talos has since tracked a significant acceleration in this trend, observing abuse across more than 25 distinct legitimate platforms in the past year. The abused infrastructure included collaboration, productivity, file-sharing, survey, payment and AI-related platforms. This demonstrates that attackers are not limiting themselves to traditional phishing infrastructure or lookalike domains. Instead, they are systematically subverting the very tools businesses trust. Order notification abuse Talos recently analyzed two order notification campaigns abusing Staples and Dell transactional email pipelines. Together, these campaigns accounted for roughly 1,400 messages observed by Talos. The malicious element was not the outer sender identity, like a simple forged sender or lookalike domain. It was the content of the legitimate order notification workflow rendered. The suspicious content appeared inside the notification itself, in fields that would normally contain shipping information. The attacker’s objective was to use a trusted retail pipeline to create urgency around a high-value purchase and push the recipient toward an attacker-controlled phone number. In the first campaign, the message appeared as a Staples order confirmation from "support@orders.staples.com” with the subject line, “Here’s your Staples order #9939818875. Thanks for shopping with us.” The body displayed a plausible purchase for an Apple iPad, including order number, delivery timing, pricing, billing information and standard Staples footer content. The suspicious element was embedded in the order details: “Helpline 806 283 7712,” “Order Confirmed,” and the Staples corporate address appeared in fields normally associated with shipping or billing information. Figure 1. Staples order notification The structure of the message matters. The attacker was not trying to persuade the user to click a crude phishing link. Instead, the message created the impression that an expensive order had already been placed and that the fastest way to resolve it was to call the embedded “helpline.” The desired user action was a phone call. Once the victim moved from email to voice, the attacker could continue the scam through social engineering, payment manipulation, credential theft, or remote assistance fraud. The headers reinforced the trust problem. The Staples message showed DKIM alignment for “orders.staples.com” and DMARC passed for the visible Staples domain. The message also passed through forwarding and re-encapsulation paths, including Proton Mail, Microsoft 365, and downstream enterprise mail infrastructure. These hops complicate provenance analysis, but they do not change the core issue: Authentication helped establish that the outer message was credible, while offering no assurance that the phone number or transaction context was legitimate. The second campaign used the same pattern in a Dell order received notification. The message was sent from “Dell | Order Received <received@order.dellcom>” with the subject line, “We have your order | Dell Purchase ID: 2009829958247.” The body presented a purchase for Microsoft Project Standard 2024, totaling $736.09. As with the Staples example, the suspicious content appeared in transaction fields. The “Ship To” and “Bill To” sections included “Confirmed Order,” “Helpline 808 232 6397,” and “Call Our Sales Team”. The Dell message used the same psychological lever: an unexpected high-value purchase, a legitimate-looking order template, and a phone number positioned as the path to resolution. The attacker did not need the recipient to trust a new domain or unfamiliar brand. The message borrowed credibility from Dell’s order pipeline and redirected the recipient toward attacker-controlled contact information. Authentication results showed DKIM passing for Dell-related infrastructure and DMARC passing for “order.dell.com”. As with the Staples sample, forwarding artifacts and SRS-style return paths appeared as the message moved through Microsoft and enterprise mail infrastructure. But the malicious intent did not live in an obviously forged sender. It lived inside the rendered order content. Figure 2. Dell order notification These campaigns illustrate the same trust-boundary failure described in our earlier research on platform-as-a-proxy (PaaP) abuse. In those cases, attackers placed malicious content into SaaS fields that trusted platforms later rendered into notifications. Here, the same concept appears in commerce infrastructure, in the retailer’s order pipeline. The attacker supplies the intent, while the trusted system performs the delivery. From a defensive perspective, sender authentication is necessary, but it is not sufficient. SPF, DKIM, and DMARC help answer whether a message came from expected infrastructure. However, they do not answer whether the transaction was legitimate, whether the rendered fields make business sense, or whether the user is being pushed toward an attacker-controlled action. Order notifications should be treated as sensitive content. Any field reflected into a transactional message — shipping name, billing name, address line, company name, order note, support field, or fulfillment instruction — can become a delivery vehicle if it is not validated before rendering. Retailers and security teams should correlate order notification content with account history, payment state, shipping context, and known support channels. Transactional templates should reject or flag unexpected phone numbers, URLs, urgency language, and support instructions when they appear in fields where they do not belong. In both campaigns, the wrapper was legitimate enough to inherit trust. The payload was attacker-influenced. That distinction is the center of this attack class. The abuse of commerce pipelines is merely one manifestation of a broader, systemic vulnerability. Whether the attacker initiates a fake purchase or a fraudulent account sign-up, the root cause remains the same: Platforms are implicitly trusting data that enters their systems from untrusted sources, only to render that data into high-reputation, automated notifications. This same mechanism of reflection — where input is echoed back into an email to a third party — is the engine behind an evolution in list bombing, moving the attack from simple denial-of-service to a sophisticated vehicle for content delivery. List bombing: From inbox disruption to content delivery List bombing is an abuse technique in which attackers submit a victim’s email address to numerous legitimate registration, subscription or notification workflows, flooding the inbox with automated messages. While this vector is traditionally viewed as a denial-of-service or distraction tactic designed to bury critical alerts in a flood of junk, Talos has observed a subset of cases where this behavior is being repurposed as a delivery vehicle for malicious content. This abuse occurs when an attacker identifies public-facing registration, profile, or project-naming forms that lack sufficient input validation. By inputting a "hostile string" — such as a cryptocurrency-themed lure — into fields intended for a name, company, or title, the attacker forces the platform to treat this data as legitimate user information. When the service reflexively incorporates this input into its automated welcome, verification, or notification templates, the system acts as an unintended carrier, delivering the attacker’s content inside a high-reputation, system-generated email that can pass standard authentication checks. The attacker’s objective in these cases is to bypass the security perimeter by laundering their malicious content through the brand reputation of a trusted service provider. While this is not yet a broad industry shift, it represents a calculated abuse of a platform's trust boundary. By embedding their lure in an automated notification, the adversary ensures that the message arrives with the appearance of administrative legitimacy, significantly increasing the likelihood that the recipient will trust the content. The goal is to reach the target's inbox with enough platform authority that the message may be evaluated as routine notification traffic rather than attacker-controlled content. Figure 3. Registration field reflection Beyond the risk to the individual recipient, this vector creates a significant brand liability. When a service provider’s infrastructure is used to broadcast fraudulent content, the resulting user complaints and bounce-backs can degrade the platform's own sender reputation, potentially triggering blocklisting that impacts their legitimate business communications. Bounce abuse: When delivery reports carry the lure The final vector moves the abuse pattern into the mail system itself. Instead of relying on a retailer, SaaS platform, or registration workflow to generate the message, the attacker attempts to make a delivery status workflow carry attacker-controlled content. Bounces, formally known as delivery status notifications (DSNs) or non-delivery reports (NDRs), are generated when a message cannot be delivered. Under normal conditions, a mail system sends the delivery report back to the original SMTP envelope sender. Depending on the implementation, that report may include the original headers, portions of the message body, URLs, attachments, or an encapsulated copy of the failed message. That returned content creates the abuse opportunity. In one example Talos observed, a mail delivery system notification carried a WeTransfer-themed file-sharing lure. The administrative wrapper made the message look like routine mail-system noise, while the nested content asked the recipient to engage with what appeared to be a file transfer workflow. The attacker’s goal was to make the phishing lure feel less like an unsolicited external message and more like part of a technical delivery failure that required review. Users are conditioned to view system errors as benign and are far more likely to engage with an "administrative" fix than a suspicious, standalone phishing email. Figure 4. Bounce carrying a file-sharing lure There are several ways this kind of message can reach a victim. In opportunistic backscatter, an attacker sends mail using the victim’s address as the forged envelope sender. If an intermediate system accepts the message and later fails to deliver it, the resulting DSN may be sent back to the forged sender. If the DSN includes enough of the original message, the bounce itself can carry the lure. A second possibility is intentional bounce abuse, where the attacker uses an authenticated, compromised, or otherwise controllable sending context to trigger a delivery failure that returns attacker-influenced content. This differs from an outright forged NDR, where the attacker simply fabricates the bounce message and sends it directly. From the recipient’s mailbox, however, these cases can look similar: A delivery-failure wrapper contains content the attacker wants the user to trust. This distinction matters for defenders. A DSN’s administrative appearance does not validate the content inside it. The outer message may be a legitimate delivery report, while the returned or encapsulated content may still contain a phishing link, credential harvesting page, malicious attachment, or social engineering prompt. The content-trust gap: A defensive imperative Across these vectors, the defensive failure is consistent: Trusted infrastructure is being treated as a shortcut for trusted content. Sender authentication remains essential. SPF, DKIM, DMARC, reputation, and infrastructure analysis help determine whether a message came from an expected source. But these controls do not answer a separate and increasingly important question: What was that trusted system instructed to carry? Order notifications, registration workflows and delivery reports all create opportunities for attacker-influenced content to appear inside messages that users and security tools are conditioned to trust. In the Staples and Dell campaigns, the suspicious content was a phone number placed into transactional fields. In list bombing and registration abuse, the lure can appear inside reflected account or profile data. In bounce abuse, the malicious content may sit inside returned message data or a nested MIME object. Defending against this requires content-aware validation at multiple points in the delivery chain. Platform owners should treat any user-controlled value rendered into an email template as untrusted data. Fields used for names, addresses, company names, project titles, order notes or support instructions should be validated before storage and again before rendering. Phone numbers, URLs, markup, line breaks, and urgent support language should be rejected or flagged when they appear in fields where they do not belong. Security gateways should inspect the fully rendered message, not only the sender or template. That includes HTML, text, URLs, redirects, attachments, personalization fields, and nested MIME content. For transactional email, defenders should also evaluate whether the message content aligns with the workflow that produced it. An order notification containing unexpected support numbers, cancellation instructions, or high-urgency language should be treated differently from a routine receipt. For delivery reports, recursive MIME inspection is especially important. Gateways should parse DSN containers, extract returned messages or message fragments, and apply normal phishing, malware, and URL analysis to the nested content. A bounce should explain a delivery failure, not provide a blind path for untrusted payloads. The broader lesson is that attackers are no longer only exploiting technical vulnerabilities in infrastructure. They are exploiting the assumptions built into business processes. When legitimate systems render attacker-influenced data into trusted notifications, the result can pass authentication checks and still deliver malicious intent. Defenders should therefore treat sender trust and content trust as separate but complementary signals. The wrapper can explain where a message came from. It cannot prove that the payload is safe. There's only so much code: the rise in vulnerability numbers
Cisco Talos | Research / advisory | 2026-10-20T15:31:22+00:00
Frontier AI models (like Mythos) are rapidly mining decades of technical debt across ubiquitous legacy libraries, driving monthly Microsoft Patch Tuesday vulnerability advisories up more than tenfold (reaching 1,415 this month). This volume is overwhelming open-source maintainers and putting pressure on the traditional 90-day coordinated disclosure model. Despite a 105% year-over-year surge in CVEs, Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEVs) grew by only 37%, and only 3 of September's 975 Microsoft advisories saw in-the-wild exploitation, demonstrating that the vast majority of AI-discovered bugs are not practically exploited. We may be able to take new-bug-class vulnerability spikes as evidence that these vulnerabilities will eventually level off and decline, once the historical code backlog is remediated and AI verification is embedded directly into new development pipelines. With attackers able to parse patches faster via AI, defenders cannot cover for all possible threats. Organizations should continue to build robust defense-in-depth. Prioritize high-exploitability flaws in their specific environments, implement interim mitigations (such as LiveProtect), deploy behavioral anomaly detection, and integrate current AI evaluation models into their software build chains. The September Microsoft Tuesday release included 975 vulnerability advisories. This month, 1,415 vulnerabilities were just dropped. Compared to a pre-June average of 100 a month, that increase is staggering. Where did they come from? As of April, frontier models came onto the scene more fine-tuned than ever toward vulnerability detection, particularly with the introduction of Mythos. The deluge brings a lot of questions: If these numbers are real, why hasn’t the internet collapsed? Are these vulnerabilities a new breed, and if not, why didn’t analysts find them in code that is sometimes decades old? Is the coordinated disclosure model still relevant in an era of this kind of speed? How in the world do defenders deal with those numbers? Should we go home, kiss our spouses, and prepare for the end? The answer to many of these questions boils down to this: The majority of these vulnerabilities are coming from repositories of old code from large-profile products and systems; and disclosure does not equal weaponization. Where is it from? AI is chewing through a backlog of technical debt in libraries and tools used as the dependable, best-practice lynchpins of so many environments. These models have a new perspective, an ability to "think" without some of the hangups or assumptions of human defenders, and they are thorough in listing those vulnerabilities. Entrenched older tools have always been prime targets because of their predictability; attackers know those tools and all their secrets, and they know what is likely to be present in a network with them. Multi-exploit attacks can be deployed with little variability. Pre-Mythos threat actors exploited log4j ( CVE-2021-44228 ) to immense success. The library was ubiquitous, included in nearly every environment, but not a component that got cataloged in visibility scans. A combination of scale and low visibility meant defenders were blindsided, scrambling to find out if they even used the log4j library before starting to concoct a mitigation plan. Talos has ongoing relationships with some of the largest technical vendors (such as the Microsoft Active Protections Process ( MAPP ) agreement). Organizations share additional information—detection guidance or maybe a POC — for vulnerabilities that are most likely to be exploited, to enable Talos to provide adequate coverage when the advisories are announced. "Microsoft believes it is important for us to provide detection content, based on their guidance," as Yves Younan, Director of Talos Vulnerability Discovery, Detection, and Testing, says. Talos has needed to invest more resources to get coverage in on time for critical vendors like Microsoft to keep up with notifications coming in fast and often on staggered or out-of-band timelines. For larger vendors, the pressure is handleable for now. However, open-source projects are getting flooded. Some projects have a trusted group that can combine to tackle patches, and some share with the full community for immediate assistance, but it is up to the project. The internet runs on open-source work. The pace of discovery presents a challenge for the traditional coordinated disclosure system vulnerability hunters have been using for years, and some are calling it into question. Security vulnerabilities should be reported, to enable an informed defender base and regular patching, and vendors responsible for vulnerable code need time to create coverage. A timed disclosure model where notifications are sent to a vendor, who then has a set period of time (typically 90 days) to provide coverage before the disclosure is released publicly, has created a more secure online environment for decades. Now, many practitioners are releasing advisories publicly without a disclosure period beforehand. The reasoning is that if one AI can find a weakness, any other could, so the vulnerability is already, effectively, released. This conversation is actively changing expectations around vulnerability disclosure, and guidance is in flux. Will it stay this high? No one knows the future, but we can always look to the past for insight. Numbers of vulnerabilities jump drastically when a new bug class is identified, as their possibilities are explored to the fullest. Then the discoveries plateau and drop off. When format string vulnerabilities were discovered as a new bug class in 2000, the initial influx of discoveries ramped up and up, until reaching a peak in 2005 and then slowly declining over the next couple of years. Format string vulnerabilities are still discovered, but they are a smaller overall fraction of the number of CVEs discovered in a year. Theoretically, AI-assisted vulnerability discovery could go the same way. While frontier models have not discovered a new vulnerability class, they are more efficient at discovering known types of software vulnerabilities. Given that a lot of vital software has a long history of code releases, there’s plenty of code to mine for weaknesses. But there’s only so much code… right? Old and commonly used tools are often under the highest scrutiny, lending them one kind of safety: Because of their popularity, utility, and stability, when popular tools have vulnerabilities, they are discovered fast and patched promptly. As the historical backlog of essential systems is turned over by AI, vulnerabilities will be found and patched. New code will ideally be run through AI bug-checkers that will prevent new vulnerabilities from being introduced. The rate of new vulns should level off and drop accordingly. There are some caveats. In the past, as vulnerability disclosures increased through coordinated disclosures, we saw increased adoption of mitigations. Exploitation became significantly harder. Attackers often need to chain together 3 or more vulnerabilities to successfully exploit a system. However, more awareness of vulnerabilities often leads to more exploitation. Attackers often use vendor patches meant to protect customers to discover where vulnerabilities might exist. The frequency of patching has delayed human attackers via parsing bottlenecks, but AI can eliminate that problem. Are the numbers the real issue? The volume is certainly up. Fresh as this blog is being finished, the October Patch Tuesday dropped almost 500 more than last month. With refinement, any vulnerability could become a link to a path of effect. But... not all vulnerabilities are exploitable. Some are wildly impractical for attacker use. In fact, the percent of Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEVs) is vastly smaller than the found vulnerabilities. Of the 975 vulnerabilities Microsoft reported in September, there were only three in-the-wild exploits detected. When viewed by percentage, vulnerabilities versus KEVs tell a different and perhaps reassuring story. Compared with the same period last year, additions to the known-exploited vulnerability list increased by 37%, while published CVEs increased by 105%. However, we wouldn't necessarily expect those figures to grow in proportion. Adversaries only need enough useful vulnerabilities to compromise their targets, and the same vulnerabilities can be reused against many organizations. The KEV count tracks distinct vulnerabilities known to be exploited, not the number of attacks or successful compromises. AI helps ingest code, advisory reports, and attack surfaces with inhuman speed, but it cannot currently make judgment calls about the value of a weakness. For defenders, the question remains the same: Which vulnerabilities provide a viable path into their environment, and how do you close those paths? What do we do now? The undeniable danger here is in exploitation time going down rapidly, while the vulnerabilities that could become exploits is still going up. The biggest hurdle for a defender is determining what is a real vulnerability for their environment, and what is not. Different defenders have different forms of control depending on their role. For those who are writing code, the new standard validation chain should include AI evaluation by models at least as up to date as those being used to hunt for vulnerabilities. It is relatively cheap to run new code through AI, but very expensive to run a cumbersome backlog of old code through. Bite it off in chunks. For those whose role involves keeping the tools ready: Upgrade ASAP. Don't stop there. For Cisco customers, implement LiveProtect to mitigate vulnerabilities between advisory release day and patching, to give time for that patching. Do this for everything you own. Move beyond static domain blocking and implement behavioral detection that alerts on anomalous traffic patterns directed toward automation platforms. Hone your focus to the vulnerabilities with the highest exploitability in your environment. KEVs are far less common among the sea of advisories, and their numbers have not risen proportionally with the AI-assisted deluge. Focus on building defense-in-depth. Employ multiple protection systems, to cover for delays in patching. Implement interim mitigations (such as LiveProtect), create custom IPS/Snort triggers to block pre-patched traffic using SnortML, deploy behavioral anomaly detection, and integrate current AI evaluation models in software build chains. Operation FlashHole: Lazarus breaks security, defies control
Securelist | Research / advisory | 2026-10-21T02:07:29+00:00
In January, we responded to the Lazarus Group’s novel attack campaign against South Korea, which followed last year’s Operation SyncHole . In this new operation, the group employed an updated malware chain that included both their old and new malware, which we disclosed in full, and used another mandatory South Korean security software program as a part of infection chain using a watering‑hole strategy. Notably, the group targeted victims more selectively, sending spear‑phishing emails with links to fabricated but relevant sites, such as portfolio and résumé pages tailored to each target. We dubbed this activity Operation FlashHole. Operation FlashHole attack flow during initial compromise Our examination reveals that the earliest evidence of this new operation dates back to July 2025. This suggests the group began preparing for the campaign shortly after ending Operation SyncHole, which lasted at least four months. We identified a minimum of three organizations impacted by the campaign in South Korea’s software, construction, and wired communication equipment manufacturing sectors. These sectors differ from those affected by Operation SyncHole, indicating that the group has expanded its target scope over time. Background After more than a year since the first observation of Operation SyncHole, we detected a suspicious execution chain originating from legitimate South Korean software. This chain mirrors the one from the previous operation; however, this time, the attacker abused CrossWeb EX instead of Cross EX, which was abused in Operation SyncHole. Both applications appear to be maintained by the same vendor, and both are related to financial security. CrossWeb EX is a financial security solution that is required to access widely used online banking and government websites. It encrypts communications between the user’s browser and the web server using public key infrastructure (PKI). This is not the first time Lazarus has abused this software. It was also used in 2021 to deploy the Racket downloader and again in 2022 and 2023 . Although the exact method by which CrossWeb EX was abused remains unclear, we determined that the attack chain ultimately leads to the execution of the malware in memory. We noted that the spawned process was running with a high integrity level. The parent process, INISAFECrossWebEXSvc.exe , originates from the installation bundle of INISAFE CrossWeb EX V3 version 3.4.1.2, released in 2023 after KrCERT’s public advisory . As we already mentioned in our SyncHole blog post, this pattern closely mirrors the long‑standing strategy employed by the Lazarus Group to target South Korea. Since CrossWeb EX and similar software is mandatory for South Korean users and typically runs in the background to interact with the browser, it has been leveraged as a critical attack vector by the group. In 2025, a paper was presented at the USENIX Security Symposium that investigated vulnerabilities in South Korea’s mandatory security software and demonstrated how such software can unintentionally create a massive attack surface. In July 2026, the South Korean government and several local security vendors also published a joint security advisory covering typical Lazarus attack techniques, including exploitation of financial security software. While this advisory overlaps with our findings from Operation FlashHole and Operation SyncHole, and AhnLab’s detailed analysis report Operation Double Barrel covers a wide range of recent Lazarus Group activities, we are conducting a more granular classification based on their TTPs. We will discuss this further in the attribution section. Initial infection vector Although we could not precisely determine what triggered the infection chain, our analysis uncovered a suspicious website hosted on GitHub Pages that is associated with the GitHub account used as the C2 channel. The GitHub account was created last September, and the initial version of the site was designed to showcase the résumé of a senior network appliance developer. In January, however, this site was replaced with another featuring an interior design showcase. The first site was likely accessed by an employee of a wired communication manufacturing company, while the second site likely affected a target in the construction sector. Appearance of the malicious websites The first site with the résumé had a photograph supposedly of a job seeker, but the lack of EXIF metadata and the unnatural texture of the image indicate that it was generated by AI. This suggests the Lazarus Group has incorporated AI into its long‑standing strategy, like other Korean‑speaking threat actors ( 1 , 2 ). The group most likely also used AI to create the lure site: the job seeker’s name resembles the Korean version of “John Doe,” and the portfolio was populated with seemingly meaningless details and random companies. The generated site was then manually tailored to specific targets. Suspected use of an AI-generated image on the résumé site The websites contained embedded links that loaded an external JavaScript file via a script tag. Malicious link embedded in the résumé site: hxxps://javadevhouse.co[.]kr/ajax/libs/jquery/1.7.3/jquery.min.js Malicious link embedded in interior design site: hxxps://pythonapischool[.]com/api/detach Based on the analysis, we assess with medium-to-high confidence that the targets received phishing emails containing links to the malicious site hosted on GitHub Pages (using the domain <username>.github.io). The research results showed a high possibility that visiting the malicious site initiated an infection process involving CrossWeb EX software. This assessment aligns with Lazarus’s recent approach, as detailed in the joint advisory published by the South Korean government and security companies. Full infection chain Following the initial compromise, the legitimate Windows binary fsquirt.exe was executed under the CrossWeb EX process and injected with shellcode containing a malicious DLL – TechHub malware. From our extensive and thorough examination, we identified several malicious payloads and reconstructed the complete infection chain. The infection chain is rather complex and reveals the Lazarus Group’s advanced operational techniques on targeted hosts. These techniques conceal their malicious payloads, intent and their sophisticated malware delivery framework. The latter was updated extensively compared to the previous campaign, despite the new operation starting less than a year later. Significantly, the execution chain below exists only in memory during the initial infiltration, highlighting the attackers’ considerable efforts to hide the initial compromise. Initial infection chain Following the initial compromise, the group sought to maintain persistence on the network by installing a pair of malware strains – TouchHub and TouchShift – leveraging DLL side‑loading under the name mscoree.dll . TouchHub was deployed by the first instance of TechHub. It then used DLL side-loading to launch TouchShift, which led to the in‑memory execution of another TechHub instance. After launching the second time, TechHub deployed another pair, ClientHub and LPEClient, rather than executing Cubridge directly. Only minimal traces of these files were found on disk; the remainder of the malicious code persisted exclusively in memory. Expanded infection chain after gaining persistence Since Cubridge remains only in memory at the initial compromise stage, we suspect the group designed this indirect, multistage chain to deliberately redeploy Cubridge after gaining persistence, as it serves as the most sophisticated orchestrator of the attack. Malware set The full chain comprises advanced variants of the known Lazarus malware strains, including TechHub, Cubridge, TouchShift, LPEClient, and the newly identified TouchHub and ClientHub. TechHub – the start of the chain TechHub, a downloader that plays a central role in this attack, communicates with GitHub using a Personal Access Token (PAT). It serves as the initial payload, providing the top of the chain with sufficient functionality to profile compromised hosts and facilitate subsequent payloads. This variant is a more advanced version of the malware named CLOUDBURST by Mandiant and NickelLoader by ESET. According to their reports, the earlier version communicated with a C2 server via HTTP(S) to fetch additional payloads. The latest iteration we found, however, communicates with GitHub instead and has updated command strings. We identified three variants of TechHub in the chain: an in‑memory version, a simple DLL side‑loading version, and a DLL side‑loading version that uses a registered Windows service. Depending on which stage loads the malware, it contacts a private GitHub repository via the GitHub API using either hard‑coded credentials or credentials retrieved from its configuration file. When TechHub loads, it creates a 4‑byte hexadecimal identifier (ID) in the range 0x1000–0xFFFF or reads one from the configuration file, enabling the attackers to distinguish between compromised hosts. TechHub then checks a GitHub issue by sending the request GET /issues?state=[open/closed] to determine whether any previous communication remains active and removes any traces of it if found. It then proceeds with the handshaking process. It creates a new issue titled 1[ID] with the body “hel” using the POST /issues request, and sets its status to open. It then retrieves a file from the repository containing the 2[ID] value in its name. This file contains the RSA public key. It generates a random 16‑byte XOR key, encrypts it with the provided RSA public key, and uploads the result, prefixed with 1[ID] , to the repository. The hard‑coded IV is XORed with the generated 16‑byte key and then used after the handshake. During file exchange, filenames follow the pattern [1|2][ID][random-16-byte-key] throughout communication. The “2” prefix indicates delivery from the repository to TechHub, while “1” indicates delivery from TechHub to the repository. The random 16‑byte key in the filename is used for AES encryption or decryption of each file’s contents, employing either a hard‑coded IV or an updated IV. After the key exchange is complete, TechHub reads a file with the prefix “2” once again and verifies that the delivered data equals “hel”. LPEClient LPEClient is the only malware that was also observed in Operation SyncHole, indicating that the actor relies heavily on it for sophisticated victim profiling and payload delivery across multiple operations. LPEClient first appeared in 2019, and was discovered in 2020 . Since then, the tool has been widely observed in the Dream Job and Cascade Hole campaigns that targeted South Korea. Recent research conducted by Check Point revealed that LPEClient was used to deploy the FudModule malware using a zero-day. LPEClient has evolved continuously over time, but we discovered that it recently underwent a major operational revamp. It no longer contains the C2 server address itself; instead, it interacts with ClientHub via a shared buffer. We believe this change is intended to enhance concealment. Timeline of LPEClient The previous version collected the computer name, memory status, CPU specifications, operating system details, version of the ntoskrnl.exe file, firmware table, antivirus name, domain name, user name, the integrity level of the current process, and a random byte sequence of 1,568 bytes. The newly updated LPEClient variant mostly collects the same information; however, it also introduces several changes. It does not collect the version of the ntoskrnl.exe file by querying the FileVersion value. Instead, depending on the specific build, it reads either the HKLM\SOFTWARE\Microsoft\Windows NT\CurrentVersion\UBR registry key or the SYSTEM_BUILD_VERSION_INFORMATION structure to retrieve the revision portion of the Windows build number. These values can be used to infer the system’s cumulative update or patch level. To collect information about installed antivirus products, it switched from querying a WQL command to reading the WMI repository file directly. It now reads C:\Windows\System32\wbem\Repository\OBJECTS.DATA and searches for keywords such as AntiVirusProduct, companyName, and displayName to identify the installed products. It also collects low‑level Windows security mitigation states. To achieve this, it queries the SYSTEM_KERNEL_VA_SHADOW_INFORMATION structure for the Kernel VA Shadow mitigation status and the SYSTEM_SHADOW_STACK_INFORMATION structure for the Shadow Stack (CET) protection status. Additionally, it generates a 32‑byte random unique identifier by using the HighwayHash algorithm and an XOR operation with the current system time. The collected information, including the generated identifier, is compressed with the XPRESS Huffman algorithm using the maximum compression setting. The compressed data is then concatenated with the message ID 0xF07D6B34 and encrypted using GOST (also known as Magma), Russia’s national cryptographic standard that employs a DES‑like structure, before being sent to the ClientHub via a shared buffer. Initially, KrCERT described the algorithm as a custom DES‑like scheme. However, further analysis by us and Check Point confirmed that it is actually the GOST algorithm. The key is randomly generated by a proprietary key‑generation algorithm and prepended to the encrypted message. The next request handles the key exchange by delivering the message ID 0xA39CF278 together with the generated unique identifier. The response is decrypted using the AES‑CBC‑256 algorithm. The SHA‑256 hash of the previously generated 1,568‑byte random value, which was originally sent to the C2 server during the collection phase, serves as the key for this decryption. Through this process, LPEClient obtains a byte array comprising four 1,568‑byte blocks from the C2 response. Each block contains an ML‑KEM‑1024 public key, where ML‑KEM is a NIST‑standardized post‑quantum key encapsulation mechanism derived from CRYSTALS‑Kyber. LPEClient then generates four distinct pairs of shared keys and ciphertexts from these blocks, encrypts the ciphertexts with the GOST algorithm, and transmits them to the C2 server via ClientHub together with the message identifier 0xEF8C0D51 and the associated identifier. Now that the preparation phase for receiving payloads is complete, the malware is ready to receive payloads forwarded by ClientHub from the C2 server, along with the necessary parameters. The use of post-quantum cryptography shows that the group intends to hide subsequent payloads. The response to the 0xEF8C0D51 message is decrypted with the Threefish algorithm using a 256-byte key, composed of the four distinct shared keys. Threefish is a tweakable block cipher that accepts a ‘tweak’ value alongside the key and the message. The concept of a tweak is similar to that of a nonce or initialization vector, which are used in different types of ciphers. In LPEClient communication, the tweak for the decryption is derived by XORing the unique identifier with 165 (0xA5) . The first four bytes of the decrypted message must specify the parameter’s length, followed by the parameter itself and then the payload. The payload must be larger than 4 KB and start with the characters 'MZ' , which indicates that the decrypted payload is an executable file. The payload is then reflectively loaded into memory. After successful loading, LPEClient sends the message ID 0xCB790A25 to the C2 server. Before loading the acquired payload, LPEClient takes measures to bypass security products. Instead of invoking VirtualProtect directly, the malware uses the identified HeapPageAllocator::ProtectPages in jscript9.dll to modify the memory page protection of the MD4Update function. The beginning of the function is then overwritten with a hard‑coded opcode sequence that serves as a syscall stub with the required arguments. LPEClient then uses the syscall stub to disable a user‑mode syscall instrumentation hook. We identified the final payload that was ultimately loaded as Cubridge. Infrastructure Based on the information we obtained from the TechHub malware, we identified at least three primary repositories used as the C2 server and one fallback repository. The earliest evidence indicates that the group has been leveraging GitHub as a C2 server since at least July 2025. We suspect that all the accounts were created using hijacked email accounts, and disguised as normal repositories by copying or creating normal projects to avoid suspicion. Victims We have identified at least three firms from the software, construction, and wired communication equipment industries in South Korea that were targeted by Operation FlashHole. As with the previous operation, however, we remain confident that a considerably larger number of organizations across a broader spectrum of industries have been affected. The software sector was the only sector targeted in both the SyncHole and FlashHole campaigns. This suggests the actor is eager to compromise the sector, steal source code, and exploit vulnerabilities for further attacks. Attribution and links to other campaigns Operation FlashHole is the latest wave in the broader Cascade Hole campaign, which involves the use of South Korean software as a potential entry point to deliver malware targeting the country. Although the malware chain has advanced significantly since last year, we assess with medium-to-high confidence that the same actor is responsible for both the SyncHole and FlashHole operations based on the initial vector, targets, multi‑stage chain, and technical and operational improvements. The use of LPEClient in particular is a strong indicator supporting this assessment. The malware strains used in the attack are historically linked to the toolsets observed in the Lazarus Group’s notorious Dream Job campaign. Earlier versions of TechHub (also known as CLOUDBURST and NickelLoader), Cubridge (also known as SIDESHOW and LightlessCan), and TouchShift were all observed in that campaign, especially between 2022 and 2023. We also discovered that both CookiePlus and ClientHub have significant overlaps in class name, configuration path, and plugins. Based on these similarities, we assess with medium‑high confidence that ClientHub is an advanced version of CookiePlus. Since July, around the time Operation SyncHole began winding down, we have also observed several notable attacks against South Korea employing the COPPERHEDGE backdoor and targeting various South Korean organizations. Interestingly, the start times of these activities align with the earliest evidence we have identified for Operation FlashHole. Although the backdoor was part of the second phase of Operation SyncHole, it has been operating independently with its custom loader since then. These incidents featured malware chains and tools that were distinct from those used in Operation SyncHole and FlashHole. As AhnLab noted in their report , the same backdoor was likely observed in atypical activity, specifically the Gunra ransomware infection. Our investigation further revealed that one instance of a backdoor infection ultimately resulted in the exfiltration of data to a dark web leak site, which clearly differed from Gunra’s. The site appears to have been crafted exclusively for this activity. In summary, unlike other attacks, the backdoor was used in conjunction with both ransomware and data exfiltration. Data leakage site linked to the COPPERHEDGE backdoor on the dark web Another noteworthy point is that the recent Dream Job campaign employs an updated malware chain that, since around 2024, has primarily involved MISTPEN and ForestTiger rather than the earlier arsenal that included tools such as TechHub and Cubridge. However, the updated versions of this older malware have now re-emerged as part of Operation FlashHole. Considering these changes and all aspects of each attack’s TTPs, we have organized a classification for each attack. Overlaps between the recent attacks by Lazarus Group Conclusion Our analysis shows that the Lazarus Group begins preparing for its next operation shortly after completing the previous one, ultimately deploying an enhanced malware chain by refining existing tools. The group continues to use the advanced version of LPEClient for profiling and payload delivery, indicating that the tool is meticulously managed and well maintained. Ironically, the group seems to have adopted more secure post-quantum cryptography faster than many legitimate security products. These observations suggest the involvement of a dedicated developer, with others orchestrating comprehensive attack scenarios against newly targeted software. We anticipated that specialized attacks targeting South Korea would persist. However, we did not foresee multiple attack campaigns being executed simultaneously, each leveraging different types of software and tools. Notably, the COPPERHEDGE cluster appears to be involved in monetizing the attacks, while Operation FlashHole is focused on espionage. We are closely monitoring these changes to understand their motivations and ultimate goals. In October 2025, the South Korean government unveiled a comprehensive cybersecurity plan that will phase out the mandatory installation of security software for users beginning in 2026. We estimate that operations exploiting this software will remain highly active for some time, maximizing efficiency, before the software is fully eliminated or mitigated.
