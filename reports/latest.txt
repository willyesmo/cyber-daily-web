IR trends Q3 2026: Actors impersonate IT help desks in vishing attacks as ransomware activity increases
Cisco Talos | Research / advisory | 2026-10-29T14:00:00+00:00
Phishing remained the leading initial access technique, appearing in slightly over one-third of all Cisco Talos Incident Response (Talos IR) engagements — a decrease from over 50 percent last quarter. In addition to traditional phishing links and attachments, we observed numerous threat actors conducting voice phishing (vishing) in tandem with IT desk impersonation to socially engineer the victim into allowing remote access via a legitimate remote monitoring and management (RMM) tool. Exploitation of public-facing applications was the next most common vector, accounting for one-third of Talos IR engagements. Threat actors predominantly targeted edge devices such as security gateways and remote network management interfaces. These are critical components within enterprise environments, often responsible for managing authentication, network traffic, or sensitive data. Compromising them can provide broad access to internal networks or user credentials. Ransomware and pre-ransomware incidents made up 27 percent of engagements in Q3 2026, an increase from a maximum of 20 percent seen in the previous four quarters. Talos responded to attacks involving prolific, technologically advanced, and well-established ransomware-as-a-service (RaaS) variants such as The Gentlemen, Qilin, and Rhysida, as well as Interlock, which operates as a closed group. Threat actors predominantly chose to disable endpoint detection and response (EDR) systems, often by exploiting vulnerable drivers or using Windows Defender Application Control (WDAC). Additionally, we observed the emerging ransomware variant VYPR, whose operator(s) tested a range of tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) instead of following a standard attack method or affiliate playbook. Salt Typhoon-affiliated threat actors exploit public-facing application vulnerabilities for cyber espionage Talos IR continues to see prolonged cyber espionage intrusions targeting the telecommunications sector. Talos attributed most of these intrusions with high confidence to China state-sponsored threat actors, including Salt Typhoon. The attackers in these intrusions routinely gain initial access by leveraging valid credentials and exploiting a known vulnerability (CVE-2023-20198) in publicly accessible web-based management interfaces—two of the most frequently observed initial access vectors this quarter. In one campaign, the threat actor deployed BadCandy, a malicious web shell implant known to be leveraged by Salt Typhoon, to maintain persistent and stealthy remote access. The adversaries continue to employ living-off-the-land (LOTL) tactics, leveraging legitimate network infrastructure features — such as VPN access, traffic mirroring, and management protocols — to establish backdoors, exfiltrate data, and conduct reconnaissance. Exploiting a vulnerability in the web-based management interface, they created privileged accounts, disabled logging to conceal their activity, and altered configurations to maintain persistence, blending their actions with normal administrative operations to evade detection. These intrusions revealed several security weaknesses in the victim environment, including reliance on password-only authentication without evidence of multi-factor authentication (MFA), use of legacy and weak management protocols (such as plaintext SNMP and outdated VPN encryption), and inadequate credential management practices. Attackers were able to create and abuse privileged accounts, disable logging, and manipulate configuration settings without encountering effective security barriers, enabling persistent and stealthy access. The absence of strong MFA and robust credential management are two of the most frequently observed security weaknesses this quarter. AI exploited as a weapon and a target Recent Talos IR investigations highlight the growing role of artificial intelligence (AI) in cybercriminal activity, both as a tool and a target. In one case, an internal threat actor who was hired under false credentials abused their privileged access to install AI-powered development tools, such as GitHub Copilot CLI and Anthropic Claude Code. While the investigation did not find evidence of malicious activity or data theft, the employee’s developer-level access — including cloning repositories, configuring plugins, and connecting to databases — could have enabled them to use AI tools to automate malicious actions within the organization. A second investigation revealed an attack chain in which a compromised macOS endpoint, infected via a trojanized application, was scripted to scrape credentials from the macOS keychain specifically associated with the Anthropic Claude Code AI assistant. This direct targeting of AI-related secrets highlights an emerging threat, where attackers seek to compromise not only traditional user and system credentials but also those tied to AI platforms, which may grant access to additional sensitive data and advanced automation capabilities. These incidents highlight the critical need for organizations to implement strong MFA and robust credential management practices, especially for AI platforms and development tools. Ransomware trends Ransomware and pre-ransomware incidents increased from a little over 20 percent last quarter to 27 percent in Q3, involving well-known threat groups like Interlock and the emerging ransomware VYPR. Frequently observed techniques involved deleting EDR systems and socially engineering victims into initiating remote management sessions. Emerging ransomware variant: VYPR During a Talos IR engagement, we observed a ransomware variant called “VYPR” that we assess is relatively new and still operationally immature. This assessment is supported by the absence of an established data leak site (DLS), as none was referenced in the ransom note and associated DLS infrastructure was not created until after the engagement. The group also appeared to rely on older leaked Conti-derived source code with only minimal modifications, reflecting a relatively unsophisticated technical approach. Their operations further suggested experimentation rather than a refined playbook, as they took an unusually long time to exfiltrate and encrypt data while testing different exfiltration parameters and deploying multiple vulnerable drivers to disable EDR protections. The actor gained initial access through voice phishing (vishing); they tricked a user into initiating a Microsoft Quick Assist session, allowing remote access to the endpoint. While the exact social engineering technique was not confirmed in this case, threat actors frequently impersonate IT help desks during vishing calls to manipulate victims into establishing remote connections — a tactic observed in several other Q3 engagements. The actor was then able to utilize existing RMM software, which subsequently executed PowerShell commands to download and install a Microsoft Software Installer (MSI) package from a remote IP address. During the attack, the actor leveraged tools that are commonly observed during ransomware investigations, including MeshAgent, Rclone, PsExec, IP scan, and Microsoft Terminal Services Client (MSTSC). Notably, we saw exfiltration over multiple days, which is not commonly observed during ransomware investigations. EDR killers: Why evade detection when you can destroy it? We observed several instances where ransomware operators disabled EDR systems, prioritizing direct disruption of security controls. We observed Interlock ransomware actors making multiple attempts to impair endpoint detection EDR capabilities by trying to uninstall Cisco Secure Endpoint (CSE) on compromised systems and used tools such as PsExec.exe and taskkill.exe to terminate security services and integrity checkers, including cscm.exe and sfc.exe. Additionally, the adversary disabled Windows Defender real-time protection and cleared various event logs to reduce visibility and hinder forensic analysis. This is in line with previously observed Interlock activity, where they deployed a malicious WDAC policy to terminate EDR solutions. In the previously mentioned VYPR ransomware incident, the threat actor disabled EDR and antivirus (AV) by using Bring Your Own Vulnerable Driver (BYOVD) techniques to evade detection and execute the ransomware. BYOVD attacks take advantage of trusted but flawed drivers to gain kernel-level access, enabling threat actors to disable EDR mechanisms and carry out their operations unimpeded. While this technique is commonly observed in ransomware attacks, this actor deployed an unusually large number of vulnerable drivers in the environment, possibly to create redundancies and maximize their chances of success. Targeting Targeting of the financial industry increased from last quarter to comprise 18 percent of all engagements, tying with public administration as the most targeted sector. Although the financial industry remains a valuable target, Talos has not ranked it among the top three most impacted sectors since 2024. One possible reason is that organizations in finance can typically invest more in cybersecurity, unlike sectors such as public administration, which has consistently been among the top-targeted industries over the past year. Meanwhile, health care fell to third place after ranking as the most targeted industry for the previous two consecutive quarters. Initial access While the number of engagements involving phishing as an initial access vector declined from last quarter, it remained the most common technique, appearing in 37 percent of Talos IR engagements. Exploitation of public-facing applications was the second most common initial access vector, accounting for 33 percent of engagements. Recommendations for addressing top security weaknesses The most common security weaknesses observed this quarter were insufficient logging, improper credential and secret management, and absent or improperly implemented authentication and MFA. These trends closely mirror last quarter, when insufficient logging and authentication weaknesses were also prevalent. Centralize logging and set retention requirements The leading security weakness was insufficient logging leading to visibility gaps, seen in over 40 percent of engagements this quarter. These gaps were in part due to the common threat actor practice of deleting logs, and in part due to insufficient log retention or configuration implemented by the customer. Talos IR recommends implementing a SIEM or centralized logging platform with a minimum of 90 days of retention, forwarding logs from servers, workstations, network infrastructure, cloud identity providers, and security appliances off-device so they survive log tampering and host rebuilds, and enabling process-creation, command-line, and cloud API (e.g., Microsoft Graph) auditing. Talos IR's Log Architecture Assessment service can identify gaps and provide a roadmap to a complete view of the environment. Tighten authentication and MFA controls Missing or insufficient authentication and MFA controls were the third most common security weakness this quarter, observed in 33 percent of engagements. The most prevalent issue was reliance on single-factor authentication, which allowed unauthorized access in several incidents. In many cases, legacy authentication protocols were still enabled, enabling adversaries to log in without any MFA requirements, and some environments failed to enforce MFA for high-privilege accounts. To reduce this risk, Talos IR recommends transitioning from push- and SMS-based MFA to phishing-resistant methods such as FIDO2/WebAuthn and hardware security keys. Organizations should also restrict self-service MFA enrollment by requiring helpdesk verification, block legacy authentication through Conditional Access, enforce number matching or verified push where phishing-resistant methods are not yet feasible, and condition access on device compliance and trusted infrastructure rather than geographic location alone. Safeguard credentials and secrets Improper credential and secret management was the second most common security weakness seen in 33 percent of engagements this quarter, reinforcing the importance of security awareness training and consistent security hygiene for every user. Observed security issues included unsafe storage and handling of credentials, such as saving passwords in browser stores, poor control mechanisms like permitting autofill for browser-stored credentials, and credential reuse and sharing. Effective secret management begins with a formal process that covers passwords, API keys, tokens, certificates, and other sensitive credentials. By requiring unique passwords for every account and encouraging the use of password managers, organizations reduce the risks associated with credential reuse and sharing. Storing secrets securely, with encryption and access restricted to only those who need them, further strengthens protection. When credentials are no longer necessary or are suspected to be compromised, they should be promptly revoked and replaced. Regular reviews of access to privileged credentials, combined with thorough logging, help detect unusual activity. Equipping staff with training on safe handling and clear reporting procedures ensures that accidental disclosures are addressed quickly and effectively. NIK C2: The new AI-enabled command-and-control framework
Cisco Talos | Research / advisory | 2026-10-29T10:00:00+00:00
Cisco Talos observed a new AI-integrated command-and-control (C2) framework in the wild called “NIK C2.” NIK C2 contains a Windows implant, using which, an actor can dump credentials, stream the victim's keystrokes to collect the targeted victim’s sensitive data, and instruct an integrated AI model to analyze the harvested data, all within the compromised session. In this framework, the actor has abused the OpenAI Chat Completions application programming interface (API), which is a HTTP endpoint that enables ChatGPT-style conversations programmatically. In addition to the features that enable theft, an actor can also deploy desktop screen-locking ransomware via the NIK C2 implant onto the victim machine, leading to a ransom demand in Bitcoin or USDT cryptocurrencies. NIK C2 reshapes adversary tradecraft by collapsing collection and exploitation into a single continuous session, effectively erasing the offline processing delays that historically granted defenders critical detection windows. Such AI-enabled adversarial frameworks require defenders to move beyond traditional detection and embrace continuous behavioral vigilance as the new baseline. NIK C2 implant: AI-integrated remote access tool The NIK C2 implant is a Go-based, 64-bit Windows remote access tool that grants persistent control over compromised hosts using a resilient dual-channel C2 architecture. It communicates over TLS-secured WebSockets on port 443 alongside an HTTP fallback polling loop every 250 milliseconds, protecting all traffic with AES-256-GCM encryption derived from a hardcoded seed. To survive takedowns, the implant routes traffic across three prioritized C2 tiers, which are runtime-updated URLs, an embedded encrypted server list, and a hardcoded fallback address. Beyond standard operations, the implant features surveillance, theft, lateral movement, and evasion capabilities, including credential dumping, RDP session hijacking, process hollowing, and a C2-driven screen lock ransomware module. Notably, the NIK C2 implant embeds a fully integrated OpenAI API client via its ai_generate command, allowing operators to route arbitrary prompts to the OpenAI Chat Completions API directly through the infected host. The NIK C2 implant manages API key resolution, request assembly, and response parsing locally, returning the encrypted generative AI results to the actor's C2. The observed NIK C2 implant samples in the wild contains an embedded build path (/opt/nik_panel/data/build_52b79724a1fc4b8f/main.go), which identifies the project as "nik_panel" and indicates that the implant was cross compiled for Windows from a Linux development environment. Furthermore, the embedded eight-byte hex token matching the build identifier highlights that the threat actor's infrastructure actively tracking and versioning the implants. Figure 1. NIK C2 implant’s operational architecture. NIK C2 implant evolution timeline Talos observed that the NIK C2 implant development trajectory spans at least three distinct versions. All binaries observed in the wild were compiled with Go 1.23.4 (which was released in December 2024), placing the entire sample set compiled no earlier than late 2024. The actor has Zeroed PE TimeDateStamp fields and near-epoch filesystem timestamps (manifesting as Nov 29, 1979) are applied consistently across all samples. We developed a timeline based on the initial sighting dates of NIK C2 implant variants in OSINT. Figure 2. NIK C2 implant evolution timeline. The pace of capability addition across the three versions indicates a technically proficient team with prior malware operations experience is actively expanding NIK C2 towards a full-service adversarial framework. Active NIK C2 server seen in the wild During our research, we observed that a threat actor hosted the NIK C2 framework on a server at IP address “87.120.196[.]120”, resolving the domain “total-sitevv[.]lol”. The C2 server hosted an SSL/TLS certificate (serial number 7d16381a9de823943b5a0987dbd0f26edf22052b and jarm:"28d28d28d00000000042d42d0000001f361a1bf80ed39a1e352adeb016a07b" ) with the Common Name (CN) set to nik-panel . This CN value matches strings embedded within the implant binary. Observing the NIK C2 infrastructure active in the wild allowed us to assess with moderate confidence that NIK C2 is not merely a proof of concept, but a fully functional, AI-integrated C2 framework. Figure 3. NIK C2 was observed in the wild during the research period. NIK C2 implant integrates OpenAI’s API AI integration is one of the most architecturally distinct features of NIK C2 implant. It constructs a fully formed HTTP request to OpenAI’s production inference endpoint, transmits it from the victim machine IP address, parses the JSON response, and returns the AI-generated text to the actor’s panel. The entire operation from receiving the actor prompt to returning the large language model (LLM) reply is executed synchronously with a 60-second timeout. The implant begins by extracting three fields from the command payload received from the C2. The first field, identified by the key prompt , is the text the actor wants to send to the AI model. If the prompt string has zero length, the handler immediately constructs an error response and returns without making any outbound network call. The second field, identified by the key api_key is the OpenAI API key to use for authentication. The third field, identified by the key model , specifies which OpenAI model to use. Figure 4. Snippet of NIK C2 implant AI handler module. After extracting the three parameters, the implant checks whether the actor supplied an API key. If not, it calls the operating system's environment variable reader with the string OPENAI_API_KEY and uses the victim’s API key. This fallback is not a backup mechanism for API key resolution. It is a deliberate design with significant operational implications. If the victim has configured an OpenAI account on their machine, that account's API key will be used to fund the actor's AI inference. OpenAI's billing system will charge the victim's payment method, and OpenAI's access logs will show the victim's API key as the one that made the requests, allowing no trace attributable to the threat actor. If the actor does not specify a model name, the implant falls back to a five-character default string stored in the binary data section. Decoding the five-character value showed gpt-4 confirming that GPT4 is the default when no model is specified. The actor can override this by passing any valid OpenAI model identifier in the command payload, allowing selection of newer models as they become available without requiring a binary update. The implant builds the OpenAI API request body by constructing a map with four fields. The first field uses the key model and contains the resolved model name. The second field uses key messages and contains a two-element array of message maps. The third field uses the key temperature and contains a floating-point value read from a constant in the binary data section. The fourth field uses the key max_tokens and contains an integer value also read from binary. The message array is constructed in the order that the OpenAI Chat Completions API expects. The system message is first followed by the user message. The system message map has the role system and a content string that is exactly 68 bytes long hardcoded in the binary. This 68-byte string is the implant's behavioral instruction to the AI. It defines the model's operational role and response style for the duration of the session. The user message map has the role user and contains the actor's prompt text verbiage. The complete map structure is then serialized to JSON. The JSON message is sent to the URL https:// api.openai.com/v1/chat/completions , the OpenAI Chat Completions endpoint, through an HTTP POST request. Figure 5. Snippet of NIK C2 implant AI handler module. When the HTTP response arrives with a 200 status code, the handler uses Golang's JSON decoder to parse the response body directly into a generic map structure without defining a typed schema. It then navigates the decoded map by string key lookups in the sequence that the OpenAI Chat Completions API response structure defines. The outermost map is queried for the key choices which in a successful response contains an array. The implant checks that this array is non-empty, then accesses its element keys message and content . The string value at key content is the AI model-generated text response. After successfully extracting the AI-generated content, the handler assembles a result map with four entries. The key status is set to a constant string that reads as generated . The key code is set to the AI model's response text, so that the actor receives the full content of the model’s replies. The key model is set to the resolved model name that was used for the request, confirming which model produced the output. The key usage is set to the raw usage object from the OpenAI response, which contains the prompt token count, completion token count, and total token count. This usage data allows the actor to track how many tokens are consumed per request. The completed result map is ultimately transmitted to the C2 server over WebSocket or through the HTTP fallback C2 address. Figure 6. AI model response is encapsulated in JSON message and will be sent to C2. Dual execution modes of NIK C2 implant Upon execution, the implant inspects its command-line arguments to determine its execution path. The argument watchdog activates supervisory monitoring to relaunch the core process if terminated, the copywatchdog tracks file copy operations, and the absence of either defaults execution to primary agent mode. In primary mode, it uses a named mutex to enforce single-instance execution, logs a message reading [NIK] Starting , generates a 32-byte cryptographic session key if uninitialized, decodes its obfuscated C2 address, and sets its masquerade name to “svchost.exe” or “simvol.exe”. After initial setup, the binary records its process ID (PID) in the victim's temporary application folder and executes its persistence and evasion routines. These routines include copying to %appdata%/Roaming/Microsoft/Network with modified access controls, configuring Windows autostart registry entries, and masquerading its process. Finally, it starts the local proxy server, initiates the WebSocket communication loop in a separate goroutine, and enters a continuous heartbeat polling loop on the main thread. Implant deploys screen-locking ransomware The ransomware module of the implant operates as a multi-stage attack controller that the actor runs in sequence to prepare, deploy, and retract a screen-locking ransomware payload. At first, it executes PowerShell to execute a raw disk copy command that reads directly from the physical drive device path (“\\.\PhysicalDrive0”) and writes the first sector, which is Master Boot Record (MBR), to a file named “mbr_backup.bin” in the Windows temporary directory. Figure 7. Snippet of NIK C2 implant’s boot ransomware handler function. Next, the module constructs a complete self-contained HTML lock screen code entirely at runtime from a hardcoded format string, parameterized with actor supplied Bitcoin and USDT wallet addresses, prices, contact email, timer duration, and custom text from the C2 command message, allowing the actor to fully customize the ransom demands. Figure 8. Screenlocker HTML code is hardcoded in NIK C2 implant binary. After generating the HTML code, the binary writes it to the file “lock_screen.html” in the system temporary directory. It then generates a Windows batch script that calls mshta.exe with the path to the HTML file, followed by an infinite loop construct with a five-second timeout between iterations, ensuring that if the user somehow closes the window it reopens within five seconds. This batch file is written to the temp directory as “lock_start.bat”. The implant then runs a reg add command that writes a registry Run key named “SystemLock” under the current user's autostart path, pointing to a cmd /c invocation of the batch file, so the lock screen is executed on every user's logon. Additionally, a background goroutine is spawned that continuously monitors and relaunches the lock screen process. Finally, the module sends a confirmation response to C2 with the path of the deployed HTML file and the timer value. The design of this ransomware module is notable for several reasons. It does not encrypt any files, which means it leaves no forensic trail of file modification timestamps or encryption markers. It relies entirely on a screen lock, which is considerably faster to deploy than a file encryption ransomware. The persistence via registry Run key and a self-healing loop ensures the screen lock survives user attempts to dismiss it and survives a system restart. Figure 9. Implant’s bootkit preparation feature The bootkit preparation module of the implant is designed to survey the victim machine's boot firmware environment and then stage a bootkit payload file. It executes bcdedit /enum , the Windows Boot Configuration Data editor, with the output flag that enumerates all boot entries while hiding the console window. The implant reads the full output and searches for three specific substrings in sequence including EFI , Windows Boot Manager , and Windows Legacy OS Loader . If any of these are found, the boot type is classified as UEFI. The binary also searches for ntldr , the legacy Windows NT loader used by BIOS-mode systems as a fallback. Based on which string was matched, the boot type is recorded as either "UEFI" or "BIOS" as a string of the appropriate length. Separately, the binary runs a PowerShell command invoking Confirm-SecureBootUEFI , which is a built-in PowerShell command that returns to the current Secure Boot state. The output is searched for the string True to detect an enabled Secure Boot and for False to detect an explicitly disabled Secure Boot. If neither is present, the value is recorded as unknown . The result is sent back to the C2, a map containing the boot type classification and the Secure Boot status string. Then the implant writes a 16-byte placeholder file named “bootkit.bin” to the system temporary directory. The bytes written spell out "NIK_BOOTKIT_PREP" in ASCII, which serves as a staging marker rather than an actual bootkit binary. This placeholder confirms that the target path is writable by the current process, establishes the file location that subsequent operations will reference, and can be overwritten by the actual bootkit binary delivered through the implant's file-upload or file-write command handlers. The path to the staged file is returned to the C2 in response. Keylogger: Keystrokes livestreamed to C2 The keylogger module of the implant uses a persistent background goroutine that polls all 256 Windows virtual key codes, detecting keypresses via dynamically resolved API calls, and transmits the captured input. When a compromised victim presses a valid key on the keyboard, it is simultaneously streamed live as an AES-256-GCM encrypted JSON payload over WebSocket to C2 and also appends to an in-memory buffer capped at 10,000 entries and halved when full to prevent memory leaks. On an actor's dump command, this buffer is concatenated into a single string and returned to C2 via WebSocket or HTTP fallback, ensuring complete data transfer even during network interruptions. Stolen data exfiltration to C2 Once the archive is assembled, the entire result map is handed to the result transmission routine of the implant. That routine constructs a new JSON object containing five specific fields. It includes the command identifier echoed back from the original command. It also contains the agent's persistent identifier, formatted as ag_<16 hex chars> . The type field is set to the string quick_steal_response . Finally, the data field holds the complete result map. Once constructed, this object is forwarded to the encryption routine. The routine serializes the object into JSON. It then generates 12 fresh random bytes to serve as a nonce. Next, it encrypts the serialized JSON using AES-256-GCM with a static key derived from a hardcoded seed. It concatenates the nonce, the ciphertext, and the 16-byte authentication tag and Base64-encodes the entire payload. The encrypted payload is placed in a wrapper object under the "e" key alongside the plain-text agent ID, and the wrapper is JSON marshaled into the final payload. This encrypted payload is then delivered through an available transmission path. The sending routine first checks whether the WebSocket connection is live. If it is, the payload is written directly into a WebSocket text frame on the existing connection, reaching the actor's panel with minimal latency. If the WebSocket is not currently connected, the routine falls back to an HTTP POST request to the endpoint path /api/c2/result on the C2 server, using the same encrypted body format. This dual-path design ensures stolen data is delivered even during WebSocket interruptions, as the HTTP channel remains functional independently of the WebSocket state. Implant’s C2 protocol The implant C2 architecture is designed for two independent, simultaneously operating communication channels. The primary channel is a persistent WebSocket connection over TLS. The secondary channel is an HTTP polling loop that posts to a fixed API endpoint at regular intervals. With the primary channel, the connection begins with a TCP dial to the C2 server address with an eight-second timeout. If the port is not explicitly present in the configured address, port 443 is appended automatically. Once the TCP socket is established, it is wrapped in a TLS session using a custom certificate configuration that pins to a specific certificate rather than validating against the system's certificate authority store. This prevents standard TLS interception proxies from observing traffic. Immediately after a successful WebSocket handshake but before any command is received, the implant transmits a full system registration message. The implant collects victim data such as the machine hostname, the local LAN IP address, the Windows username from the environment, a user security identifier, the local reverse-proxy port number that was assigned when the embedded proxy server was started, and the detected upstream proxy configuration derived from the Windows registry proxy settings. All of this is assembled into a JSON object and encrypted and sent to the C2. Figure 10. Snippet of NIK C2 IMPLANT’s C2 communication handler function. The HTTP polling channel runs independently. Implant’s defense evasion and anti-analysis techniques The NIK C2 implant deploys a layered set of defense evasion measures that activate immediately at startup and operate across the full compromise session. Conclusion The emergence of NIK C2, an AI-enabled command-and-control framework, underscores a critical shift in modern malware design: the transition from static intrusion toolsets to dynamic, modular implants that adapt on demand. By embedding a live AI pipeline directly into the C2 loop, the compromised host is no longer a passive foothold but an autonomous operational asset. Operators can dynamically generate context-aware lure material, execute localized deception, synthesize operational intelligence, and craft targeted payloads directly on the compromised hosts — all while lowering the technical barrier which was traditionally required for such sophisticated tradecraft. Inside KeyDrift: Analyzing historical UAT-11473 activity on Cisco SD-WAN
Cisco Talos | Research / advisory | 2026-10-28T21:32:02+00:00
Cisco Talos has identified UAT-11473, a highly sophisticated advanced persistent threat (APT) actor, who exploited CVE-2026-20127 , an authentication bypass vulnerability in Cisco SD-WAN Catalyst Controller instances to bypass authentication and gain unauthorized access to affected systems. UAT-11473 then installed a custom malware framework we track as “KeyDrift” consisting of multiple components geared towards collecting sensitive keying information, acting as backdoors and intermediate command-and-control (C2) hosts. Observed targeting spans across space and satellite services, defense and aerospace technology, and policy research. Talos assesses with high confidence that UAT-11473’s operating infrastructure overlaps considerably with Volt Typhoon’s “Silent Train” covert access network . KeyDrift is a custom malware framework that is designed to operate in Cisco SD-WAN controller environments. Talos assesses with high confidence that the framework is designed to provide long-term persistent access and automated exfiltration of sensitive keying material. KeyDrift operates inside SD-WAN control-plane processes and is capable of intercepting decrypted control traffic, extracting control-session metadata and data-plane keying material, and staging or transmitting the collected material using encrypted records. The framework's primary component is PolyFlame, a memory-resident module deployed through modified runit service paths. PolyFlame is injected into legitimate running vdaemon processes, and it can also masquerade as a packed “aaamgrd” loader variant. Once resident, PolyFlame interposes socket, DTLS, and runtime-linker behavior through functions such as recvfrom, sendto, getpeername, dlopen, dlsym, mprotect, and “/proc/%d/maps” parsing. This gives KeyDrift access to SD-WAN control-plane traffic from inside legitimate controller daemon execution rather than through a conventional endpoint beacon. LittleFilch, another component of the KeyDrift framework, provides the collection and exfiltration capability identified in the framework. The module hooks “SSL_read” inside vdaemon, inspects decrypted control-plane buffers, extracts keying material for a list of predefined edge nodes, and stages the data locally in encrypted format for exfiltration. Talos assesses with high confidence that KeyDrift's intended operational purpose is Cisco SD-WAN control-plane collection: persistent controller residence, in-band traffic access, key-material extraction from decrypted control messages, and encrypted staging or exfiltration of the recovered material. Observed variations of the KeyDrift modules and deployment mechanisms suggest active development of the framework’s capabilities. KeyDrift analysis KeyDrift uses Cisco SD-WAN runit service paths as its primary execution anchor. In the PolyFlame sshd deployment, the actor modifies the service run script, remounts /rootfs.rw executable, launches a malicious script “conf_sshd” in the background, and then starts the legitimate sshd service. The loader script enumerates running vdaemon PIDs, records them in “/tmp/system-vdaemon”, and invokes the injector component “rsshd” to inject PolyFlame (“libdssl.so”) into each process. Recovered conf_sshd logic uses the same control flow across reviewed samples: Figure 1. conf_sshd control flow logic. Runit launchers The observed sshd runit launcher preserves normal Cisco SD-WAN sshd startup logic while adding two malicious lines before service execution, which remount “/rootfs.rw” executable and background execution of conf_sshd, which then drives rsshd injection into vdaemon. Figure 2. conf_sshd execution logic. The recovered aaamgr runit launcher from an affected vBond appliance uses a parallel service-wrapper pattern, but it executes the packed aaamgrd PolyFlame loader directly. The deletion of the writable-overlay PAM configurations for sshd and login before remounting “/rootfs.rw” executable if part of the legitimate script, along with the invocation of the aaamgrd daemon via Python: Figure 3. aamgrd daemon launch. PolyFlame PolyFlame is the primary operational module in the KeyDrift framework. PolyFlame operates at the socket and DTLS control-plane layer. PolyFlame serves two primary purposes: Command execution via a TinyShell-styled backdoor Intermediate C2 that can forward commands to and from the C2 to other peers PolyFlame is primarily injected into the vdaemon process, providing operational obfuscation and access to all in-process state: DTLS session keys, vEdge topology, OMP routes, and policy tables. No hardcoded IPv4 addresses, binding ports, callback domains, URLs, host headers, or polling interval were recovered from the analyzed PolyFlame samples. PolyFlame is designed for in-band control or tasking over Cisco SD-WAN control-plane traffic rather than a conventional fixed external beacon. This implant is meant to serve as a hook of the recvfrom() API call. The original recvfrom is replaced by a malicious subroutine in the vdaemon process. When recvfrom is called in the vdaemon process, the malicious subroutine will first execute the real/legitimate recvfrom() API to obtain the C2 IP address info for communicating with it in the future. PolyFlame’s time check The recvd data from the C2 consists of the MD5 value of a specific DATE. PolyFlame will calculate the local time on the device in format “%Y-%m-%d" and calculate its MD5 hash. If the MD5 calculated by the device does not match with the one sent by the C2, the implant will stop further execution. Otherwise, the implant then uses an “administrative code” from the C2 and performs an action. PolyFlame’s tasking PolyFlame contains an administrative mode that processes the admin code sent by the C2, that defines operations — it's one of the first flags checked in the backdoor: Code = 1: Generate random bytes and send to C2. This is likely a check-in beacon or heartbeat for the C2. Code = 2: Set implant type to backdoor (TinyShell) or intermediate C2. Code = 3: Set up the command handler for the intermediate C2. Any other code value: Check the implant type flag currently set. Based on the implant type flag, either send the accompanying data to TinyShell or the intermediate C2 component for processing. PolyFlame’s command execution PolyFlame contains a backdoor that matches the capabilities of TinyShell: file read, file write, and arbitrary command execution, but not interactive shell. This backdoor component’s implementation is quite similar to TinyShell, but the command codes differ. PolyFlame handles the following command codes: Code = 0x100: Execute command using “/bin/sh -c <command>” Code = 0x200: Write file to disk. Code = 0x300: Read file from disk and send to the C2. PolyFlame as an intermediate C2 component The intermediate C2 component is a one-to-many handler (somewhat like a one-to-many multiplexer) — meaning the implant can connect to only one C2 but can connect to many peers who can be served by the intermediate C2 component. Similar to the TinyShell-backdoor component, the intermediate C2 component also accepts and processes specific command codes from the C2: Code = 0x403: Create a thread to connect to the specified peer IP. Make a new connection and keep socket alive until specified. Code = 0x404: Send data recvd from the C2 to a connected peer. Code = 0x405: Remove a peer from the list of active connections. Code = 0x407: Send a response recvd from a peer, back to the C2. Code = 0x408: Set a specific value in memory. PolyFlame variant: aaamgrd Talos has observed a distinct PolyFlame variant that masquerades as the aaamgr service. The legitimate Cisco aaamgr component is a Python package; it does not normally appear as a compiled ELF runit service. This PolyFlame variant is executed by a malicious runit service under “/etc/sv/aaamgr/” with aaamgrd and a modified run launcher. aaamgrd is a statically linked ELF64 executable with no section headers, a single RWE loader segment, and an RC4-encrypted embedded ELF payload. The loader uses direct syscalls including ptrace, fork, wait4, kill, tgkill, mmap, and mprotect. It performs an anti-debug check by deobfuscating /proc/%d/status and TracerPid: and exits if a tracer is present. The observed staging sequence was: Figure 4. PolyFlame staging sequence. Note that in addition to the implant execution staging, there are also indications of timestomping. LittleFilch: libcommon.so TLS interception module LittleFilch is a control-session interception module positioned to collect data-plane keying material from control sessions visible to the injected vSmart process. LittleFilch installs an SSL_read hook inside vdaemon. The hook calls the original SSL_read, then processes returned buffers only when the returned size is greater than 0x18F, the first decrypted byte is 0x07, and three marker patterns are checked for presence. These marker bytes are followed by the actual artifacts that LittleFilch aims to record: Marker bytes Parsed field Length Notes 40 03 06 Public IP 4 bytes vEdge public / transport IP 40 0C 04 Peer system-IP 4 bytes vEdge OMP system-IP 05 01 0C 06 40 Binary key field 32 (0x20) bytes Assessed OMP-distributed AES-256 IPsec data-plane key material LittleFilch records the extracted keying material and RSA-encrypts it before writing the output to “/var/tmp/vdaemon.bin”. It can then send the encrypted content over UDP in a DTLS 1.2 application-data style record. Figure 5. Keying material recording format used by LittleFilch. LittleFilch expects its configuration file — located at “/var/tmp/vdaemon.conf” — to contain two host port lines: one local bind IP/port and one remote destination IP/port. It deletes “/var/tmp/vdaemon.conf” after reading it. Talos has not been able to recover the “vdaemon.conf” file. Tracking AI adoption in North Korean threat actor tradecraft
Microsoft Security | Research / advisory | 2026-10-29T16:00:00+00:00
In this article Stages of AI adoption by North Korean threat actors AI use across North Korean threat operations AI-assisted targeting and reconnaissance AI-assisted social engineering and identity deception AI-assisted software and tooling development AI-assisted operational scaling and analysis Mitigation and protection guidance Microsoft Defender detections As Microsoft Threat Intelligence continues to track how threat actors operationalize AI to increase the speed, efficiency, and complexity of attacks, we’ve identified North Korean threat actors expanding their use of AI with varying levels of adoption. Threat actors use AI as a force multiplier across their operations, particularly to accelerate and scale established workflows. Recent activities illustrate this AI-enabled acceleration and scaling: Jade Sleet launched campaigns through three front companies in a single week, Onyx Sleet generated dozens of tailored lures in minutes, Coral Sleet rapidly built a new capability to trojanize private source code repositories, and Diamond Sleet used AI to enhance tailored targeting workflows, including persona development and front company creation. Further, Onyx Sleet and Pearl Sleet are leveraging the open-source agentic AI penetration testing framework ARTEX to conduct operations, further indicating North Korea’s willingness to quickly adopt new AI technologies for their operations. The Democratic People’s Republic of Korea operates under extensive international sanctions and uses cyber operations and other illicit mechanisms to evade these restrictions and advance state objectives. Within this operating environment, North Korean threat actors have demonstrated the willingness to adopt new technologies that help sustain or advance existing operations. AI adoption by North Korean threat actors therefore largely supports existing goals and targeting priorities rather than representing a shift in actor motivations. North Korean threat actors conduct cyber operations primarily to generate revenue for the regime, collect strategic intelligence, and, in some cases, pursue politically motivated disruptive or destructive objectives. Across these objectives, North Korean threat actor tradecraft remains grounded in social engineering, malware delivery, and the abuse of legitimate tools. These operations commonly target cryptocurrency and financial organizations, information technology companies and software supply chains, government entities, and education-related organizations. Despite threat actors’ adoption of AI, Microsoft Threat Intelligence assesses that AI-enabled operations remain human-directed and have not become fully autonomous. Microsoft Threat Intelligence assesses the overall adoption of AI across North Korean threat actors as high, with threat actors such as Jade Sleet, Coral Sleet, and Jasper Sleet demonstrating an established use of AI across all operational workflows, alongside increasing adoption across the broader North Korean threat actor set. This blog breaks down how North Korean threat actors are utilizing AI across their operations, reflecting a broader trend of threat actors incorporating AI into their attack chains. We also provide mitigation guidance and Microsoft Defender detections to help organizations defend against these threats. Stages of AI adoption by North Korean threat actors North Korean threat actors are increasingly integrating generative and agentic AI platforms into their operations, but the rate and maturity of adoption vary. Based on identified AI use, Microsoft Threat Intelligence assesses high-impact North Korean threat actors across four stages of adoption: emerging, expanding, accelerating, and operationalized. Across these actors, AI use spans research, content generation, development, malware and tooling, infrastructure, and broader operational activities. Mapping high-impact North Korean threat actors by stages of AI adoption Operationalized: Threat actors at this stage have embedded AI into routine operational workflows, using it as an established part of their existing processes. Microsoft Threat Intelligence assesses that the North Korean threat actors Jade Sleet, Coral Sleet, and Jasper Sleet have operationalized AI across their entire operations, which frequently encompass identity-based attacks built on deceptive social engineering. These attacks can be accelerated with AI capabilities that tailor lures or personas to the intended audience. Accelerating: Threat actors at this stage demonstrate rapid adoption of AI across multiple operational functions. Microsoft Threat Intelligence assesses that Diamond Sleet , Onyx Sleet, Pearl Sleet, and Sapphire Sleet are at the accelerating stage of AI adoption. Expanding: Threat actors at this stage are expanding their use of AI across selected operational activities, but adoption is not yet embedded across broader workflows. Microsoft Threat Intelligence assesses that Emerald Sleet is at the expanding stage of AI adoption. While evidence suggests Emerald Sleet has incorporated AI into some workflows, Microsoft Threat Intelligence has not identified the same level of adoption and impact as other North Korean threat actors. Emerging: Threat actors at this stage demonstrate limited or experimental use of AI. At this time, Microsoft Threat Intelligence has not assessed any North Korean threat actors as operating at the emerging stage. AI use across North Korean threat operations Microsoft Threat Intelligence has identified North Korean threat actors using AI platforms to support a range of operational activities, from reconnaissance to developing social engineering content, maintaining fraudulent identities, supporting tool development, and scaling repetitive operational tasks. Current evidence indicates that these actors primarily use AI to enhance existing tradecraft and operator productivity rather than to enable autonomous operations. Microsoft Threat Intelligence has identified North Korean threat actors leveraging the following AI-enabled tactics as part of their threat operations. AI-assisted targeting and reconnaissance Based on industry reporting and changes in targeting activity in 2026, North Korean threat actors are scaling reconnaissance capabilities by using generative AI platforms, including gathering information on target organizations prior to launching social engineering operations. Microsoft Threat Intelligence has identified that these actors: Research organizations and employees, then use information from this research to craft highly bespoke and convincing lures, target-specific infrastructure, and job applications Research wallets, exchanges, and decentralized finance (DeFi) and blockchain workflows, accelerating the development of domain knowledge needed to support targeted operations Explore vulnerabilities, APIs, security products, and cloud services, leading to a demonstrated change in the speed and sophistication of threat activity against specific technologies Research software development roles, job requirements, and technology organizations to support job searching, resume development, and persona alignment, resulting in rapid tailored persona or lure creation, application to newly posted job roles, or spear phishing operations For example, between June 8 and June 12, 2026, Microsoft Threat Intelligence identified Jade Sleet conducting job-themed targeting against individuals across various organizations, including those involved in cybersecurity, AI, blockchain, and talent recruitment companies. The actor leveraged tailored domains, social media profiles, and industry-specific knowledge to pose as recruiters for a spoofed AI-powered blockchain intelligence company, using software engineer-themed opportunities to initiate contact and schedule interviews with individuals who engaged with the outreach. Within this short time frame, Jade Sleet conducted similar job-themed targeting using two additional front companies, supported by comparable infrastructure and relevant domain knowledge. Microsoft Threat Intelligence assesses that the pace and breadth of this activity demonstrates how Jade Sleet is using generative AI to accelerate operational workflows, including rapidly developing target- and industry-specific knowledge, tailoring reconnaissance and targeting, and scaling outreach to target individuals. Jasper Sleet also continues to leverage generative AI platforms to build fake digital personas. The actor’s use of suspected generative and agentic AI-assisted resume workflows demonstrates an improvement in the speed, scale, and relevance of persona preparation over the past year. Identified application activity indicates that the actor has invested in repeatable tooling to support employment-themed operations, ultimately helping the actor scale application activity and interact more credibly with the hiring processes of impacted organizations. Since August 2026, Microsoft Threat Intelligence has observed Diamond Sleet returning to targeting the oil and gas sector for the first time since 2023, expanding their focus on communications and internet policy-related organizations, and continuing to target defense-related organizations. Further, Diamond Sleet has created fake communications technology companies, complete with supporting websites and tailored personas, to target telecommunications companies under the guise of legitimate business partnerships. These shifts have occurred alongside Diamond Sleet’s increased use of generative AI platforms to support reconnaissance and technical development, suggesting that AI could be improving the efficiency of existing operational workflows. AI-assisted social engineering and identity deception Several North Korean threat actors leverage job-themed social engineering techniques to gain access to target environments. For example, Sapphire Sleet, Jade Sleet, and Coral Sleet use employment- and interview-themed social engineering to target users and organizations, while Jasper Sleet uses fraudulent identities to obtain legitimate remote employment. These actors leverage AI to improve the process of creating convincing lures, aid in the development of fake personas, and evade detection when interacting with targets. Specifically, these actors use AI to: Produce tailored phishing, recruiter, and investment lures Create job-themed payload-delivery content and malicious repository lures Translate content and adjust tone to support foreign-language impersonation and more natural communications Build resumes, biographies, and realistic interview responses Support coding assessments, job-related tasks, and the management of multiple worker personas Enhance profile images and other identity artifacts Alter profile images, video, voice, and backgrounds Sustain recruiter, developer, investor, and other social identities across interactions and platforms Microsoft Threat Intelligence has identified over 100 PDF lure files attributed to Onyx Sleet that were created in August and September 2026 with target-specific details. Metadata indicated that the actor created dozens of files at a time in rapid succession within minutes, highly likely to be the output of an AI platform. This automation supports the actor’s high-volume phishing campaigns against South Korean users and could make disruption and detection efforts more difficult given the rapid regeneration or modification of additional lures. Microsoft Threat Intelligence has also identified possible synthetic media generation, focused on AI-created voice and video, by Emerald Sleet. While we have not been able to determine the full operational impact of such tooling, it is highly likely that synthetic media creation is valued by multiple North Korean threat actors, as it significantly enhances existing social engineering capabilities. AI-assisted software and tooling development Microsoft Threat Intelligence has identified evidence indicating that North Korean threat actors are leveraging AI-enabled software and tools to assist in malware development. These tools include AI-assisted development platforms, such as Cursor, which have likely enabled Jade Sleet, Coral Sleet, Pearl Sleet, and Sapphire Sleet to: Generate, convert, debug, and refactor code Assist with scripts, commands, persistence mechanisms, and obfuscation Troubleshoot errors and help operators diagnose tool behavior Support the adaptation or reimplementation of functionality across languages, libraries, and environments Additionally, AI-enabled development cycles further allow threat actors to circumvent detection and blocking efforts, as AI platforms streamline retooling, helping enable threat actors to rapidly pivot in reaction to defensive measures. For example, Microsoft Threat Intelligence has identified evidence indicating that Sapphire Sleet, who has conducted several supply chain attacks, likely incorporated AI during the August 2026 Rust software compromise. The activity spanned from initial stages targeting Rust developers to development and deployment of malware. Notably, Sapphire Sleet’s malware in this campaign was a marked improvement compared with other attributed campaigns, as the malware used dynamic .NET compilation during post-exploitation, most likely for defense evasion. And, since April 2026, Coral Sleet has operationalized a workflow to trojanize private GitHub repositories, integrating them into their existing malware chain. This new capability, its breadth and scale, and its rapid implementation were highly likely engineered using an AI-enabled development platform. AI-assisted operational scaling and analysis North Korean threat actors are leveraging AI platforms to scale and manage diverse operations. For example, Jasper Sleet’s AI tooling helps maintain consistent communication and performance across multiple fraudulent worker personas. We have also seen evidence of AI use for automating repetitive tasks, summarizing collected information from victim organizations, and accelerating post-compromise decision making while human operators likely continue to direct intent and objectives. This operational adoption extends to emerging agentic AI technologies. Since late October 2026, Microsoft threat Intelligence has identified Onyx Sleet and Pearl Sleet leveraging open-source agentic AI penetration testing ARTEX in operations, highlighting how North Korean threat actors are adopting new AI technologies as they become available. Mitigation and protection guidance Threat actor misuse of AI accelerates risk across enterprise environments by amplifying existing threats such as phishing, malware threats, and insider activity. To help organizations stay ahead of AI-enabled threat activity, Microsoft has introduced the Security Dashboard for AI . The dashboard provides users with a unified view of AI security posture by aggregating security, identity, and data risk across Microsoft Defender , Microsoft Entra , and Microsoft Purview . This allows organizations to understand what AI assets exist in their environment, recognize emerging risk patterns, and prioritize governance and security across AI agents, applications, and platforms. To learn more about the Microsoft Security Dashboard for AI see: Assess your organization’s AI risk with Microsoft Security Dashboard for AI . Additionally, Microsoft Agent 365 serves as a control plane for AI agents in enterprise environments, allowing users to manage, govern, and secure AI agents and workflows while monitoring emerging risks of agentic AI use. Agent 365 supports a growing ecosystem of agents, including Microsoft agents, broader ecosystems of agents such as Adobe and Databricks, and open-source agents published on GitHub. As the AI-enabled operations conducted by North Korean threat actors vary across TTPs, the following guidance can help organizations harden their networks against multiple classes of threats. Vulnerability and exposure management Use Microsoft Defender Vulnerability Management to identify, assess, remediate, and track vulnerabilities across critical assets. Use the exposure score to reduce your vulnerability exposure and the Microsoft Secure Score to identify and remediate security misconfigurations in your environment. Use a perimeter scanning tool like Microsoft Defender External Attack Surface Management to understand your organization’s digital footprint and potential attack surface. Targeting web-facing vulnerabilities continues to be one of the most effective attack methods for initial access. Ensure any web-facing systems are isolated from the public internet with a secure network boundary and access them with a virtual private network (VPN). If certain servers must be accessible on the public internet, position them behind a web application firewall (WAF) , reverse proxy , or perimeter network (also known as a DMZ), which can reduce the risk of vulnerability exploitation in some cases. Consider automating patching where possible. Windows Update client policies offer automated patching, which can greatly reduce the amount of time IT admins must spend identifying, configuring, and deploying updates. To help manage infrastructure distributed across cloud, on-premises, and hybrid networks, use a centralized patching solution like Azure Update Manager . Protecting against social engineering and phishing Educate users about protecting personal and business information on social media, filtering unsolicited communication, identifying lures in spear-phishing emails and watering holes, and reporting reconnaissance attempts and other suspicious activity. Educate users about preventing malware infections , such as ignoring or deleting unsolicited and unexpected emails or attachments sent through instant messaging applications or social networks. Review the recommended settings for Exchange Online Protection and Microsoft Defender for Office 365 to ensure your organization has established essential defenses and knows how to monitor and respond to threat activity. Invest in user awareness training and phishing simulations. Attack simulation training in Microsoft Defender for Office 365, which also includes simulating phishing messages in Microsoft Teams, is one approach to running realistic attack scenarios in your organization. Enable Zero-hour auto purge (ZAP) in Defender for Office 365 to quarantine sent mail in response to newly acquired threat intelligence and retroactively neutralize malicious phishing, spam, or malware messages that have already been delivered to mailboxes. Responders could also manually check for and purge unwanted emails containing URLs and/or Subject fields that are similar, but not identical, to those of known bad messages. Investigate malicious email that was delivered in Microsoft 365 and use Threat Explorer to find and delete phishing emails. Turn on Safe Links and Safe Attachments in Microsoft Defender for Office 365. Only allow device code flow where necessary. Microsoft recommends blocking device code flow wherever possible . Where necessary, configure Microsoft Entra ID’s device code flow in your Conditional Access policies. Vetting potential employees Exercise greater scrutiny for employees of staffing companies, since this is the easiest avenue for North Korean workers to infiltrate target companies. Check to make sure a potential employee’s social media/professional accounts are not highly similar to the accounts of other individuals. In addition, check that a contact phone number listed on a potential employee’s account is unique and not also used by other accounts. Scrutinize resumes and background checks for consistency of names, addresses, and dates. Consider contacting references by phone or video-teleconference rather than email only. Search whether a potential employee is employed at multiple companies using the same persona. Ensure the potential employee is seen on camera during multiple video telecommunication sessions. If the potential employee reports video and/or microphone issues that prohibit participation, this should be considered a red flag. Confirm the potential employee has a digital footprint. This includes a real phone number (not VOIP), a residential address, and social media accounts. EDR in block mode works behind the scenes to remediate malicious artifacts that are detected post-breach. Configure investigation and remediation in full automated mode to let Microsoft Defender for Endpoint take immediate action on alerts to resolve breaches, significantly reducing alert volume. Configure automatic attack disruption in Microsoft Defender XDR. Automatic attack disruption is designed to contain attacks in progress, limit the impact on an organization’s assets, and provide more time for security teams to remediate the attack fully. AES encryption scheme for network traffic All traffic between the malware and the C2 server is encrypted and authenticated using AES‑128‑CBC with HMAC. The encryption and authentication keys are derived from a master key – specified in the configuration as "ec"="9fT04e03tAiZAPogcDWt0w==" – by applying HMAC with distinct seed values. For a given master key and a single-byte seed, the derivation works as follows: Generate a 16‑byte sequence from the seed; this serves as the HMAC key. Compute HMAC‑SHA256 over the master key to obtain an intermediate key. Use this intermediate key as the HMAC key to compute HMAC‑SHA256 on the constant byte 0x01. Take the first 16 bytes of the resulting hash; this becomes the final derived key. Accordingly, the server-side encryption and decryption keys mirror those on the client (malware) side. It uses a randomly generated IV to symmetrically encrypt the plaintext in CBC mode and produces ciphertext. Next, it calculates an SHA256 message authentication code for the concatenated “IV+ciphertext” using a separate HMAC key. Finally, it concatenates the three segments – IV (16 bytes) | ciphertext | HMAC (32 bytes) – into a single byte array that constitutes the final encrypted message. This follows the standard cryptographic Encrypt-then-MAC construction. The receiver must validate the HMAC before decrypting with the corresponding key and IV, which ensures confidentiality, integrity, and tamper resistance. Discovering proxies .NET 2 Plugin detects the system proxy using three methods: Retrieve the system‑wide proxy configuration using the Win32 WinHTTP API function WinHttpGetDefaultProxyConfiguration() , then extract the proxy server with the regular expression (?:([a-zA-Z]+)=)?(.*) . Execute downloaded XAML payload in an isolated AppDomain This technique abuses MulticastDelegate again by using reflection to modify its private _invocationList array. .NET 2 Plugin creates a multicast delegate ( CrossAppDomainDelegate ) and replaces one entry in its invocation list with a malicious delegate pointing to XamlReader.Parse() . When AppDomain.DoCallBack() executes this multicast delegate, it iterates through the invocation list and invokes the injected XamlReader.Parse() to parse and execute the downloaded XAML. In more detail, the process is as follows: Create a new isolated AppDomain with a randomly generated GUID as its name. Copy all configuration data from the current AppDomain into the new one. Use appDomain.DoCallBack() to invoke XamlReader.Parse() within the isolated AppDomain, executing the downloaded XAML payload. Transfer any updated configuration data from the new AppDomain back to the original AppDomain. Unload the isolated AppDomain to complete the cleanup. This technique keeps the downloaded malicious payload out of the main AppDomain, which increases stability and allows the payload to be fully cleaned up after execution. In today’s geopolitical climate, a growing number of countries are building their own core software platforms, including operating systems, databases, middleware, and cloud services, with operating systems often the top priority. The advantage is that each country controls the development, operation, and upgrading of its own operating systems, granting it full autonomy over the entire process. On the other hand, it also means that each country must protect this entire vast ecosystem on its own. Bringing the operation and maintenance of such a system to maturity is a lengthy process. Until then, both the system and its emerging supply chain could be exposed to attacks through multiple vectors, increasing potential vulnerabilities.
