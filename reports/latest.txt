Bring a tent, I've got the coffee: Lessons from it-sa
Cisco Talos | Research / advisory | 2026-11-05T19:00:00+00:00
Welcome to this week's edition of the Threat Source newsletter. I'm just back from an interesting and demanding week of booth duty at it-sa Expo&Congress in Nuremberg, where Talos had a Point of Demonstration at the Cisco booth. I personally see it-sa as the German or European counterpart to the RSA Conference in San Francisco, with a slightly different center of gravity in at least two dimensions. First, the expo is the main event, not the congress. The congress is organized by associations, industry groups, and exhibitors, and plays more of a supporting role. Second, the focus is more local and European. Major topics were (of course) AI for defense tooling, securing AI systems themselves, and post-quantum cryptography. And here comes the local touch (in my opinion): digital sovereignty and regulatory compliance, with NIS2, CRA, and DORA. But local doesn't translate to small, or to the city of Nuremberg. With more than 1,000 exhibitors and 33,000 visitors from 70 countries over three days of exhibition, preceded by a congress day, it was huge. Even as a trade show veteran, I was overwhelmed. My conversations at the booth had a different focus, though. It was a lot of chatter about vulnerabilities like php2shell or broccoli2shell and state-sponsored actors, but again with a local touch: education. I was very surprised how many teenagers stopped by to ask for advice. Here in Germany, you mainly have two paths for your career. One is the university route, and the other is vocational (apprenticeship). Across all industries the split is roughly 50 / 50 , while in IT it is more like 80% university, 20% apprenticeship. The common minimum age to start university is 18, while it is 16 for apprenticeships. Reality is way more complex, but the details don’t matter for the point I want to make. For university opportunities in cyber, I count close to 30 bachelor programs . For apprenticeships, I count ZERO. There is the "IT Specialist" (Fachinformatiker), but it only has specializations like application development, system integration, digital networking, or data and process analysis. That is why an initiative exists to get an "IT Security" specialization going. Even the degree route is thin. There are no official statistics, but my guess is 800 to 900 graduates a year, compared to a demand of more than 100,000. We need a second route into the field for the 16-year-old who finished various CTFs or Hack the Box challenges and wants to join the good side now. Honestly speaking, I would rather have them on our side of the fence at 16 than wonder at 26 where they ended up. How does it look in your country? On a personal note: If you consider visiting it-sa in 2027, book your accommodation early. The hotels are gone fast. That is the one downside, because Nuremberg isn't exactly a city with the infrastructure for more than 30,000 visitors. (Don't ask me how I learned that there are also camping spots close to the exhibition area.) See you in Nuremberg next year. Bring a tent, just in case. I've got the coffee. The one big thing Cisco Talos recently discovered VOLTA , a mature, self-deleting Windows infostealer that uses AI to act as a personal intelligence analyst for cybercriminals. After harvesting a massive haul of credentials, sessions, and cryptocurrency wallets, VOLTA sends a structured evidence bundle to a large language model to rank the victim’s assets by monetization value. The malware even uses stolen session access to enrich this data, searching the victim's emails for purchase receipts to corroborate home addresses. If an AI model refuses to perform the target assessment, VOLTA seamlessly fails over to a multi-model chain to ensure the attackers get their prioritized dossier. Why do I care? VOLTA replaces the manual work of data sorting with automated, AI-driven victim profiling. By letting an LLM determine a target's worth, attackers drastically reduce the human effort required to monetize stolen data. This commoditized threat allows even low-level actors to bypass security measures like Chrome's App-Bound Encryption and receive ranked target assessments, escalating the risk of severe follow-on attacks. So now what? Organizations should implement application control policies to restrict or alert on processes launching Chrome with the --headless flag. Defenders must also enforce best practices for storing credentials in hardware-backed stores. Revoke any potentially compromised tokens and change passwords. For cryptocurrency wallets, if the seed phrase was in plaintext on disk, treat the wallet as fully compromised and migrate funds to a new wallet with a seed generated on an air-gapped device. Threat actors use agentic AI tooling to find and abuse exposed secrets
Microsoft Security | Research / advisory | 2026-11-05T23:46:50+00:00
In this article From exposed secrets to cloud compromise Follow-on actions Mitigating application credential abuse activity Defending against agentic AI attacks Microsoft detections Advanced hunting queries Microsoft Threat Intelligence has observed threat actors using an agentic AI offensive framework to search public GitHub repositories for exposed secrets, including credentials, application credentials, and AI inference-related API keys across multiple applications and cloud services. These threat actors can then use the secrets for further malicious activity against the compromised organization. Searching open code repositories for exposed credentials, secrets, keys, and other authentication material is a well-documented technique that has historically been limited by the time required for human operators to find code repositories, review the data in the repositories, and leverage authentication material for further action. However, agentic tooling like ARTEX allows threat actors to not only scan for secrets across multiple repositories at a quicker pace but also use the agents to analyze the output and conduct follow-on attacks, cutting down on the need for the threat actor to have hands-on-keyboard interactions throughout the attack chain. In at least one case, Microsoft observed an AI agent advance from scanning GitHub for secrets to exfiltrating data from Azure Key Vault in just five minutes. Microsoft has also investigated follow-on activity involving the misuse of valid application credentials. As early as October 28, 2026, Microsoft began observing successful service principal sign-ins from the IP address 199.19.77[.]84 involving multiple application IDs across some impacted customers, including cases where a multitenant application credential was compromised at the source (vendor-owned application) and used to access multiple downstream tenants. The threat actor used the compromised credentials for cloud resource discovery, and credential and data exfiltration, including from Azure Key Vaults and Storage Accounts, and abuse of Azure AI services consistent with the activity described above. Additionally, Microsoft observed malicious activity originating from two IP addresses potentially associated with this campaign. We identified malicious operations from 65.87.223[.]26, including indications of vulnerability exploitation by embedded shell commands in a user agent and attempts to obtain App Service authentication tokens. A similar campaign involving the same IPs addressed was also reported by Todyl . Separately, Microsoft observed reconnaissance operations from 157.22.126[.]128 targeting AI workloads deployed by victims on Azure AI services. Microsoft encourages organizations to scan their code in GitHub and determine where any secrets are present. In addition to removing the secrets from the code base, affected organizations should immediately rotate any exposed keys or credentials. GitHub monitors public repositories by automatically scanning for secrets. In addition to scanning their own repositories, organizations can enable public monitoring through GitHub to detect secrets leaked in secondary public repositories across GitHub. This extends secret scanning beyond the repositories an enterprise owns to follow potential secret exposure across the platform. GitHub Advanced Security for Azure DevOps scans for credentials and other sensitive content in source code. Push protection can also block supported secrets before they’re pushed. Organizations need either GitHub Advanced Security for Azure DevOps or, for organizations using the standalone experience, GitHub Secret Protection for Azure DevOps enabled. Secrets are exposed in public repositories when code containing the secret is committed to GitHub. Developers might store secrets in configuration files, scripts, or other code to quickly operationalize the OAuth application or avoid configuring more modern authentication flows. Organizations can reduce exposure by protecting workload identities and secrets, enforcing least privilege, safeguarding recovery resources, and enabling relevant Microsoft Defender for Cloud protections. Publicly exposed credentials remain usable until revoked or rotated; removing the original disclosure alone does not remediate the exposure. Microsoft recommends that developers migrate OAuth applications away from secret-based authentication . While storing long-lived application client secrets in code is a common practice, using managed identities for application authentication eliminates the possibility of secret exposure in code, manual processes associated with secret management, and barriers to securely scaling applications. This activity illustrates an ongoing trend of threat actors accelerating all stages of the attack chain with agentic AI penetration testing frameworks, including ARTEX. As these capabilities evolve, defenders must similarly use AI to investigate and respond across large environments. From exposed secrets to cloud compromise Starting in August 2026, Microsoft observed threat actors using an agentic AI offensive framework to submit large volumes of queries to GitHub for secrets. The queries submitted by the AI framework often included the name of a target organization, keywords for secrets, a keyword linked to cloud services, or a keyword linked to another AI service provider. Organizations that find their secrets exposed in public repositories must assume that the exposed secrets and associated applications are compromised. Follow-on actions Credential abuse via Entra ID In one follow-on attack that began as early as October 28, 2026, an agentic framework used valid leaked or stolen application credentials to access Microsoft Entra ID tenants of certain organizations and connected Azure and Microsoft 365 resources. The sign-ins primarily came from two IP addresses: 199.19.77[.]84 and 199.19.77[.]18. The latter IP address is a Tor exit node, which might be associated with unrelated malicious activity, including from unrelated sources. The agent successfully conducted service principal sign-ins on dozens of associated applications across multiple tenants.. Service principals are the identities that applications use to authenticate and give direct, programmatic access to whatever the application is authorized to reach. The threat actor targeted operations that return credentials, including storage account keys, App Service settings, connection strings and publishing credentials, AI and machine learning (ML) connections and datastores, and Azure Kubernetes Service (AKS) user credentials. The compromised applications subsequently accessed SharePoint and OneDrive in the targeted organization tenants, including file downloads and mailbox-item access. In some tenants, the threat actor sent Microsoft Graph API requests, which were mostly searches to find content of interest. The threat actor enumerated subscriptions, resource groups, permissions, AI model deployments, and service configurations in Azure to map the environment. Additionally, the threat actor attempted to validate stolen Azure resources, including compromised Azure AI services, as identified using Defender for Cloud’s AI threat protection. Observed activity suggests that the threat actor might have attempted to abuse some of these compromised AI resources to facilitate their broader attack activity. Figure 1. Tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) observed across the credential abuse attack chain Cloud API adjustments indicative of adaptive behavior Additionally in this attack the agent’s operations on a victim’s Azure Resource Manager (ARM) instance originating from 199.19.77[.]84 demonstrated sequences consistent with adaptive troubleshooting, where failed cloud API requests were followed by changes to HTTP methods or API versions and subsequent HTTP 200 responses: A batch of ARM requests attempting to enumerate secret metadata across multiple Azure Key Vault resources returned HTTP 400 (Bad Request). These requests specified a version associated with Key Vault’s data-plane APIs against ARM (control-plane) APIs. Approximately 28 seconds after the first failed response, a batch rerun began against the same vault resource paths using the ARM API version 2023-07-01 and returned HTTP 200. A GET request to enumerate an Azure SQL server’s firewall rules specified an outdated API version and returned HTTP 400. Approximately 60 seconds after that request, a matching GET began using a supported version and subsequently returned HTTP 200. The URL changed only in its api-version parameter. An ARM POST request to an Application Insights query endpoint returned HTTP 400 because an outdated API version was specified. Approximately 80 seconds after the failed request, another POST began against the same endpoint, specifying a supported API version, and subsequently returned HTTP 200. OAuth application abuse In multiple cases, Microsoft observed the same agentic AI tools subsequently obtain a token for a single-tenant OAuth application in the same target organization, then use the compromised OAuth application to enumerate other cloud resources within the organization. In some cases, the agents exfiltrated secrets from the organization’s Azure Key Vault. The agents once authenticated have also searched Azure DevOps for secrets. Until the secrets are revoked or rotated, they can allow a threat actor to maintain persistence in the organization, compromise additional identities, or hijack resources. In one observed case, an AI agent progressed from searching for the client secrets in GitHub to exfiltrating data from Azure Key Vault in five minutes. The agents also used the compromised OAuth application to use the Run Command , which runs scripts in Azure VMs. With access to this functionality on a VM, a threat actor can access unsecured credentials and other stored data, compromise associated workload identities, or run code that provides persistence or lateral movement. During this activity, the AI agents repeatedly demonstrated a focus on credentials that provide access to AI services, such as API keys for major AI service providers and OAuth applications with access to Foundry Tools (previously known as Azure Cognitive Services). Multiple public reports have documented threat actors using agentic AI tools to compromise credentials for AI services, sometimes to run the same agentic AI workflow. Mitigating application credential abuse activity Microsoft recommends the following mitigation steps to contain, detect, and investigate this threat. Containment Block 199.19.77[.]84/32 as a named location in a Conditional Access policy targeting workload identities, assigned directly to service principals, and in a user-scoped block policy to cover replayed sessions. Note that workload identity policies require Workload ID Premium licensing and cover only single-tenant apps registered in your tenant. Block the exact IP rather than the whole ASN. For affected users, revoke Entra sessions, Azure DevOps PATs and SSH keys. For any affected application, remove all client secrets, certificates and federated credentials, including those added by the threat actor and disable the service principal if needed. Tokens already issued remain valid until expiry. Rotate everything stored in any Key Vault the affected application could read, and any credentials found in accessed documents. MSPs: Review Partner Center access, GDAP relationships, and customer tenants, and notify affected customers per your agreements. Detection and hunting Review Entra service principal and non-interactive sign-in logs for 199.19.77[.]84 and 199.19.77[.]18 including failed attempts. Sign-in errors AADSTS7000215 (invalid secret) or AADSTS7000222 (expired secret) indicate someone holds a credential for your app; a failure followed by a success is urgent. Review Azure Activity, Key Vault and Storage diagnostics, Entra audit, Azure DevOps audit and Microsoft 365 unified audit logs for the address, including secret reads, blob and file downloads, and changes to apps, permissions or pipelines. In Azure DevOps auditing, review PAT creation and first use, federated service connections, pipeline runs and edits, and unfamiliar user agents. Enable auditing if it is off. Look for behavior as well as the IP: one address authenticating to three or more service principals with mixed success, or one app requesting tokens across Azure Resource Manager, Key Vault, Storage and Graph in a short window. Search as far back as retention allows. Defending against agentic AI attacks Threat actors can and do change the source IP address, user agent strings, and other fingerprints left by the agentic AI penetration testing tools they use. This can make identifying and blocking malicious network traffic early difficult for defenders. Several behavioral signals can help organizations determine that they are being targeted by threat actors using agentic AI. Agentic AI frameworks scanning a target send a wide variety of requests in a brief time and retry variations on the same technique or move on to next-stage actions at machine speed. Agentic AI frameworks use existing penetration testing tools. For example, ARTEX by default uses Nmap, curl, wget, Python HTTP clients, Norma SDK WebFetch, and headless Chromium through Playwright, among others. These tools may send network traffic with distinctive characteristics, such as default user agent strings. While blocking network traffic with these characteristics will not stop an attack by an AI agent, which will quickly change them, identifying them can alert defenders to the activity. Defensive honeypots , decoys, tarpits, and fake wins can pull autonomous AI attacks away from legitimate targets. Honeypots and decoy surfaces such as Microsoft Sentinel Deception give the agent attractive but worthless targets to pursue. Tarpits consume the agent’s time and budget. Fake wins present convincing evidence that the agent’s objective is complete, which can cause it to stop on a false success. In Microsoft’s testing, these controls helped reduce the attack traffic reaching real assets and produced high-confidence, attribution-quality signal. Microsoft recommends the following mitigations to reduce the impact of this threat. Protect and continuously assess application credentials and secrets. Avoid storing service principal credentials, storage keys, connection strings, and other secrets in source code, configuration files, public repositories, issues, or other locations where they might be inadvertently exposed. To find credentials in source code, consider using tools like GitHub Advanced Security or other OWASP source code analysis tools . Rotate compromised or exposed credentials immediately and establish credential lifecycle practices. Treat credentials that have been publicly exposed as compromised, even if the original location has subsequently been edited or deleted. Removing the content does not invalidate the credential or eliminate copies retained in edit history, caches, archives, logs, or other systems. Immediately revoke or rotate the affected credentials and investigate their historical use. Where supported, organizations should favor mechanisms that reduce reliance on long-lived credentials. Learn more about protecting secrets with Defender for Cloud . Enable appropriate Microsoft Defender for Cloud plans for critical Azure workloads. Consider enabling workload protections relevant to the resources in your environment, including Defender for Resource Manager, Defender for Storage, Defender for Key Vault, Defender for App Service, and Defender for Databases. Learn more in the Microsoft Defender for Cloud overview . Apply least privilege to service principals and other workload identities. Review the Azure role-based access control (RBAC) permissions assigned to service principals and restrict their privileges to the resources and operations required by their applications. Learn more about best practices for Azure RBAC . Protect backup and recovery infrastructure as part of resilience. Restrict access to backup and recovery resources and closely monitor attempts to modify or remove their protection controls. Learn more about Azure Backup security best practices . Hardening OAuth applications against compromise involves managing the permissions of the applications in your organization and securing application secrets. To triage applications in your environment, enforce security policies with new applications as they are created, and address existing applications over time beginning with the most recent ones. Identify and catalog applications running in your cloud environment. The Cloud app catalog page in Defender for Cloud Apps is one service that can help accomplish this. Use Microsoft Defender for Cloud Apps Cloud Discovery tools to identify unmanaged apps, and prioritize the most frequently used apps to migrate and integrate. Use Conditional Access policies for workload identities . Consider augmenting identity signals with workload identity risk protection . Set permission restrictions to limit who can create an application in the tenant. This can be achieved by setting the ability for users to register apps to “No” and limiting the application developer role to privileged users. Review the list of least privileged roles by task to help scope role-based access control in your environment. Integrate enterprise applications with Microsoft Entra ID . New investments to reduce on-premises security risk
Microsoft Security | Research / advisory | 2026-11-05T17:10:14+00:00
On-premises servers can be essential to business operations, but they are increasingly attractive targets to attackers. AI can help less-skilled actors analyze software, develop malicious tools, and repeat attack workflows at greater speed and scale, shortening the window between exposure and exploitation. Organizations need to strengthen security faster without disrupting the critical workloads they rely on. Today, we’re announcing a new on-premises security initiative, initially focused on server workloads. Email threat landscape: Q3 2026 trends and insights
Microsoft Security | Research / advisory | 2026-11-05T16:00:00+00:00
In this article Spoofed sender domains QR code phishing attacks CAPTCHA-gated phishing tactics Malicious payloads Business contact impersonation Microsoft Teams threats Notable phishing campaigns Defending against email and collaboration threats Hunting queries Indicators of compromise (IOCs) Microsoft Threat Intelligence detected approximately 7.3 billion email-based phishing threats from July through September, including a sharp increase to nearly 3 billion in September alone, up 41% from August. Yet the increase did not extend across the techniques that had driven threat actor activity earlier in the year. QR code phishing remained far below its early-year peak, while CAPTCHA-gated phishing reversed several months of decline, driven in part by renewed use of SVG attachments. Q2 in review Shifting tactics and other trends › Figure 1. Monthly detected email threat volume (January – September 2026) The quarter also highlighted how sender identity and security exceptions can shape the outcome of detected threats. Microsoft detected approximately 129 million malicious messages spoofing the sender domains of tracked business services. Many were delivered because organization- or user-configured exceptions overrode security protections, even after the messages failed authentication checks and were identified as malicious, showing how overly broad exceptions intended to preserve legitimate workflows can also expose them to impersonation. In Microsoft Teams, attackers increasingly used ordinary personal names rather than explicit help desk identities while continuing to use live interactions for social engineering. Across malicious attachment activity more broadly, credential theft remained the dominant objective in Q3. Two campaigns observed during the quarter illustrate the range of activity behind these trends. An August campaign targeting legal organizations used personalized salary review documents and QR codes to lead recipients into device code phishing , despite the broader decline in QR code attacks. In September, purchase order and payroll lures used disguised archives to deliver Agent Tesla, malware commonly used to collect credentials and other information. Together, the campaigns show how familiar business requests remained effective lures across distinct attack paths. TYCOON2FA Inside the PhaaS platform › The effects of the March disruption of the Tycoon2FA phishing-as-a-service (PhaaS) platform also remained evident. By July, Microsoft detected approximately 21,000 phishing messages linked to the platform, down 99.8% from 8.9 million in February, the last full month before the operation. Figure 2. Tycoon2FA-linked phishing email volume (January 2026 – July 2026) This blog provides a view of email threat activity across Q3 2026, examining shifts in phishing techniques, malicious payloads, business contact impersonation (BCI), and Teams-based threats. We also highlight spoofed business-service sender domains and two campaigns that show how attackers used familiar work requests to make malicious messages appear credible. Together with recommendations, Microsoft Defender detections, and hunting queries, these findings can help defenders assess where existing protections may be weakened and investigate activity across email and collaboration platforms. Spoofed sender domains Requests to sign a document, review an invoice, or respond to a survey are routine business communications. Attackers exploit that familiarity by spoofing the sender domains of the legitimate services that deliver them. Rather than relying only on a misleading display name or a lookalike domain, these messages claim the service’s actual domain as their sender identity. Microsoft detected approximately 129 million malicious messages spoofing the business-service domains tracked in this analysis during Q3. Activity had remained consistently high since at least May, at roughly 41 million to 46 million messages a month. DocuSign was the most frequently spoofed brand in September, accounting for 29% of that month’s total. Adobe, SurveyMonkey, and Barracuda also saw increases toward the end of the quarter, even as overall spoofing volume remained broadly steady. Figure 3. Malicious messages spoofing tracked business-service sender domains (May – September 2026) The more consequential finding was how security exceptions affected these messages after detection. Despite failing email authentication checks and being identified as malicious, many were associated with tenant- or user-level overrides. In September, overrides applied to 63% of messages with a recorded security outcome, with tenant-level overrides accounting for most of them. The figures do not establish that recipients opened the messages or that accounts were compromised. They do show why exception policies deserve attention alongside detection coverage. An organization may create an exception to avoid interrupting a legitimate business workflow, but an overly broad exception can also weaken protection against messages impersonating that workflow. Familiar branding is not a substitute for an authenticated sender. QR code phishing attacks QR code phishing ended Q3 far below the levels seen during its early-year surge. Microsoft detected approximately 12.1 million attacks over the quarter, down 67% from Q2. The decline continued through September despite the broader increase in email threats, leaving monthly QR code volume 86% below its March peak. Figure 4. Monthly QR code phishing volume (January – September 2026) Much of the volume reduction came from the attachments that had supported that earlier growth: PDF attachments remained the most common delivery format in Q3, accounting for 78% of QR code attacks in July and August before dropping to 61% in September. Nonetheless, the volume of PDFs carrying malicious QR codes fell by roughly 65% by the end of the quarter. DOC/DOCX attachments accounted for 14% of QR code attacks in July and August before rising to 20% in September. Word document delivery volume similarly contracted in Q3, falling 39% between July and September following a sharp drop at the start of the quarter. Email-embedded QR codes increased from 5% of attacks in July to 12% in September. After dropping to near-zero in Q2, the volume of malicious QR codes embedded in email bodies more than tripled by the end of Q3, although the method remained a smaller delivery category than attachments. Figure 5. QR code phishing delivery method share by month (January – September 2026) The distinction between delivery methods matters for inspection coverage, but not for the recipient’s experience: either attachment or embedded formats can move a user from a message on a managed device to a phishing page on a personal phone. The reduction in volume has not changed that underlying challenge. CAPTCHA-gated phishing tactics CAPTCHA-gated phishing began to recover while QR code attacks continued to fall. By September, Microsoft detected 3.1 million CAPTCHA-gated attacks, nearly twice the volume seen at July’s low. Activity remained well below its March peak, but the recovery ended several months of sustained decline. Figure 6. CAPTCHA-gated phishing volume (January – September 2026) The increase in CAPTCHA-gated phishing attacks was not confined to a single attachment format as delivery methods shifted throughout Q3, though SVG attachments were central to the initial recovery. SVG attachment volume increased sixfold in August, accounting for almost half of September’s total CAPTCHA-gated phishing attacks. Up from 17% of attacks in July, SVGs represented 65% of CAPTCHA-gated phishing attacks in August, remained elevated at 49% in September, and were the leading delivery method at the quarter end. Email-embedded URLs accounted for 49% of attacks in July before falling to 19% in August as SVG activity surged. URL-delivered attacks more than doubled in September, reclaiming 30% of attacks. PDF attachments declined from 22% of attacks in July to 12% in August, remaining at 12% in September. HTML attachments declined from 9% of attacks in July to 2% in August, then more than tripled in volume in September to account for 6% of attacks. DOC/DOCX attachments remained comparatively uncommon throughout Q3, representing only 1% of attacks each month. Figure 7. CAPTCHA-gated phishing distribution method share by month (April – September 2026) Malicious payloads The dominant risk from malicious attachments continued to be credential theft, not malware installation. Approximately 94-96% of payload-based attacks during Q3 directed recipients toward a phishing site or presented a fraudulent sign-in page locally. An attachment did not need to install software to put an account at risk. HTML and PDF attachments remained the two most common malicious payload types during Q3, together accounting for approximately 56–62% of payload-based attacks each month, while the more significant change was the return of SVG files: Figure 8. Malicious payloads by type HTML attachments accounted for nearly two-thirds of malicious payloads and remained relatively steady across the quarter. HTML files ranked first in July at 32% of attacks before declining to 26% in August and recovering to 29% in September. PDF attachments accounted for 27–30% of attacks and were the leading payload type in August and September. Volume remained comparatively stable, declining only 2% between July and September. SVG attachments increased from 13% of attacks in July to 20% in August, when volume reached 10.1 million—the highest monthly volume in more than a year. Volume then fell 34% in September, although SVGs still represented 13% of attacks and remained roughly 50% higher than the monthly average for the first half of 2026. DOC/DOCX attachments declined from approximately 5% of attacks in July and August to less than 3% in September, falling 76% below its monthly average volume for the first half of 2026. ZIP/GZIP attachments declined from 5% of attacks in July to 3% in August before recovering slightly to 4% in September. Raw volume remained 24% below July. The reduced use of Word documents alongside the renewed use of SVG files left a different mix of attachment threats, though credential theft remained the dominant objective. Figure 9. Malicious payload file type distribution by month (June – September 2026) CAPTCHA-gated phishing accounted for only part of the malicious SVG activity. The resurgence was therefore broader than the use of SVG files to deliver that particular technique. Business contact impersonation Microsoft defines business contact impersonation (BCI) as a text-based attack targeting enterprise users that impersonates a trusted entity for the purpose of persuading a recipient into initiating a fraudulent financial transaction or sending the attacker sensitive documents. BCI volume declined throughout Q3, totaling approximately 6.2 million attacks. That was substantially below Q2, although the comparison is influenced by the unusually large spike in April. By September, monthly activity had fallen to its lowest level since at least July 2025. Figure 10. Monthly BCI attack volume (January – September 2026) The reduction in overall volume also concealed differences among financial lures. Generic messages continued to dominate initial BCI contact during Q3, but explicit task and financial requests gained share as overall attack volume declined: Generic outreach messages , such as “Are you at your desk?”, accounted for 82% of attacks in July and August before declining to 79% in September. Generic task requests represented 8–10% of attacks during Q3, increasing from 9% in July to 10% in September despite a 9% decline in raw volume. Within the smaller subset of explicit financial requests: Payroll update requests were the most common financial lure, accounting for 6% of attacks in July and August and 7% in September. Raw volume remained broadly stable at approximately 126,000–130,000 attacks per month. Invoice payment requests recovered from June’s low. Volume increased 34% in July, 25% in August, and 89% in September, more than tripling between June and September. Their share reached 3% in September, its highest level since March. Initial BCI email content by type (Q3 2026) The decline in BCI overall did not extend equally to the requests reaching payment and payroll staff. Activity peaked in July before declining 31% by September, although September volume remained 38% above the monthly average for the first half of 2026. Teams-based attacks continued to use live interactions across chat, voice, screen sharing, and remote-support tools to facilitate remote access, credential theft, and hands-on-keyboard compromise. Malicious Teams call impersonation comparison (Q3 2026) This shift does not necessarily mean attackers abandoned IT support stories. A personal name can accompany a claim to work for the help desk, while the underlying account address retains technical or service-related wording. Notable phishing campaigns The following campaigns were observed during the quarter and highlight notable credential phishing, BCI, and malware delivery activity. For analysis of a separate Teams-based IT-support impersonation campaign observed in August, see Impersonating IT support: Threat actors turn remote session into enterprise-wide access . Salary review lure delivers personalized QR code attachment for device code credential capture targeting legal organizations On August 19, 2026, Microsoft Defender Research observed a phishing campaign targeting more than 10,000 users across more than 4,800 organizations, almost exclusively located in the United States. While most phishing attacks typically don’t focus on a single vertical, this campaign targeted the professional services sector, specifically law firms and legal organizations. Messages in this campaign were sent out over a 13-hour period between 13:27 UTC on August 19, 2026, and 2:59 UTC on August 20, 2026. Figure 14. Intended campaign targets by industry Emails in this campaign were sent with the subject line “Salary Review” from smtp[@]wekash[.]net via Amazon Simple Email Service. The email body carried no visible text and the sole payload was a Word document attachment named Your-Q3-Compensation-Summary.docx . Figure 15. Sample of the campaign document attachment The Word document impersonated the recipient’s own organization, displaying company branding and addressing the recipient by name. The message claimed that the recipient’s compensation had been revised following a Q2 performance review and that updated details were ready for review. A QR code in the document carried an instruction to scan it to access the revised details. This approach moved the phishing URL out of the email body and into an image, bypassing email gateway controls that scan text-based links but do not decode QR code images. Scanning the QR code would have directed users to an attacker-controlled phishing page impersonating a Microsoft OneDrive sign-in portal hosted on the domain splace[.]co[.]za . The page presented an alphanumeric code and instructed recipients to copy it and proceed to the Microsoft sign-in portal. Figure 16. Sample phishing landing page The phishing page used a device code phishing technique, which works by making an authentication request to Microsoft’s device authentication service before the recipient arrived, receiving a user code in return, and embedding that code into the page. When a recipient copied the code and proceeded, the page redirected them to the legitimate Microsoft device authentication portal at microsoft.com/devicelogin , where they entered the code and signed in. Completing that sign in would have transferred a valid session token to the attacker. The token granted persistent access to the recipient’s Microsoft 365 account without ever exposing the password and without re-triggering multifactor authentication (MFA). A countdown timer on the page signaled that each code had a limited validity window, pressing recipients to act quickly. Spanish-language purchase order and payroll campaign leads to Agent Tesla malware On September 21, 2026, Microsoft Defender Research observed a phishing campaign targeting more than 56,000 users across more than 8,100 organizations. The campaign primarily targeted users in Spanish-speaking countries, with Colombia accounting for the largest share of intended recipients (15%), followed by the United States (12%), Mexico (12%), Chile (11%), and Spain (8%). Like most large-scale phishing attacks, this campaign did not focus on a single vertical, but instead targeted a broad range of industries, most notably retail and consumer goods (27%), manufacturing and agriculture (19%), and financial services (13%). Messages in this campaign were sent out over an almost nine-hour period between 14:57 UTC and 23:35 UTC on September 21, 2026. Figure 17. Timeline of campaign message volume Two lure variations were observed throughout the duration of the campaign: a purchase order theme, which was used in 63% of messages, and a payroll theme, which was used in the remaining 37%. Both variations were sent from the address contacto06[@]carloxmextos[.]org . Sender Policy Framework (SPF), DomainKeys Identified Mail (DKIM), and Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance (DMARC) all passed for that domain, so the messages did not depend on simple address forgery. The actor changed the display name to resemble a sales or human resources identity associated with the recipient’s organization. That combination gave the messages technically valid authentication while using the visible sender identity and familiar business context to support the impersonation. The purchase order lure variation used the reply-style subject “Re: solicitud de cotización”. Its body said, “Buen Dia”, claimed that a purchase order was attached, and asked the recipient to confirm receipt before closing with the lowercase “saludos”. The abrupt wording, inconsistent capitalization, and copied professional signature made the message look like a short continuation of an existing commercial exchange while avoiding details that a recipient could easily challenge. The attached file, Nuevo orden de compra – 21092026.pdf.uu , was a RAR archive despite its uncommon .uu ending and PDF-like name. Inside it was Nuevo orden de compra – 21092026.pdf.js , a JavaScript downloader whose second extension exposed the executable script type only after extraction. If run, the script fetched a file named Lincoln.ps1 from a campaign-controlled object in Firebase Storage, and that PowerShell payload led to Agent Tesla malware. The nested archive and misleading extensions separated the visible business document theme from the code that executed, while the cloud-hosted download gave the actor a remotely accessible staging point. Figure 18. Redacted rendering of the purchase order lure email The payroll-themed lure used the subject “Nomina 21.09.2026”. It addressed the recipient by name, said the September 21 payroll list was attached, and signed off as a human resources manager. The date-specific payroll pretext created an immediate work-related reason to open the file, while the personalized greeting made a bulk message appear locally relevant. The payroll lure’s attachment, Nomina 21.09.2026.xml.001 , was also a RAR archive. Extraction produced Nomina 21.09.2026.xml.exe , an Agent Tesla executable disguised with an XML-themed double extension. Unlike the purchase order delivery chain, this branch did not require a separate downloader in the supplied evidence, but both paths used the same sender infrastructure, attachment camouflage, date, and malware family. Agent Tesla is commonly used to collect credentials and other information from an infected host, so the shared final payload suggested that credential and information theft were the campaign’s likely objective. The SOC Files 5: Ghost in the pipeline, or a fileless SharePoint breach by Storm-2603
Securelist | Research / advisory | 2026-11-05T10:00:11+00:00
Executive summary In 2025, several threat actors exploited CVE-2025-53770 and CVE-2025-53771 (ToolShell vulnerabilities) in on-premises Microsoft SharePoint to achieve remote code execution through crafted HTTP requests. One of the threat actors deployed ransomware on the target’s systems after successful exploitation. Microsoft attributed this ransomware-related ToolShell activity to Storm-2603, distinguishing it from other actors whose observed activity was primarily focused on credential theft, espionage, and data exfiltration. In December, our retrospective analysis identified activity consistent with Storm-2603’s TTPs. This activity involved the attacker using the IIS worker process as an in-memory bridge to the customer’s infrastructure, keeping the access and control layer entirely within w3wp.exe without writing anything to disk. Although subsequent post-exploitation activity generated disk artifacts, the bridge used to maintain access remained memory-resident. Let’s look in more detail at this attack. The attack chain, from reconnaissance to infrastructure compromise Intrusion timeline: Strategic latency and exploitation The intrusion analyzed in this report occurred in two stages, with a temporal gap between initial access and subsequent exploitation activity. The attack timeline was as follows: Dec 20 – Vulnerability fingerprinting: A single HTTP request targeted a SharePoint administrative endpoint associated with the ToolPane component to determine whether the host was vulnerable to CVE-2025-53770. Dec 21-23 – Tactical latency: A three-day period of silence followed the initial probe, separating the vulnerability fingerprinting from subsequent malicious activity and potentially reducing the likelihood of immediate detection. Dec 24 – High-intensity re-engagement: On Christmas Eve, when security staffing at many companies is reduced, the full RCE chain was executed through the IIS worker process (w3wp.exe) on the same endpoint as the initial probe. Dec 26 – Incident discovery: The three-day gap initially prevented the December 20 probe from being correlated with subsequent malicious activity, marking the event as low priority. Retrospective analysis established that the probe was the initial phase of the subsequent intrusion. Intrusion timeline Initial access validation phase Vulnerability fingerprinting A vulnerability fingerprinting request on December 20 marked the first observed interaction with the target. The request originated from infrastructure associated with ONYPHE, an internet-scale attack surface discovery and management platform for identifying exposed assets, vulnerabilities, and cyberthreats. Unlike widely used tools such as Shodan and Censys, which merely provide a list of internet-facing assets correlated with the open-source data about known vulnerabilities, ONYPHE actually checks if the target system is vulnerable. Fingerprinting with ONYPHE The request characteristics were consistent with ONYPHE’s vulnerability checks, which use active, non-intrusive probes to identify internet-exposed systems vulnerable to specific CVEs. For ToolShell, ONYPHE adopted the approach used by Niels Hofmans (hazcod). Namely, it attempts to inject a harmless marker into the target server to find out if it is vulnerable. OFFICIAL ONYPHE CVE CHECKER (TOOLSHELL) This phase served as a validation step against the SharePoint execution surface exposed over HTTP: The target processed structured requests through the ASP.NET pipeline without rejection. The request path reached application logic capable of handling serialized input. No defensive controls interrupted or altered the execution flow at the application boundary. Approximately 12 minutes later, a second inbound connection originated from infrastructure associated with Pfcloud UG, a provider operating under a bulletproof hosting (BPH) model. Given the timing and the IP’s documented history of scanning and abusive activity, the connection may represent follow-on reconnaissance against the newly identified target. No exploitation was attempted at this stage. A connection from an IP address belonging to Pfcloud UG BPH Active exploitation: Santa took your data and left a backdoor The main malicious activity occurred on December 24. The actor targeted the same SharePoint host with a crafted request from 45.91.94.204 (45.91.92.0/22, ASN 63473, HostHatch, LLC). This resulted in a webshell execution within the IIS worker process (w3wp.exe). The payload was not written to disk; rather, it was loaded directly into the ASP.NET runtime and used during subsequent request processing. The implant remained resident for the lifetime of the affected application domain. This runtime state was lost when the application pool was recycled or IIS was restarted. Attack originated from an SPN node The IP address 45.91.94.204 is a Safing Privacy Network (SPN) node. SPN routes each connection through a multilayer VPN path with multiple relay nodes that can obscure the originating IP. SPN promotes itself as a network that offers strong privacy protection for internet traffic. Its infrastructure resembles Tor’s in many ways, but with some differences. It features the following capabilities: Speed: Near-VPN performance for high-speed data transfer. Layer 3 support: Supports multiple protocols like TCP, UDP, ICMP (unlike Tor, which only supports TCP). App-routing: Direct tunneling without a browser. Multi-identity: Simultaneous exit nodes to bypass IP rate limits. Fixed-size packets and padding make it harder to analyze its traffic. It is less common than Tor, and frequently bypasses standard Tor blocklists. Webshell architecture The webshell the attackers injected into the w3wp.exe runtime is a multi-module in-memory framework, where execution is orchestrated through the main component (G.cs) and extended via multiple functional units across namespaces. The implementation follows a modular C# project structure in which each capability is isolated into its own compilation unit and invoked dynamically at runtime. Webshell components “G.cs” pipeline control and C2 Pipeline control The main webshell component is G.cs. It orchestrates the implant’s execution context, lifecycle, C2 and session management. To ensure persistence within the runtime, G.cs performs the following actions: Registers a custom VirtualPathProvider using the non-public RegisterVirtualPassProviderInternal method from the ASP.NET HostingEnvironment class so that ASP.NET uses it for virtual-path processing. The provider is automatically added to the chain of registered virtual path providers. Delegates the provider’s .NET Remoting lifetime policy to the previous provider in the chain, preserving the existing lifetime behavior. Additionally, to evade defenses and enable the VirtualPathProvider to be registered even if the application in whose AppDomain the webshell is running is precompiled, the module implements the trust control subversion (T1553) technique: Uses reflection to check if the application is precompiled. Extracts pointers for internal state fields for the BuildManager class, which manages application compilation, and modifies them at runtime. Uses reflection once again to force runtime injection. G.cs uses the GetCacheKey() function as the interception point for the virtual path processing flow. It processes incoming requests to the SharePoint server and checks their headers to filter out requests from the attacker. The specific User-Agent in the request headers triggers the webshell execution. If the request doesn’t match this condition, the implant remains dormant, making its traffic harder to recognize. Command and control layer (in-memory transport) The same core module (G.cs) implements the communication layer. Instead of relying on external frameworks or standard beacons, it uses a custom binary protocol with XOR-based obfuscation to exchange data with the C2. These communications blend into normal application traffic. The module uses the GetCacheKey() hook to intercept standard HTTP requests. When a specific User-Agent is detected in the request headers, the module parses it, extracts the payload, and establishes a TCP connection to a target host inside the target’s network if needed. Bidirectional TCP tunnel initialization G.cs establishes a full-duplex proxy tunnel to support interactive control. It stores active target sockets in memory to pair concurrent HTTP read and write channels. TCP connections from the C2 server to arbitrary hosts inside the target network are supported for pivoting and lateral movement. The traffic is proxied through the SharePoint host. Target-to-attacker data channel Attacker-to-target data channel As can be seen in the screenshots, the attackers used the suo5 tool to implement traffic proxying. Its authors promote it as a stable forward-proxying tool for scenarios where outbound internet connections are prohibited. To increase its performance, G.cs manipulates ThreadPool by raising its limits for concurrent operations and maximum timeout. ThreadPool manipulation “Prog.cs”: Execution entry point and task dispatcher Prog.cs is the entry point that receives command-line arguments and passes them to uk/London.cs. The latter acts as the orchestrator, handling command parsing and privilege escalation (via named pipe impersonation). We’ll review it in more detail later. If the “suo5” command is provided, London.cs triggers G.cs to hook into the ASP.NET runtime as a memory shell. Once active, G.cs independently processes incoming web traffic inside the web server memory. Host interaction modules The following modules provide direct system interaction capabilities, each implemented as a separate unit. London.cs triggers these modules when it receives a corresponding command from Prog.cs. “FileDownloader.cs” The FileDownloader.cs module downloads arbitrary files from external URLs provided by the C2. It stores the downloaded files in the Program Data directory without any user interaction. “LoggedOnUsers.cs” This module enumerates logged-on users and identifies their session types (interactive, service, remote). The results are stored in memory. The attackers can retrieve them using the proxy and tunneling capabilities provided by G.cs. “ProcessList.cs” This module lists running processes with full paths to the corresponding executables on the disk. The results are stored in memory and the attackers can retrieve them via G.cs. “NetworkInfo.cs” This module extracts information about existing network connections, including protocols, local and remote IPs and ports, and connection state. It also enumerates network interfaces and headers. The results are stored in memory and can be retrieved by the attackers via G.cs. “MSI.cs” This module silently installs and executes MSI packages previously downloaded by FileDownloader.cs. “User.cs” This module creates administrative accounts using the native SAM RPC subsystem (SAMRPCNative namespace) rather than standard command-line tooling. This may indicate an attempt to evade detection and provide the attackers with privileged stealthy access to target systems that persists after the webshell has finished its work. SAMRPCNative The SAMRPCNative namespace implements a low-level C# P/Invoke wrapper for the native Windows samlib.dll library, comprising three types: SAMStructs.cs, SAMMethods.cs, and SAMEnums.cs. “SAMEnums.cs” Implements structured bitwise lists and constant flags mapping numeric codes to their security meaning in SAM, and translates system status return codes. “SAMStructs.cs” Defines native data structures required for P/Invoke memory alignment and for marshalling. It securely formats and aligns data structures. “SAMMethods.cs” Contains external API declarations (P/Invoke) imported from samlib.dll to perform actions such as connecting to the SAM server, creating users and opening domains. This layer is crucial for the attackers because it enables account operations. Privilege escalation The uk namespace contains offensive primitives for privilege escalation. To elevate privileges to SYSTEM, it uses a local token impersonation technique via the Encrypting File System Remote Protocol (EFSRPC) interface adopted from the EfsPotato tool. This functionality is implemented in the uk/London class. It creates a malicious named pipe while the EfsrTiny class (a tiny version of the EfsPotato tool) connects to the LSASS.exe RPC server. Then it’s London.RpcThread function calls the EfsRpcEncryptFileSrv function from EfsrTiny with a crafted \\localhost path. This tricks LSASS.exe into connecting back to the attacker’s named pipe. Finally, the London.Exp function uses ImpersonateNamedPipeClient to coerce and impersonate the LSASS.exe token and spawn a new process via CreateProcessAsUser with SYSTEM privileges. As the webshell resides in the IIS worker process, it has the necessary SeImpersonatePrivilege to perform this technique. The uk namespace also implements a minimal MSRPC client layer by explicitly defining structures such as RPC_CLIENT_INTERFACE and MIDL_STUB_DESC , thereby spoofing an interface stub. This enables direct interaction with the Windows RPC runtime (Rpcrt4.dll) without relying on MIDL-generated stubs or higher-level RPC abstractions. This provides direct control over binding and marshaling that goes beyond the constraints of standard client APIs. Post-exploitation activity Shortly after the webshell injection, the infected worker process spawned msiexec.exe to install a remote MSI payload hosted in Supabase cloud storage. The payload was Velociraptor, an open-source digital forensics and incident response tool – namely version 0.73.4.0 vulnerable to CVE-2025-6264 – that the attackers abused for command execution on the target network. In October 2025, Cisco Talos reported Velociraptor being leveraged by threat actors as a post-compromise execution framework and attributed this activity to Storm-2603. This tool enables arbitrary command execution, and can be used for various purposes. In our case, it was used for persistence, discovery, data collection and payload execution. The threat actor used Velociraptor to deploy multiple independent tunneling mechanisms, including Zoho Assist-Unattended Support for unauthorized remote access, VS Code CLI tunneling, Cloudflare Tunnel, and a lightweight reverse proxy utility. By doing so, they attempted to maintain access even if some of their tools were detected and deleted. At this stage, they also no longer depended on the IIS worker process lifecycle and ToolShell vulnerability. EDR-killing attempts Once the threat actor had access to the target system, they were able to upload a vulnerable driver that is used by many attackers to terminate EDR solutions. We were able to detect the main driver at C:\programdata\rsndispot.sys (054a32d6033b1744dca7f49b2e466ea2). In addition, the adversary used a malicious DLL (2d9ebf9c042f596f435865b40edad928) in a sideloading attack. This DLL was identified as Warlock ransomware’s AV killer. It was possibly intended to kill security solutions using the vulnerable rsndispot.sys driver. Ransomware deployment attempt The attackers made attempts to launch ransomware on the compromised system. However, the Proactive Defense Module in Kaspersky Endpoint Security blocked the ransomware process, effectively preventing this activity. The malware DLL we obtained was identified as Warlock ransomware. What defenders really need to look for ToolPane deserialization and CLR artifacts When the attackers tried to access ToolPane, both the dynamically compiled App_Web_toolpane.aspx assembly and the Microsoft.GeneratedCode helper assembly were present within the same application domain. Both assemblies are used in legitimate scenarios. App_Web_* assemblies are generated during normal ASP.NET page compilation, while Microsoft.GeneratedCode is typically produced by the XmlSerializer during legitimate serialization workflows. However, a request to the ToolPane endpoint that results in immediate loading of Microsoft.GeneratedCode within the same execution window indicates that the runtime is processing serialized data supplied through the request. Detecting deserialization exploitation First, it is highly recommended to read this article (in Russian) on how deserialization exploit attempts in the CLR should be detected, with examples of YARA rules and monitoring recommendations. A summary of the main idea of the detection logic is provided below. Deserialization vulnerabilities should be treated as a distinct class of exploitation events. In the CLR, deserialization is not merely the conversion of bytes into values – it is the reconstruction of an object graph from an incoming stream. Serialization and deserialization are performed by formatters. One of the most widely used formatters is BinaryFormatter . It uses the NRBF format, which defines the stream as a sequence of records describing classes, arrays, primitives, and references between objects. The shape of the resulting graph is determined by the data supplied to the deserializer. This means that the input stream defines not only the values but also the specific types, relationships, and lifecycle points that the runtime must materialize. Microsoft states explicitly that BinaryFormatter is unsafe and cannot be made safe; beginning with .NET 9, the in-box implementation is effectively removed from ordinary use. However, Microsoft continues to use it in its products, including SharePoint, because it is powerful and easy to use. To fix BinaryFormatter-related issues, architectural changes are needed. Similar warnings also apply to related unsafe mechanisms, including LosFormatter (used in the vulnerable page). The primary risk of unsafe deserialization is that the CLR may resolve unexpected types, instantiate objects outside normal constructor invariants, and invoke special lifecycle mechanisms such as ISerializable , IObjectReference , IDeserializationCallback , and methods marked [OnDeserialized] . In other words, the attacker-controlled data can force the runtime to construct a graph containing types with executable side effects. A practical detection strategy should therefore focus on graph-construction artifacts. CLR loader events identify the relevant assemblies and application domains. Memory and metadata inspection then reveal the type names and generated serializer structures associated with the suspected gadget chain. In ToolShell-like scenarios, unsafe deserialization usually produces helper assemblies (evidence in loader events) with metadata containing fully qualified type names, gadget class names, and string representations of the constructed generic types (just scan via YARA for it). In our case, the artifacts to look for include Microsoft.GeneratedCode , App_Web_toolpane.aspx* (evidence of deserialization in loader events) as well as strings identifying ObjectDataProvider , ExpandedWrapper , and LosFormatter (evidence that the input stream was deserialized into dangerous objects). Note that these indicators are not significant in isolation; their value lies in the fact that they show the runtime has already received data requiring helper code generation to reconstruct a dangerous object graph. Gadget objects that exploit vulnerabilities like those associated with ToolShell are conveniently represented in corpus-style tooling such as ysoserial.net/YSoNet, which aggregate known gadget chains for unsafe .NET deserialization. For defensive purposes, the value of these projects lies not in their offensive capability but in the catalog of types and structures that can be searched for in streams, memory, and helper assemblies. The presence of any of them in memory or in a helper assembly should be treated as a strong indicator of exploitation. To simplify deserialization exploit detection, we created a tool named CLRDeserializationExploitFinder . It can detect ToolShell exploitation attempts, as well as other gadget chains covered by ysoserial.net. Detecting a VPP-based webshell/runtime implant A mechanism based on VirtualPathProvider should be treated as a separate class of runtime implant (no files involved). In ASP.NET, HostingEnvironment.RegisterVirtualPathProvider registers a new virtual-path provider (VPP) in the application’s compilation system. Once registered, the application can serve virtual paths during dynamic compilation as though they were ordinary on-disk pages. Dynamic compilation does not require the payload to exist as a file in the filesystem. It operates entirely within the trust boundary of the application itself. During the compilation process, temporary files may be generated, but they cannot be treated as reliable forensic artifacts. Because VPPs can also be used for legitimate purposes, the defensive model should begin with an inventory of the legitimate VirtualPathProvider implementations. In all other cases, the registration or use of a new VPP should be considered anomalous. To see how the dynamic generation of virtual pages using ASP.NET, IIS, and the CLR works, and to learn how to detect such activity, use our project VirtualPathProvider Lab . The most useful telemetry source for this technique is the Microsoft-Windows-IIS-Configuration ETW provider. To detect virtual paths, events 25 and 47 are of primary value. Event 25 records the mapping of ConfigPath to PhysicalPath , and event 47 records the mapping of the configuration path to a directory. In the case of a VPP-backed path, these events create an inconsistency: IIS and ASP.NET behave as if the path has a physical representation, while the corresponding file does not exist on disk. In the example provided by VirtualPathProvider Lab, this appears as a mapping of the virtual resource googlecheck.aspx to a physical path where no real file is present. This discrepancy between configuration resolution and filesystem reality is the primary indicator of virtual content. The second indicator is more negative than positive. For disk-backed files, IIS normally creates a FileChangeNotificationMonitor chain, as reflected in events 17, 18, 19, and 21 . For virtual paths served by a custom VirtualPathProvider , this chain is absent or does not match the normal profile of an ordinary file because there is no physical object or corresponding on-disk change lifecycle. Consequently, the presence of positive events 25 and 47 combined with the absence of the expected events 17, 18, 19, and 21 for the same path forms a reliable detection pattern: the path exists for IIS as a configuration-resolved resource, yet it is not backed by the filesystem and is not accompanied by normal file-change monitoring. To sum up, the hunting target here is the discrepancy between how IIS/ASP.NET resolves a resource and what is actually present on the disk and in the change-monitoring chain. For known applications, such cases can be filtered through an allowlist of legitimate VirtualPathProvider implementations. Anything else should be treated as a potential runtime implant and examined using ETW, filesystem verification, and memory inspection of the w3wp.exe process. Attribution Microsoft tracks Storm-2603 as a standalone threat actor with no known links to other groups. While they are careful about assessing the actor’s motivation, they mention that it has previously deployed Warlock and LockBit ransomware following active exploitation of on-premises SharePoint vulnerabilities. In the analyzed incident, we observed similar SharePoint exploitation activities, as well as an EDR killer associated with Warlock ransomware and unsuccessful Warlock launch attempts. Other documented intrusions have identified Storm-2603 as leveraging known flaws within perimeter mail gateways to achieve initial access, subsequently staging the Velociraptor framework with elevated privileges and establishing persistent encrypted tunnels via commercial remote management software to orchestrate secondary lateral movement. This aligns with the post-exploitation behavior we observed in our case. Together with the ToolShell exploitation scenario, this allows us to attribute this case to Storm-2603 with moderate confidence. Conclusion From the incident analyzed in this article, the main defensive lesson is that exploiting an internet-facing SharePoint server can enable fileless code execution directly inside the application trust boundary. Storm-2603 did not require a conventional dropper, persistent service, or exposed C2 endpoint; instead, it deployed a webshell inside w3wp.exe, from which tunneling, privilege escalation, credential access, and lateral movement could be orchestrated. Detection therefore needs to move beyond file-based webshell hunting toward runtime behavior, such as anomalous w3wp.exe activity, unexpected outbound connections, RPC coercion, token impersonation, and the introduction of remote-management or DFIR tooling after application compromise. To correctly attribute malicious activity and develop appropriate response measures, defenders should reconstruct execution chains and temporal relationships across application, host, and identity layers. They should also preserve memory and IIS telemetry, and correlate low-signal reconnaissance with subsequent privileged activity rather than evaluating events in isolation. A confirmed SharePoint exploit with code execution should trigger host-level containment, persistence hunting, credential and exposed MachineKey rotation, and TTP-driven investigation rather than remediation limited to the initial exploit or known IOCs.
