IR trends Q3 2026: Actors impersonate IT help desks in vishing attacks as ransomware activity increases
Cisco Talos | Research / advisory | 2026-10-29T14:00:00+00:00
Phishing remained the leading initial access technique, appearing in slightly over one-third of all Cisco Talos Incident Response (Talos IR) engagements — a decrease from over 50 percent last quarter. In addition to traditional phishing links and attachments, we observed numerous threat actors conducting voice phishing (vishing) in tandem with IT desk impersonation to socially engineer the victim into allowing remote access via a legitimate remote monitoring and management (RMM) tool. Exploitation of public-facing applications was the next most common vector, accounting for one-third of Talos IR engagements. Threat actors predominantly targeted edge devices such as security gateways and remote network management interfaces. These are critical components within enterprise environments, often responsible for managing authentication, network traffic, or sensitive data. Compromising them can provide broad access to internal networks or user credentials. Ransomware and pre-ransomware incidents made up 27 percent of engagements in Q3 2026, an increase from a maximum of 20 percent seen in the previous four quarters. Talos responded to attacks involving prolific, technologically advanced, and well-established ransomware-as-a-service (RaaS) variants such as The Gentlemen, Qilin, and Rhysida, as well as Interlock, which operates as a closed group. Threat actors predominantly chose to disable endpoint detection and response (EDR) systems, often by exploiting vulnerable drivers or using Windows Defender Application Control (WDAC). Additionally, we observed the emerging ransomware variant VYPR, whose operator(s) tested a range of tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) instead of following a standard attack method or affiliate playbook. Salt Typhoon-affiliated threat actors exploit public-facing application vulnerabilities for cyber espionage Talos IR continues to see prolonged cyber espionage intrusions targeting the telecommunications sector. Talos attributed most of these intrusions with high confidence to China state-sponsored threat actors, including Salt Typhoon. The attackers in these intrusions routinely gain initial access by leveraging valid credentials and exploiting a known vulnerability (CVE-2023-20198) in publicly accessible web-based management interfaces—two of the most frequently observed initial access vectors this quarter. In one campaign, the threat actor deployed BadCandy, a malicious web shell implant known to be leveraged by Salt Typhoon, to maintain persistent and stealthy remote access. The adversaries continue to employ living-off-the-land (LOTL) tactics, leveraging legitimate network infrastructure features — such as VPN access, traffic mirroring, and management protocols — to establish backdoors, exfiltrate data, and conduct reconnaissance. Exploiting a vulnerability in the web-based management interface, they created privileged accounts, disabled logging to conceal their activity, and altered configurations to maintain persistence, blending their actions with normal administrative operations to evade detection. These intrusions revealed several security weaknesses in the victim environment, including reliance on password-only authentication without evidence of multi-factor authentication (MFA), use of legacy and weak management protocols (such as plaintext SNMP and outdated VPN encryption), and inadequate credential management practices. Attackers were able to create and abuse privileged accounts, disable logging, and manipulate configuration settings without encountering effective security barriers, enabling persistent and stealthy access. The absence of strong MFA and robust credential management are two of the most frequently observed security weaknesses this quarter. AI exploited as a weapon and a target Recent Talos IR investigations highlight the growing role of artificial intelligence (AI) in cybercriminal activity, both as a tool and a target. In one case, an internal threat actor who was hired under false credentials abused their privileged access to install AI-powered development tools, such as GitHub Copilot CLI and Anthropic Claude Code. While the investigation did not find evidence of malicious activity or data theft, the employee’s developer-level access — including cloning repositories, configuring plugins, and connecting to databases — could have enabled them to use AI tools to automate malicious actions within the organization. A second investigation revealed an attack chain in which a compromised macOS endpoint, infected via a trojanized application, was scripted to scrape credentials from the macOS keychain specifically associated with the Anthropic Claude Code AI assistant. This direct targeting of AI-related secrets highlights an emerging threat, where attackers seek to compromise not only traditional user and system credentials but also those tied to AI platforms, which may grant access to additional sensitive data and advanced automation capabilities. These incidents highlight the critical need for organizations to implement strong MFA and robust credential management practices, especially for AI platforms and development tools. Ransomware trends Ransomware and pre-ransomware incidents increased from a little over 20 percent last quarter to 27 percent in Q3, involving well-known threat groups like Interlock and the emerging ransomware VYPR. Frequently observed techniques involved deleting EDR systems and socially engineering victims into initiating remote management sessions. Emerging ransomware variant: VYPR During a Talos IR engagement, we observed a ransomware variant called “VYPR” that we assess is relatively new and still operationally immature. This assessment is supported by the absence of an established data leak site (DLS), as none was referenced in the ransom note and associated DLS infrastructure was not created until after the engagement. The group also appeared to rely on older leaked Conti-derived source code with only minimal modifications, reflecting a relatively unsophisticated technical approach. Their operations further suggested experimentation rather than a refined playbook, as they took an unusually long time to exfiltrate and encrypt data while testing different exfiltration parameters and deploying multiple vulnerable drivers to disable EDR protections. The actor gained initial access through voice phishing (vishing); they tricked a user into initiating a Microsoft Quick Assist session, allowing remote access to the endpoint. While the exact social engineering technique was not confirmed in this case, threat actors frequently impersonate IT help desks during vishing calls to manipulate victims into establishing remote connections — a tactic observed in several other Q3 engagements. The actor was then able to utilize existing RMM software, which subsequently executed PowerShell commands to download and install a Microsoft Software Installer (MSI) package from a remote IP address. During the attack, the actor leveraged tools that are commonly observed during ransomware investigations, including MeshAgent, Rclone, PsExec, IP scan, and Microsoft Terminal Services Client (MSTSC). Notably, we saw exfiltration over multiple days, which is not commonly observed during ransomware investigations. EDR killers: Why evade detection when you can destroy it? We observed several instances where ransomware operators disabled EDR systems, prioritizing direct disruption of security controls. We observed Interlock ransomware actors making multiple attempts to impair endpoint detection EDR capabilities by trying to uninstall Cisco Secure Endpoint (CSE) on compromised systems and used tools such as PsExec.exe and taskkill.exe to terminate security services and integrity checkers, including cscm.exe and sfc.exe. Additionally, the adversary disabled Windows Defender real-time protection and cleared various event logs to reduce visibility and hinder forensic analysis. This is in line with previously observed Interlock activity, where they deployed a malicious WDAC policy to terminate EDR solutions. In the previously mentioned VYPR ransomware incident, the threat actor disabled EDR and antivirus (AV) by using Bring Your Own Vulnerable Driver (BYOVD) techniques to evade detection and execute the ransomware. BYOVD attacks take advantage of trusted but flawed drivers to gain kernel-level access, enabling threat actors to disable EDR mechanisms and carry out their operations unimpeded. While this technique is commonly observed in ransomware attacks, this actor deployed an unusually large number of vulnerable drivers in the environment, possibly to create redundancies and maximize their chances of success. Targeting Targeting of the financial industry increased from last quarter to comprise 18 percent of all engagements, tying with public administration as the most targeted sector. Although the financial industry remains a valuable target, Talos has not ranked it among the top three most impacted sectors since 2024. One possible reason is that organizations in finance can typically invest more in cybersecurity, unlike sectors such as public administration, which has consistently been among the top-targeted industries over the past year. Meanwhile, health care fell to third place after ranking as the most targeted industry for the previous two consecutive quarters. Initial access While the number of engagements involving phishing as an initial access vector declined from last quarter, it remained the most common technique, appearing in 37 percent of Talos IR engagements. Exploitation of public-facing applications was the second most common initial access vector, accounting for 33 percent of engagements. Recommendations for addressing top security weaknesses The most common security weaknesses observed this quarter were insufficient logging, improper credential and secret management, and absent or improperly implemented authentication and MFA. These trends closely mirror last quarter, when insufficient logging and authentication weaknesses were also prevalent. Centralize logging and set retention requirements The leading security weakness was insufficient logging leading to visibility gaps, seen in over 40 percent of engagements this quarter. These gaps were in part due to the common threat actor practice of deleting logs, and in part due to insufficient log retention or configuration implemented by the customer. Talos IR recommends implementing a SIEM or centralized logging platform with a minimum of 90 days of retention, forwarding logs from servers, workstations, network infrastructure, cloud identity providers, and security appliances off-device so they survive log tampering and host rebuilds, and enabling process-creation, command-line, and cloud API (e.g., Microsoft Graph) auditing. Talos IR's Log Architecture Assessment service can identify gaps and provide a roadmap to a complete view of the environment. Tighten authentication and MFA controls Missing or insufficient authentication and MFA controls were the third most common security weakness this quarter, observed in 33 percent of engagements. The most prevalent issue was reliance on single-factor authentication, which allowed unauthorized access in several incidents. In many cases, legacy authentication protocols were still enabled, enabling adversaries to log in without any MFA requirements, and some environments failed to enforce MFA for high-privilege accounts. To reduce this risk, Talos IR recommends transitioning from push- and SMS-based MFA to phishing-resistant methods such as FIDO2/WebAuthn and hardware security keys. Organizations should also restrict self-service MFA enrollment by requiring helpdesk verification, block legacy authentication through Conditional Access, enforce number matching or verified push where phishing-resistant methods are not yet feasible, and condition access on device compliance and trusted infrastructure rather than geographic location alone. Safeguard credentials and secrets Improper credential and secret management was the second most common security weakness seen in 33 percent of engagements this quarter, reinforcing the importance of security awareness training and consistent security hygiene for every user. Observed security issues included unsafe storage and handling of credentials, such as saving passwords in browser stores, poor control mechanisms like permitting autofill for browser-stored credentials, and credential reuse and sharing. Effective secret management begins with a formal process that covers passwords, API keys, tokens, certificates, and other sensitive credentials. By requiring unique passwords for every account and encouraging the use of password managers, organizations reduce the risks associated with credential reuse and sharing. Storing secrets securely, with encryption and access restricted to only those who need them, further strengthens protection. When credentials are no longer necessary or are suspected to be compromised, they should be promptly revoked and replaced. Regular reviews of access to privileged credentials, combined with thorough logging, help detect unusual activity. Equipping staff with training on safe handling and clear reporting procedures ensures that accidental disclosures are addressed quickly and effectively. Filter generation in practice: from algorithms to LLMs
Securelist | Research / advisory | 2026-10-30T10:00:44+00:00
A modern organization protects its information systems with a whole suite of technologies that generate thousands of security alerts every day. Looking at these alerts in bulk, you’ll see that a significant share are false positives. Ideally, these alerts would be filtered out automatically so that analysts could focus on the ones that may point to a real incident. However, writing filters is a time-consuming task in itself. To create a correct filter, an analyst needs to: Review the logs Identify the source of the activity Define filtering boundaries Choose filtering criteria Write a rule Check that the filter is robust This post looks at how filter creation itself can be automated to reduce analyst workload. Filter validity criteria In the context of security alerts, a filter is a combination of event field values that marks a detection as a false positive and not worth the analyst’s attention. Filters can be generated for any combination of event fields, but not every combination yields a valid filter. That’s why we need to define validity criteria: conditions showing that a specific filter actually removes noise and doesn’t suppress useful alerts. Detection rules in security systems typically contain a set of conditions, and each of them can generate an alert. A specific event triggers a given fragment of a detection rule. The set of events that a given fragment responds to is what we’ll call its context. A good filter should cover the entire context where possible, without going beyond it. Filters that are too broad or too narrow are useless. For example, a filter based solely on PowerShell would exclude too many events, including some that deserve the analyst’s attention. Most of the filtered events would also be unrelated to the rule in question. A filter on a specific ID, on the other hand, would cover only one event, which may never recur. Problems in filter generation We can run into a number of problems when creating filters. First, events often contain an excessive number of fields (around 400 on average), and most of them aren’t needed for filtering. At the same time, generating and ranking combinations of these fields is also fairly resource-intensive. So, the first step is to discard the irrelevant fields and keep only those worth using in the filter. For example, out of 400 fields in an event, four may be enough for filtering. Another problem is string normalization. Field values (for example, the path to the same application on different systems) can vary depending on system settings, user folder names, and so on. For a filter to account for all these variations, the strings must be normalized. For file paths, in particular, you can replace the drive letter and user folders with standard environment variables and convert all paths to a single case. Finally, if a filter completely mirrors the query of a detection rule, it effectively disables that rule. To fix this, remove the event fields used in the detection rule from the filter. For example, if a rule triggers on a file path, there are two ways to filter out unwanted alerts: In the filter, use fields other than the file path. Use a part of the file path that the rule doesn’t use. For example, for the rule file_path: ".../something.exe" , you can set up a filter that excludes events with the user/unique_files/ directory in the file path. Filtering security alerts with an algorithm Given the problems described above, automatically generating filters for security alerts should consist of the following steps: An analyst receives a security alert that they assess as a false positive and uses the filter generator. The generator takes the 400-field event from the alert, along with the rule that triggered on it, and determines the context. The generator selects the fields that are relevant to the filter. The generator normalizes the strings. The generator builds different combinations of the selected fields. To do this, we applied the FP-Growth (frequent pattern growth) algorithm to the context determined earlier. FP Growth builds a tree of frequently occurring items (fields) from a dataset (in our case, the context) and discards items that aren’t frequent enough. This reduces the number of combinations to check, which lowers the cost of finding the most relevant ones. The generator ranks the resulting combinations and offers the analyst the top three to choose from. For example, consider an event described by the following JSON: After generating and ranking the combinations, we got three filters based on file paths: processcmdline:("nodejs\\node.exe" AND "*****\\appdata\\roaming\\npm\/node_modules\/pi-coding-agent\/dist\/cli.js") file_path:"*****/.pi\\agent\\bin\\fd.exe" processcmdline: "programfiles\\nodejs\\node.exe" The last filter turned out to be too broad, but the analyst marked the other two as valid. Pros and cons of the algorithmic approach The algorithmic approach to filter generation has several advantages. First, it’s simple and deterministic: the same alert always produces the same result. Second, it’s interpretable, meaning we can see why a given combination ranked near the top of the list and why a particular field didn’t make it into the list of candidate fields at all. Third, the stages of the process are independent of each other, so individual steps can be improved and tuned without the risk of breaking the algorithm elsewhere. Finally, it has no external dependencies or models, which makes it easy to deploy in any customer environment. However, it also has drawbacks. The first is its dependence on context. If there’s no context for some reason — for example, the rule’s first-ever trigger — a filter can’t be created. The second problem is string normalization. There’s a high chance of running into atypical cases that the normalization rules don’t cover. Often such cases can’t be described correctly at all, because they contradict each other. These problems can be solved with large language models (LLMs). When an LLM generates the filters, no context is needed, because the LLM doesn’t iterate over or rank combinations. The LLM also takes a more flexible approach to string normalization, including non-standard cases. That said, an LLM doesn’t have to be involved at every stage of the generation process. We decided to keep the algorithmic solution where it works well. For example, a simple algorithm selects suitable fields without errors, so we didn’t move this stage to the model. Filter generation with an LLM The LLM-based filter generation looks like this: The input is still an alert containing an event with 400 fields, plus the rule that triggered on that event. Then an algorithm selects the most suitable fields for the filter. Next, the LLM processes the input — the event, the selected fields, and the detection rule — and generates three filter options along with an explanation for them. The explanation helps the analyst understand why these particular filters were suggested and make an informed choice. Consider the following event in JSON format: The LLM suggested three filter options that all contain the same command line fragment: file_path: "\\WindowsPowerShell\\powershell.exe" AND filecmdline: "Get-VM -Name kafka\*" filecmdline: "Get-VM -Name kafka\*" filecmdline: "kafka*" As in the previous example, two of the three output filters were valid. The last one was too broad to be usable. The analyst chose the first filter with minor edits: file_path: "powershell.exe" AND filecmdline: "Get-VM -Name kafka\*" Pros and cons of generating filters with an LLM Using an LLM solved the problems of purely algorithmic filter generation: dependence on context and errors in string normalization. However, the single-request approach had its own drawbacks. First, it had no output validation. The LLM returned filters as-is, with no syntax check and no way to correct errors. Second, unlike in the algorithmic version, all filter generation tasks were handled by a single system prompt. As a result, a minor fix in one place could break the logic in another. Using multiple AI agents To address the problems of generating filters with a single model and a single system prompt, we tried distributing the tasks across different LLM agents. In this version, we brought in agents as early as the stage of selecting relevant fields. The chain includes four agents in total: Selector: selects the relevant fields. Generator: generates filters. Validator: evaluates the generated filters. If the filters are found to be invalid, it returns the task to the generator. If the selected fields are found to be irrelevant, it starts a new field selection cycle. If the filters pass validation, or the limit on regeneration attempts is reached, the valid filters (or the best ones available) are passed to the analyst. Commentator: analyzes the output filters and explains which field values were used and why. For the following JSON event, the agents produce three valid filters based on the file path. The filters differ in the additional parameters that support the detection: file_path:"sysvol\\domain\\scripts\\logon.bat" file_path:"sysvol\\domain\\scripts\\logon.bat" AND file_md5:"0x7..6E" autorun_entry: "Group Policy\\State" AND file_path: "SYSVOL\\domain\\scripts\\logon.bat" Pros and cons of the agent-based approach LLM agents give us a system that can validate and correct filters. Its modular architecture also lets us tune and improve different stages of generation independently. However, this approach makes more LLM calls than the previous ones, so the system is slower. It also depends on access to a model, which may not be available in the organization’s isolated environment. Evaluating the filter generation system To evaluate how well the filter generation system works, we use two main metrics: Approval rate. For each security alert, the system suggests three filters. If the analyst selects at least one of them, we count the generation as successful. For example, in the case below, the approval rate is 2/3. False positive reduction rate (FP drop). This metric shows how much a filter helps reduce the number of false positives. It can only be measured over time, because we need to compare the number of false positive alerts received during a set period before and after the filter is deployed. At the same time, the LLM agentless and agent-based approaches perform almost identically. So we chose the simpler and faster option: an LLM with a single system prompt. As a result, we cut false positives by 99%, increased the number of filters generated per day by 28%, and made analysts’ alert handling 17% more efficient. Takeaways Practice has shown that filter generation can be automated effectively. We’ve looked at several options for automatically generating filters for false-positive security alerts: a purely algorithmic approach, an LLM-based approach, and an approach built on a group of LLM agents. All of them can generate valid filters and make the analyst’s work much easier, but each has its own pros and cons. The algorithmic approach scored lower on the evaluation metrics, but in two-thirds of cases, it still produces filters that satisfy the analyst. It works especially well when there’s already a set of similar events to filter out, meaning enough context has accumulated. The algorithmic generator can also be deployed in any environment. Both standalone LLM and multi-agent LLM approaches show high accuracy, and their results differ only slightly, so the slower agent-based approach looks excessive at this stage. If an LLM is available, the best option appears to be a single model that handles several stages of the generation cycle.
